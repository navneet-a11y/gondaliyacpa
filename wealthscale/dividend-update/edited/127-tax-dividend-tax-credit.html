Dividend Tax Credit in Canada 2026

Dividend tax credit Canada rules for 2026 work in two steps. First, a taxable Canadian dividend is grossed up: eligible dividends by 38% and other than eligible dividends by 15%. Then a federal credit of 15.0198% (eligible) or 9.0301% (other than eligible) of the grossed-up amount comes off your tax, and your province adds its own credit. The result is a lower rate than on interest: in Ontario, $10,000 of eligible dividends on top of $90,000 of other income costs $801.81 in tax, against $3,058.30 on the same amount of interest. This guide gives every confirmed 2026 provincial rate, the marginal rates on dividends and the effect of the gross-up on OAS.

1. How the Dividend Tax Credit in Canada Works

Why there is a credit at all. A Canadian corporation pays dividends out of profit that has already been taxed inside the company. Without relief, the same dollar of profit would be taxed twice: once in the corporation and again in your hands. The dividend tax credit gives you a credit for the corporate tax already paid, so the total tax on profit paid out as a dividend comes closer to the tax you would have paid had you earned that income directly. Tax specialists call this integration.

Step one: the gross-up. You do not report the dividend you actually received. You report a larger, grossed-up amount meant to approximate the corporation's pre-tax profit behind the dividend. For 2026, eligible dividends are grossed up by 38%, so a $1,000 dividend becomes $1,380 of taxable income. Other than eligible dividends are grossed up by 15%, so $1,000 becomes $1,150.

Step two: the credits. Tax is calculated on that grossed-up amount at your normal rates, and then the federal and provincial dividend tax credits are subtracted. Federally, the credit equals 15.0198% of a grossed-up eligible dividend, and 9.0301% when the dividend is other than eligible. Each province sets its own credit, also usually as a percentage of the grossed-up amount.

The net effect. Because the credits are larger than the extra tax at low and middle incomes, eligible dividends can carry a lower rate than any other kind of income. At some incomes the rate is negative, which means the credit also shelters part of the tax on your other income. The credits are non-refundable, though: they can reduce your tax to zero, but they cannot create a refund, and unused credits are lost rather than carried forward.

Only taxable Canadian dividends. The rules cover dividends from taxable Canadian corporations held in non-registered accounts. Dividends from US or other foreign companies are taxed as ordinary income, with a separate foreign tax credit for any tax withheld abroad. Dividends inside registered accounts are not reported at all while they stay in the account.

Reading the table. The credit as a share of the actual dividend is the gross-up factor times the credit rate: 1.38 x 15.0198% = 20.7273% for eligible dividends and 1.15 x 9.0301% = 10.3846% for other than eligible dividends. At the top federal rate, the federal tax on $100 of eligible dividends is ($138 x 33%) - $20.73 = $45.54 - $20.73 = $24.81.

Why the gross-ups differ. Eligible dividends usually come from income taxed at the general corporate rate, which is higher, so they get a bigger gross-up and a bigger credit. Other than eligible dividends usually come from income that benefited from the lower small business rate, so less corporate tax has to be credited back.

Quebec residents. Federal tax for Quebec residents is reduced by the 16.5% Quebec abatement, which is calculated after the federal dividend tax credit. Quebec then applies its own credit on the Quebec return, at 11.70% of grossed-up eligible dividends and 3.42% of grossed-up ordinary dividends.

The 2026 federal brackets. The credit is subtracted from tax calculated at the 2026 federal rates, from 14% on the first $58,523 of taxable income to 33% above $258,482. Our federal tax brackets 2026 page lists every band.

3. Eligible vs Non-Eligible Dividends: How to Tell Them Apart

Eligible dividends. Most dividends from Canadian public companies listed on a stock exchange are eligible dividends. A Canadian-controlled private corporation can also designate dividends as eligible, generally to the extent they come from income taxed at the general corporate rate. The paying corporation makes the designation, not you: it must notify shareholders that a dividend is eligible, and your slip then reports it that way.

Other than eligible dividends. Often called non-eligible dividends, these are mainly paid by Canadian-controlled private corporations out of active business income taxed at the small business rate. If you own a small incorporated business and pay yourself dividends, they are usually other than eligible dividends.

What your slip shows. Investment slips report eligible and other than eligible dividends separately, each with the actual amount, the taxable (grossed-up) amount and the federal dividend tax credit. Tax software carries these amounts into your return, so you rarely calculate the gross-up yourself.

Which is taxed less. At most incomes, eligible dividends carry a lower personal rate. That does not mean they are better overall: the corporation behind an other than eligible dividend paid less tax first. The two systems are designed to come out roughly even once corporate and personal tax are added together, though the fit is not exact in every province.

Other kinds of corporate payouts. Capital dividends from a private company's capital dividend account are tax-free and get no gross-up or credit. Distributions from mutual fund trusts and ETFs can include Canadian dividends, which keep their eligible or other than eligible character, alongside interest, capital gains and return of capital, each taxed its own way.

4. How the Dividend Tax Credit Is Calculated, Line by Line

The formula in words. Tax on a dividend = (grossed-up dividend x your federal marginal rate) - federal credit + (grossed-up dividend x your provincial marginal rate) - provincial credit. When the grossed-up amount spans more than one bracket, each slice is taxed at its own rate, which is what the calculators do.

A worked example. This example is hypothetical. Mira lives in New Brunswick, has $60,000 of other taxable income and receives $5,000 of eligible dividends from Canadian public company shares held in a non-registered account.

  1. Gross-up. $5,000 x 38% = $1,900, so $6,900 is added to taxable income, which rises to $66,900.
  2. Federal tax on the $6,900. Her income stays inside the 20.5% federal band ($58,523 to $117,045): $6,900 x 20.5% = $1,414.50.
  3. Federal credit. $6,900 x 15.0198% = $1,036.37, so the extra federal tax is $1,414.50 - $1,036.37 = $378.13.
  4. New Brunswick tax on the $6,900. She is in New Brunswick's 14% band ($52,333 to $104,666): $6,900 x 14% = $966.00.
  5. New Brunswick credit. $6,900 x 14% = $966.00, so the extra provincial tax is $0.00.
  6. Result. $378.13 of tax on $5,000 of dividends, an effective rate of 7.56%. She keeps $4,621.87. The same $5,000 as interest would have cost $1,725.00 ($5,000 x 34.5%).

Why the provincial part was zero. New Brunswick's 14% credit happens to equal its 14% bracket rate, so at that income the provincial credit cancels the provincial tax on the grossed-up dividend exactly. Every province has its own mix of rates and credits, which is why the table in section 6 varies so much.

8. The Dividend Gross-Up 2026 and the OAS Clawback

The gross-up raises net income. The gross-up is added to your net income, not just your taxable income, and the dividend tax credit does not reduce net income. Any benefit that is reduced as net income rises therefore sees the grossed-up amount. The best-known example is the OAS pension recovery tax, often called the OAS clawback.

The 2026 rule. For the 2026 income year, OAS is reduced by 15% of net world income above $95,323. It is fully recovered at $155,320 for people aged 65 to 74 and at $161,320 for those 75 and older.

A worked example. This example is hypothetical. Arthur, 70, lives in Victoria and has $90,000 of net income including his OAS. He receives an extra $10,000 of eligible dividends, so his net income becomes $103,800 and his recovery tax is ($103,800 - $95,323) x 15% = $8,477 x 15% = $1,271.55. Had the $10,000 been interest, net income would be $100,000 and the recovery tax ($100,000 - $95,323) x 15% = $701.55. Had it been a capital gain, only $5,000 would be added, net income ($95,000) would stay below the threshold and there would be no recovery tax. Income tax comes on top: section 7 shows $248.88 on the dividends, $2,820.00 on the interest and $1,410.00 on the gain at $90,000 in British Columbia, counting only the basic personal amounts. The dividends still cost less than interest overall, but the gross-up hands a larger share of the dividend to the clawback.

9. Interaction With Registered Accounts, Foreign Dividends and Other Rules

Registered accounts. Dividends earned inside a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, FHSA or RESP are not reported when they are paid, so there is no gross-up and no credit. Withdrawals from an RRSP or RRIF are taxed as ordinary income, whatever the account earned. That means the credit is lost on Canadian dividends held in an RRSP. It is one reason asset location matters: Canadian dividends keep the credit only in a non-registered account, while interest is fully taxed there, so interest-paying assets gain the most from an RRSP or TFSA.

Foreign dividends. Dividends from US and other foreign corporations get no gross-up and no dividend tax credit; they are taxed like interest. US withholding tax is generally 15% under the Canada-US tax treaty in a non-registered account, and you may claim a foreign tax credit for it. Our pages on US stocks in a TFSA and the foreign tax credit explain the rules by account.

Credits you cannot use. The dividend tax credit is non-refundable and cannot be carried forward. If your other credits already reduce your tax to zero, extra dividend credits are wasted. The negative rates in section 6 only help when there is tax on other income to absorb them.

Spouses. If your spouse or common-law partner has dividends but too little income to use the credit, an election may let you report all of those dividends on your return, but only if doing so creates or increases your spousal amount. Money you give your spouse to invest can also have its dividends attributed back to you.

Private company dividends to family. Dividends from a private corporation paid to family members can be taxed at the top rate under the tax on split income rules unless an exclusion applies. If you own a business with family shareholders, please have that reviewed before dividends are paid.

Minimum tax. Alternative minimum tax treats dividends differently from the regular calculation. We have not verified its 2026 rules, so the tables and calculators on this page leave it out.

10. How to Report Dividends and Claim the Credit

Use the taxable amount. On your return you enter the taxable (grossed-up) amount of eligible and other than eligible dividends, not the cash you received. Your slips show both, along with the federal credit, so check that you are copying the taxable amount if you file by hand.

Slips. Brokerages and banks issue investment income slips for dividends paid in non-registered accounts, and trusts such as ETFs and mutual funds issue trust slips. Both separate eligible from other than eligible dividends. Many slips are available through the CRA's auto-fill service in certified tax software; our guide on how to file taxes online covers that step.

The credits are calculated for you. Federal and provincial dividend tax credits are worked out on the return from the taxable amounts you enter. Tax software applies the provincial rates for your province of residence on December 31. Quebec residents claim the Quebec credit on the separate Quebec return.

No slip, still taxable. If you receive a dividend and no slip arrives, for example a small amount below a payer's reporting threshold, the dividend is still taxable and still earns the credit. Please keep your own record of dividends from brokerage statements.

Dividend reinvestment. Dividends reinvested through a DRIP are taxed in the year they are paid, exactly as if you received cash. The reinvested amount is added to your adjusted cost base, which reduces the capital gain when you later sell.

11. Special Situations

Retirees with modest income. At $30,000 of taxable income in Ontario, the next eligible dividends carry a rate of minus 8.24% when the only credits are the basic personal amounts, so dividends can lower the total bill. A retiree who also claims the age amount and the pension income amount has less tax for the dividend credit to absorb, so the saving can be smaller. Watch net income too: the gross-up reduces the age amount once net income passes $46,432.

Business owners paying themselves. Dividends from your own corporation are not earned income for RRSP purposes and do not require CPP contributions. Salary does both. The choice between salary and dividends is a planning decision that depends on your company's tax rate, your RRSP plans and your need for CPP, so please model both before choosing.

Low-income students and young adults. Someone with no other income can receive a meaningful amount of eligible dividends before owing any federal or provincial tax, because the credits offset the tax on the grossed-up amount. The dividend tax calculator page shows that amount by province.

Non-residents. A non-resident of Canada does not file a return to claim the dividend tax credit on Canadian dividends; tax is withheld at source instead. If you are leaving Canada, the credit stops applying to dividends paid after you become a non-resident.

Quebec. Quebec residents file two returns. The federal return applies the federal credit and then the 16.5% abatement; the Quebec return applies Quebec's own credit of 11.70% or 3.42%. Our Quebec tax brackets page covers the provincial side.

12. Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Reporting the cash amount. Entering the dividend you received instead of the taxable amount understates your income, and the credits, which are worked out on the taxable amount, come out too low as well. Fix: use the taxable amount from the slip; if you already filed, request a change to the return.

Mixing up the two types. Reporting other than eligible dividends as eligible applies the larger gross-up and credit, which usually understates your tax. Fix: follow the slip's split. Private company owners should confirm with the company which dividends were designated eligible.

Expecting a credit on US dividends. Foreign dividends get no credit. Fix: claim a foreign tax credit for any foreign tax withheld, and see our foreign tax credit guide.

Ignoring the gross-up for benefits. A retiree who thinks in terms of cash received can be surprised by the OAS recovery tax. Fix: budget on the grossed-up amount when you estimate net income.

13. Dividend Tax Credit Planning Strategies

These are general, rule-based approaches, not recommendations to buy any investment.

1. Hold Canadian dividends where the credit counts

The credit only works in a non-registered account. Interest is fully taxed outside registered accounts, so interest-paying investments gain the most from the shelter of an RRSP or TFSA.

2. Use low-income years

In years with little other income, eligible dividends can be received at a very low or negative rate. Retirement before OAS and CPP begin, or a sabbatical year, can be a good time to realize dividend income.

3. Mind the OAS line

Above $95,323 of 2026 net income, each $10,000 of eligible dividends costs $2,070 in OAS recovery tax. Capital gains raise net income by only half their amount, which can make them the better choice near the threshold.

4. Plan around the Ontario surtax

In Ontario, grossed-up dividends can trigger the surtax above about $95,000 of taxable income. Spreading dividends between spouses with their own capital, or across years, can keep more of them below that point.

5. Consider the spousal election

If your spouse cannot use the credit, reporting their dividends on your return may increase your spousal amount. It only helps when the spousal amount rises, so run both versions.

6. Watch the 2027 Ontario change

Ontario's other than eligible credit drops to 1.9863% in 2027. Owners of Ontario corporations planning dividends may want to compare the timing of 2026 and 2027 payouts.

7. Keep DRIP records

Reinvested dividends raise your cost base. Tracking them avoids paying tax twice on the same money when you sell.

8. Compare salary and dividends

For owner-managers, dividends save CPP contributions but create no RRSP room. Modelling both in the income tax calculator and the take-home pay calculator shows the trade-off.

Dividend Tax Credit Canada: Three Worked Examples

This example is hypothetical. Three people receive Canadian dividends in non-registered accounts in 2026.

  1. Grace, Prince Edward Island, $45,000 of other income and $6,000 of other than eligible dividends from her family company. Grossed-up amount $6,900 ($6,000 x 1.15). The $6,900 is taxed at 14% federal plus 13.47% PEI: $6,900 x 27.47% = $1,895.43. Credits: federal $6,900 x 9.0301% = $623.0769 and PEI $6,900 x 1.3043% = $89.9967. Tax: $1,895.43 - $623.0769 - $89.9967 = $1,182.3564, shown as $1,182.36, or 19.71% of the dividend.
  2. Owen, Newfoundland and Labrador, $130,000 of other income and $12,000 of eligible dividends. Grossed-up amount $16,560 ($12,000 x 1.38), taxed at 26% federal plus 15.8% provincial: $16,560 x 41.8% = $6,922.08. Credits: federal $16,560 x 15.0198% = $2,487.2789 and provincial $16,560 x 6.3% = $1,043.28. Tax: $6,922.08 - $2,487.2789 - $1,043.28 = $3,391.5211, shown as $3,391.52, or 28.26%.
  3. Nadia, Ontario, $95,000 of other income and $10,000 of eligible dividends. Grossed-up amount $13,800, which lifts taxable income to $108,800. Federal tax rises by $756.27 after the $2,072.73 federal credit. Ontario tax on the grossed-up amount is $1,283.10 ($12,785 x 9.15% + $1,015 x 11.16%), the Ontario surtax rises by $256.62 because it is measured before the credit, and the Ontario credit is $1,380.00, so Ontario tax rises by $159.72. Total: $756.27 + $159.72 = $915.99, or 9.16% of the dividend.

All three use 2026 federal and provincial brackets, the basic personal amounts and the confirmed 2026 dividend tax credits.

Dividend Tax Credit Canada FAQ

What is the dividend tax credit in Canada for 2026?

It is a non-refundable credit for taxable dividends from Canadian corporations. For 2026, the gross-up is 38% on eligible dividends, with a federal credit worth 15.0198% of the grossed-up figure. Other than eligible dividends have a 15% gross-up and a 9.0301% federal credit. Each province adds its own credit on top of the federal one.

What is the difference between eligible and non-eligible dividends?

Eligible dividends are mostly paid by public Canadian companies, or by private ones from income taxed at the general corporate rate. Non-eligible, or other than eligible, dividends usually come from small business income taxed at a lower rate. Eligible dividends get a larger gross-up (38% vs 15%) and a larger credit, so they are usually taxed less in your hands.

What is the dividend gross-up for 2026?

The 2026 gross-up is 38% for eligible dividends and 15% for other than eligible dividends. So $1,000 of eligible dividends adds $1,380 to your taxable and net income, and $1,000 of other than eligible dividends adds $1,150. Tax is calculated on that larger amount before the federal and provincial dividend tax credits are subtracted.

What are the provincial dividend tax credit rates for 2026?

Confirmed 2026 rates, as a share of the grossed-up dividend, are: Ontario 10% and 2.9863%, B.C. 12% and 1.96%, Quebec 11.70% and 3.42%, New Brunswick 14% and 2.75%, Nova Scotia 8.85% and 1.5%, PEI 10.50% and 1.3043%, Newfoundland and Labrador 6.3% and 3.2%, Alberta 8.12% and 2.18%, Saskatchewan 11% and 2.519%, Manitoba 8% and 0.7835%, Yukon 12.02% and 0.67%, the Northwest Territories 11.5% and 6%, and Nunavut 5.51% and 2.61%.

Why can my tax rate on dividends be negative?

At lower incomes, the federal and provincial credits on eligible dividends can exceed the tax on the grossed-up amount. The excess reduces the tax on your other income. In Ontario at $30,000 of taxable income the rate on the next $100 of eligible dividends is minus 8.24%. The credit is non-refundable, so it cannot create a refund.

How much tax will I pay on $10,000 of eligible dividends?

It depends on your province and other income. With $90,000 of other income, 2026 tax on $10,000 of eligible dividends is $801.81 in Ontario, $248.88 in British Columbia, $1,638.88 in Quebec and $2,067.27 in Newfoundland and Labrador. With $40,000 of other income, it is negative in Ontario, B.C. and New Brunswick.

Do dividends affect my OAS?

Yes. The grossed-up amount is part of net income, which sets the OAS recovery tax. For 2026, OAS is reduced by 15% of net income above $95,323. Above that line, $10,000 of eligible dividends adds $13,800 to net income and $2,070 of recovery tax, more than the $1,500 the same interest would cost.

Do US dividends qualify for the dividend tax credit?

No. Dividends from US and other foreign companies get no gross-up and no dividend tax credit; they are taxed as ordinary income. In a non-registered account, US tax withheld at the treaty rate of 15% can usually be claimed as a foreign tax credit, which reduces Canadian tax on the same income.

Do I get the dividend tax credit in a TFSA or RRSP?

No. Dividends inside a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, FHSA or RESP are not reported when paid, so there is no gross-up and no credit. TFSA withdrawals are tax-free, while RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income regardless of what the account earned, which means the credit is lost on Canadian dividends held there.

Why are Ontario dividend tax rates higher above $95,000?

Ontario works out its surtax on Ontario tax before the dividend tax credit is subtracted. Grossed-up dividends can therefore push Ontario tax above the surtax thresholds even though the credit later reduces the bill. That is why Ontario's top rate on eligible dividends is 39.34%, compared with 36.54% in British Columbia.

What is Manitoba's dividend tax credit for 2026?

Manitoba's dividend tax credit is 8% of the grossed-up amount for eligible dividends and 0.7835% for other than eligible dividends, the rates on CRA's Worksheet MB428 (line 61520). CRA publishes the 2026 worksheet in January 2027; Manitoba's 2026 budget made no change to personal income tax. Our calculators now work out Manitoba dividend tax.

Can a dividend tax credit be carried forward?

No. The dividend tax credit is non-refundable and must be used in the year the dividend is taxed. If your tax is already zero, the unused part is lost. In some cases a spouse with too little income to use the credit can have their dividends reported on the other spouse's return, if that increases the spousal amount.
