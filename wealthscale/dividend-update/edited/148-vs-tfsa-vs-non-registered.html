TFSA vs Non-Registered Account

TFSA vs non registered comes down to tax. A TFSA never taxes interest, dividends or capital gains, while a non-registered account taxes them each year or when you sell. In Ontario, at $100,000 of taxable income in 2026, a $100,000 portfolio earning 4% interest pays $1,259.20 a year in tax outside a TFSA and nothing inside one. The taxable account has no contribution limit, lets you deduct capital losses and gives a credit for US withholding tax, so it is where money goes once your TFSA room is full. At a glance

The short answer. For tax, the TFSA wins for almost every investment and every income: nothing earned inside it is taxed. A non-registered account is the place for money that does not fit in your TFSA or RRSP, or that needs features a TFSA does not allow.

Tax drag in a taxable account. At $100,000 of income in Ontario in 2026, $4,000 of interest costs $1,259.20 a year, $3,000 of eligible Canadian dividends costs $267.45, and a realized $5,000 capital gain costs $787.00. In a TFSA, all three cost $0.

Over 25 years. $7,000 a year earning 4% interest grows to $303,182 in a TFSA but $253,466 in a taxable account at a 31.48% tax rate, a gap of $49,716.

Where the taxable account wins. No contribution limit, capital losses you can deduct against gains, a foreign tax credit for US withholding, and margin and short selling in a margin account.

Order of use. As a general rule, people fill TFSA room before investing outside a TFSA, unless they have no room, need margin, or are deciding between a TFSA and an RRSP first. Within both accounts together, heavily taxed income usually goes in the TFSA.

1. TFSA vs Non Registered: The Short Answer

Same money in, different tax out. Neither account gives you a deduction: you contribute money you have already paid tax on. The difference is what happens to the growth. In a TFSA, interest, dividends and capital gains are never taxed, and neither are withdrawals. In a non-registered account, the same income is taxed every year, and gains are taxed when you sell.

The TFSA wins on tax. For the same investment, a TFSA leaves you with at least as much as a taxable account, and usually more. The only tax situations where a taxable account does better are a capital loss you want to deduct, and US dividends, where a taxable account lets you credit the 15% US tax that a TFSA loses. Even with US dividends, the TFSA usually keeps more overall, because there is no Canadian tax at all.

The taxable account wins on space and features. A TFSA has a yearly limit, $7,000 for 2026, and money taken out returns as room only on January 1 of the next year. A non-registered account has no limit, no room to track and no over-contribution tax. It also allows margin borrowing and short selling, which a TFSA does not.

So most people use both, in order. The general pattern is to fill TFSA room first, then invest outside the TFSA. People with a high income weigh the RRSP alongside the TFSA, and our TFSA vs RRSP comparison covers that choice. Section 12 sets out an order of priority, and our hub of side-by-side comparisons lists every other head-to-head page.

What this page does not do. It explains tax rules and works through hypothetical numbers. It does not recommend any investment. Our guides to the TFSA and to non-registered investment accounts cover each account in depth, and our overview of investment accounts in Canada covers the rest.

2. How Each Account Works

The TFSA. A tax-free savings account is open to residents of Canada who are 18 or older (19 where that is the age of majority) and have a social insurance number. Room starts in the year you turn 18 and builds by the annual limit, $7,000 for 2026, with unused room carried forward. Someone who has been 18 or older and resident every year since 2009 and has never contributed has $109,000. Withdrawals are tax-free and return as room on January 1 of the next year.

The non-registered account. A taxable account at a broker or bank, such as a cash, margin or joint account, has no special tax status. Interest is taxed in full at your marginal rate. Eligible Canadian dividends are grossed up by 38% and other than eligible dividends by 15%, then reduced by dividend tax credits. Half of a capital gain is included in income when you sell.

The same investments. Both can hold cash, GICs, stocks, ETFs, bonds and mutual funds. A TFSA can hold listed options at some brokers, but not short positions or margin loans. Choosing investments rarely decides between the two; tax and room do.

The same protection. At an investment dealer that is a CIPF member, a TFSA and a cash or margin account all fall in the general accounts group, covered up to $1,000,000 combined if the firm fails and client property is missing. At a CDIC member, eligible deposits in a TFSA are insured up to $100,000 in their own category, separate from deposits held in your name.

Where to open them. Most brokers, banks and robo-advisors offer both, often in one app. Our rankings of the best TFSA accounts and the best online brokers follow our published broker rating methodology, and our page on how WealthScale makes money explains our business model.

Interest. In the TFSA, $7,000 a year at 4% grows to $303,182. In the taxable account, tax at 31.48% each year leaves an after-tax return of about 2.74%, and the balance reaches $253,466. The TFSA ends $49,716 ahead, with no sale needed.

Dividends and growth. In the TFSA, a 6% total return grows to $407,095. In the taxable account, the dividends are taxed each year at 8.92% and the after-tax amount is reinvested, so the balance before selling is $391,098, with an adjusted cost base of $278,008. Selling it all in one year with $60,000 of other income creates a $113,090 gain and $17,713 of tax, leaving $373,384. The TFSA is $33,710 ahead.

Pure growth. With no yearly income, the taxable account also grows to $407,095 before selling, because nothing was taxed along the way. But selling it all in one year with $60,000 of other income creates a $232,095 gain and $43,922 of tax, leaving $363,173. Spreading the same sale over five years at the same income cuts the tax to $35,158, but never to zero. In a TFSA, the tax is $0 whenever you sell.

What the examples teach. The TFSA wins in every case. The gap is widest for interest, because it is taxed in full every year, and narrowest for eligible dividends with growth, because both are taxed lightly. A taxable account holding growth investments can defer tax for decades, but the bill arrives on sale or at death. Our compound interest calculator shows growth at other rates.

5. Capital Gains, Deferral and Losses

How gains differ. In a TFSA, selling at a gain has no tax effect. In a taxable account, half of the gain is added to your income in the year you sell. The 2026 inclusion rate is one half; the proposed increase to two thirds was cancelled.

Deferral is the taxable account's partial answer. You choose when to sell. Selling in a year when your income is lower, such as early retirement before pensions start, can reduce the rate on the gain. Spreading sales over several years keeps more of the gain in lower brackets, as section 4 shows.

Losses are the taxable account's one clear edge. A loss in a TFSA has no tax value and also shrinks the room that returns when you withdraw. A loss in a taxable account can offset capital gains: half of it reduces taxable capital gains, and unused net capital losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely. Our guide to capital losses in Canada explains how.

The superficial loss rule. A loss is denied if you or an affiliated person, such as your spouse, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after. Buying the same investment back inside your TFSA within that window can also deny the loss. See our page on the superficial loss rule.

Moving shares into a TFSA. Transferring an investment in kind from a taxable account into a TFSA counts as selling it at its current value and contributing that value. A gain is taxable. A loss on that transfer is not allowed, so selling a losing investment in the taxable account first, then contributing the cash, keeps the loss.

6. Flexibility and Withdrawals

Taking money out. Both accounts let you withdraw at any time. A TFSA withdrawal is tax-free. A withdrawal of cash from a taxable account is not taxed either, but selling an investment to raise the cash can create a taxable gain.

Putting money back. This is where the accounts differ most. A TFSA withdrawal returns as room only on January 1 of the next year. If your room is full and you put the money back in the same year, the redeposit is an excess taxed at 1% a month. A taxable account has no such rule: money can go in and out as often as you like. Our page on the TFSA over-contribution penalty shows the cost of getting this wrong.

Short-term money. For money you expect to move in and out several times a year, a savings account or taxable account avoids the TFSA's same-year trap. For long-term money, the TFSA's tax-free growth is usually worth the room rules.

Timing a TFSA withdrawal. A withdrawal in December returns as room on January 1, a few weeks later. The same withdrawal in January waits almost a year. Our guide to TFSA withdrawal rules covers the timing.

Margin and borrowing. Only a non-registered margin account offers margin borrowing and short selling; a TFSA offers neither. Interest on money borrowed to invest in a taxable account may be deductible; please keep records of how borrowed money was used. Our ranking of the lowest margin rates in Canada compares rates.

7. Effect on OAS and Other Benefits

The hidden tax in a taxable account. Interest, grossed-up dividends and taxable capital gains from a taxable account all count in net income. Net income decides income-tested benefits such as Old Age Security, the Guaranteed Income Supplement and the Canada child benefit. TFSA income and withdrawals do not count.

The OAS recovery tax. For the 2026 income year, the OAS recovery tax takes 15% of net income above $95,323. This example is hypothetical. A retiree with $90,000 of net income earns $10,000 of interest in a taxable account, raising net income to $100,000. The recovery tax is $701.55 (($100,000 - $95,323) x 15%), on top of the income tax on the interest. The same $10,000 drawn from a TFSA adds nothing to net income and triggers no recovery tax.

The gross-up effect. Eligible dividends add more to net income than you actually receive, because of the 38% gross-up. $5,000 of eligible dividends adds $6,900 to net income ($5,000 x 1.38). For the same retiree, that would bring net income to $96,900 and a recovery tax of $236.55 (($96,900 - $95,323) x 15%).

Families with children. The Canada child benefit is also based on family net income. Investment income in a taxable account can reduce it, while TFSA income cannot. Our Canada child benefit guide explains how income affects the payment.

A planning point. For retirees near the OAS threshold, or families on income-tested benefits, the TFSA's advantage is larger than the tax tables alone suggest. Our OAS clawback guide shows the full recovery range.

8. Limits and Room

The TFSA limit. The 2026 TFSA dollar limit is $7,000. Your room is that limit plus unused room from earlier years, plus withdrawals from the previous year, minus contributions this year. Room starts in the year you turn 18, or the year you become a resident if later, and never before 2009. The 2027 limit has not been published yet.

Room by year. The cumulative room for someone eligible every year since 2009 and who has never contributed is $109,000 in 2026. Our TFSA contribution limit page lists each annual limit and the total for every birth year.

No limit outside the TFSA. A non-registered account has no room, no annual limit and no lifetime cap. That is its main reason to exist: it takes whatever does not fit in registered accounts.

Over-contributions. Putting more into a TFSA than your room allows costs 1% a month on the highest excess, with no grace amount. A taxable account cannot be over-contributed. If you are unsure of your TFSA room, a deposit in a taxable account is safe while you check, and you can move money into the TFSA once you know the figure.

Newcomers and people returning. Newcomers get room only from the year they become a resident, and no room is added for a full year spent abroad. Our page on the TFSA for non-residents and newcomers works through those cases. Until a newcomer has built TFSA room, a taxable account may be the only place for larger savings.

9. US Investments in Each Account

The same withholding. US dividends paid to a Canadian resident have 15% US tax withheld under the Canada-US tax treaty when a W-8BEN is on file, or 30% without one. That happens in both a TFSA and a taxable account.

The difference is recovery. In a taxable account you report the gross dividend and claim a foreign tax credit on Form T2209 (line 40500), plus Form T2036 for the provincial part outside Quebec. The credit is the lesser of the US tax and the Canadian tax on that income. In a TFSA there is no Canadian tax to credit it against, so the 15% is a permanent cost.

Which keeps more. This example is hypothetical. $1,000 of US dividends at $100,000 of income in Ontario: in a TFSA, $150 is withheld and you keep $850. In a taxable account, $150 is withheld and credited, but Canadian tax on the $1,000 at 31.48% is $314.80, so you keep $685.20. The TFSA still keeps more, even though its withholding is never recovered.

The RRSP exception. Under the treaty's exemption for retirement accounts, US dividends on US-listed shares held directly in an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF have no US withholding. For someone with room in all three accounts, US dividend payers tend to fit the RRSP best, Canadian dividend payers the taxable account or TFSA, and interest the TFSA or RRSP. Our page on US stocks in a TFSA and our list of the best TFSA accounts for US stocks cover the TFSA side.

US dollars. Many brokers let both a TFSA and a taxable account hold US dollars, and some charge a monthly fee for the US dollar side of an account. Our guide to the foreign tax credit for US dividends explains the taxable-account forms in detail.

10. Special Cases

Active traders. CRA states that TFSA holders who trade with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have the income taxed as business income and the account deregistered. CRA gives no trade-count test. Many active traders keep that activity in a taxable account, where profits are reported as business income or capital gains in the normal way, and leave the TFSA for long-term investing.

Leaving Canada. You can keep a TFSA after you become a non-resident, and Canada does not tax its income or withdrawals, though your new country may. No room is added for a full year abroad, and contributions made while non-resident are taxed at 1% a month. A taxable account stays taxable, and the tax on it depends on the rules of both countries; please get advice before you move.

At death. A TFSA's value at death is not taxed, and a spouse or common-law partner named successor holder can take it over with its tax-free status intact. A taxable account's unrealized gains are generally taxed on the final return unless the investments pass to a spouse. Our page on the TFSA successor holder vs beneficiary explains the TFSA side.

Spouses. Money given to a spouse to invest in their own taxable account can be taxed back to the giver under the attribution rules. Money given to a spouse to fund their own TFSA earns tax-free income while it stays there. For couples with one higher earner, filling both TFSAs before using a taxable account is a common general approach.

Low income. At low incomes, eligible Canadian dividends in a taxable account can carry little or even negative tax, because the dividend tax credit can exceed the tax on the dividend. The TFSA's tax advantage is then small for those dividends, though it still matters for interest and for income-tested benefits.

11. Using Both: What to Hold Where

Asset location. When you have money in both a TFSA and a taxable account, the same set of investments can be split between them in different ways. Because each kind of income is taxed differently in the taxable account, the split changes your total tax without changing what you own.

The general rule. Put the most heavily taxed income where it is sheltered, and the most lightly taxed income where it is exposed. Interest and foreign dividends are taxed at your full marginal rate, so they usually fit the TFSA (or RRSP). Eligible Canadian dividends and long-term capital gains are taxed more lightly, so they usually fit the taxable account.

A worked split. This example is hypothetical. An Ontario investor with $100,000 of taxable income has $30,000 of TFSA room and $60,000 to invest: $30,000 in GICs paying 4% ($1,200 of interest) and $30,000 in Canadian dividend shares paying 3% ($900 of eligible dividends). With the dividend shares in the TFSA and the GICs in the taxable account, the yearly tax is $377.76 on the interest. With the GICs in the TFSA and the shares in the taxable account, the yearly tax is $80.24 on the dividends. The second split saves $297.52 a year ($377.76 - $80.24), or $2,975.20 over 10 years at the same income, before any growth.

The growth caveat. Holding the higher-growth investment in the TFSA shelters more future gains, which can outweigh the yearly income tax saved. There is no single right answer: it depends on expected income, growth, holding period and your tax rate now and later. These are general rules, not a recommendation of any investment.

Rebalancing. Selling inside a TFSA to rebalance has no tax effect. Selling in a taxable account can create a gain. When your mix drifts, rebalancing inside the TFSA, or directing new money in the taxable account to the holdings that are short, can avoid realizing gains. Our guide to how to invest in Canada covers building a mix.

12. TFSA or Taxable Account: Order of Priority

A rule-based order. This is general information, not advice for your situation. Many people use an order like the one below, adjusting it for their income, pension, benefits and plans.

  1. An emergency reserve. A few months of expenses in savings, often in a savings TFSA, so a sudden cost does not force a sale.
  2. Any employer match. A workplace plan that adds money to yours is usually taken first.
  3. TFSA and RRSP. Fill one or both, depending on your tax rate now and later. Our TFSA vs RRSP comparison works through that choice, and first-time buyers can add the FHSA.
  4. An RESP for children. Where education savings are a goal, the government grant on RESP contributions usually comes before a taxable account.
  5. Then invest outside the TFSA. Once registered room is full, a non-registered account takes the rest, with tax-favoured investments placed there where possible.

When the order changes. If you need margin or short selling, or you move money in and out often, part of your savings may belong in a taxable account sooner. If you have unused TFSA room from earlier years, the TFSA usually comes before any new taxable investing. Each January 1, new TFSA room is a chance to move money from the taxable account into the TFSA, after checking the tax on any gain.

Moving money across. Moving a taxable investment into a TFSA in kind counts as a sale: a gain is taxable and a loss is denied. Contributing cash, or selling a loser first, avoids the problem. The TFSA calculator confirms your room before any move.

13. Common Mistakes

Investing outside a TFSA with room unused. Every dollar of income in a taxable account is taxed; in the TFSA it would not be. Please check your room before opening a taxable account.

Using the TFSA for money that moves in and out. Same-year redeposits with no room create an excess taxed at 1% a month. A taxable or savings account suits short-term money better.

Holding interest in the taxable account and dividends in the TFSA. With both accounts, the reverse split usually costs less tax, as section 11 shows.

Moving a losing investment into a TFSA. A transfer in kind is treated as a sale, and the loss is denied. Sell in the taxable account first, then contribute cash.

Ignoring the benefits effect. Taxable income and grossed-up dividends raise net income, which can trigger the OAS recovery tax or reduce income-tested benefits. TFSA income does not.

Not tracking ACB. In a taxable account, every purchase, reinvestment and return of capital changes the adjusted cost base. Without a record, you may pay tax twice on the same money. Our editorial standards explain how we check the tax figures on this page.

Strategies for Using a TFSA and a Taxable Account Together

1. Fill the TFSA each January

New room arrives on January 1. Moving money from a taxable account into the TFSA early in the year shelters more months of growth. Contribute cash, or move investments with little or no gain, to keep the tax on the move low.

2. Shelter interest first

Interest is taxed at your full marginal rate in a taxable account. If you hold both accounts, keeping GICs, savings and bond funds in the TFSA removes the most tax per dollar of room.

3. Keep Canadian dividends in the taxable account

Eligible dividends are taxed lightly in a taxable account because of the dividend tax credit, and at low incomes the tax can be close to zero. They often lose less to tax there than interest would.

4. Harvest losses only in the taxable account

Losses inside a TFSA have no tax value. In a taxable account, selling a loser before year end can offset gains. Avoid buying the same investment back within 30 days in any account, including your TFSA.

5. Rebalance inside the TFSA

Selling in a TFSA has no tax effect. When your mix drifts, make the trades in the TFSA where you can, and use new money in the taxable account to top up holdings that are short.

6. Time taxable sales

Gains in a taxable account are taxed when realized. Spreading large sales over several years, or selling in a lower-income year, keeps more of the gain in lower brackets.

7. Claim the foreign tax credit

US dividends in a taxable account have 15% withheld with a W-8BEN on file. Reporting the gross dividend and claiming the credit stops the US tax from being an extra cost on top of Canadian tax.

8. Draw from the TFSA near benefit thresholds

TFSA withdrawals do not count as income. Retirees near the OAS recovery threshold of $95,323 for the 2026 income year can keep taxable income lower by drawing on the TFSA instead of selling taxable investments.

Illustrative Example: Filling the TFSA, Then Investing Outside It

This example is hypothetical.

Elena lives in Ontario, earns $100,000 a year and has never contributed to a TFSA. She was 18 or older and resident in Canada every year since 2009. She has $130,000 to invest in January 2026.

  1. TFSA room. Her room for 2026 is $109,000, the total of every annual limit from 2009 to 2026. She contributes $109,000 to her TFSA.
  2. The rest. The remaining $21,000 ($130,000 - $109,000) goes into a non-registered account, because it does not fit in the TFSA.
  3. What goes where. She holds her interest-paying GICs and bond fund in the TFSA, and Canadian dividend shares in the taxable account.
  4. Tax on the taxable account. If the $21,000 earns 3% in eligible dividends, $630 ($21,000 x 3%), the 2026 tax at her income is about 8.92% of the dividend: $56.16 ($630 x 8.915%). Had she held GICs paying 3% there instead, the tax on $630 of interest would be $198.32 ($630 x 31.48%).
  5. Each January. When new TFSA room arrives on January 1, 2027, she can move cash, or investments with little gain, from the taxable account into the TFSA.

Elena pays no tax on anything earned inside her TFSA, and keeps the tax on her taxable account low by holding lightly taxed investments there.

TFSA vs Non-Registered FAQ

What is the difference between a TFSA and a non-registered account?

Neither gives a tax deduction. In a TFSA, interest, dividends, capital gains and withdrawals are never taxed, but contributions are limited to your room, $7,000 for 2026 plus unused room. A non-registered account has no limit, but its interest, dividends and realized gains are taxed each year, and capital losses can offset gains.

Should I invest in a TFSA or a taxable account first?

As a general rule, the TFSA comes first, because nothing earned inside it is taxed. A taxable account is usually used once TFSA and RRSP room is full, for money you move in and out often, or for margin and short selling. Whether the TFSA or the RRSP comes first depends on your tax rate now and later.

How much tax do I pay on investments outside a TFSA?

It depends on the income type and your rate. At $100,000 of taxable income in Ontario in 2026, $4,000 of interest costs $1,259.20, $3,000 of eligible Canadian dividends costs $267.45, and a realized $5,000 capital gain costs $787.00. Inside a TFSA, each of those costs $0.

How much more does a TFSA leave me over 25 years?

In our hypothetical Ontario example at $100,000 of income, $7,000 a year at 4% interest grows to $303,182 in a TFSA and $253,466 after tax in a taxable account, a $49,716 gap. With 6% growth and no income, the gap is $43,922 if everything is sold in one year at $60,000 of other income.

Is there any reason to invest outside a TFSA if I have room?

A few. A taxable account lets you deduct capital losses, offers margin borrowing and short selling, has no room to track for money you move often, and lets you credit US withholding tax. For most long-term investors with unused room, those points do not outweigh the TFSA's tax-free growth.

What should I hold in my TFSA versus my taxable account?

As a general rule, the most heavily taxed income, interest and foreign dividends, tends to fit the TFSA or RRSP, while eligible Canadian dividends and long-term capital gains tend to fit the taxable account. Holding a high-growth investment in the TFSA can also make sense. This is general information about tax, not a recommendation of any investment.

Can I move investments from a non-registered account to my TFSA?

Yes, as a contribution in kind, up to your TFSA room. It is treated as selling the investment at its current value. A gain is taxable in the year of the move, and a loss is not allowed. Selling a losing investment first and contributing the cash keeps the loss. Please check your TFSA room before any move.

Are capital losses in a TFSA deductible?

No. A loss inside a TFSA has no tax value, because gains there are not taxed either, and the loss also reduces the room that returns when you withdraw. In a taxable account, half of a capital loss offsets taxable capital gains, and unused net losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely.

Are US stocks better in a TFSA or a taxable account?

Both have 15% US tax withheld on dividends with a W-8BEN. A taxable account can credit it against Canadian tax, a TFSA cannot. Even so, a TFSA usually keeps more, because there is no Canadian tax: on $1,000 of US dividends at $100,000 in Ontario, a TFSA keeps $850 and a taxable account $685.20. An RRSP has no US withholding.

Does income in a taxable account affect OAS?

Yes. Interest, grossed-up dividends and taxable capital gains raise net income, and the OAS recovery tax takes 15% of net income above $95,323 for the 2026 income year. $10,000 of interest that raises net income from $90,000 to $100,000 costs $701.55 in recovery tax. TFSA income and withdrawals do not count.

Is a TFSA or taxable account better for active trading?

CRA states that TFSA holders who trade like a professional may have the income taxed as business income and the account deregistered, with no trade-count test. A taxable account carries no such risk to its status, and it offers margin and short selling. Many active traders keep trading in a taxable account and long-term investing in the TFSA.

What happens to a TFSA and a taxable account at death?

A TFSA's value at death is not taxed, and a spouse named successor holder can take it over with its tax-free status. A taxable account's unrealized gains are generally taxed on the final return, unless the investments pass to a spouse. Earnings in a TFSA paid to a beneficiary after death are taxable to them.
