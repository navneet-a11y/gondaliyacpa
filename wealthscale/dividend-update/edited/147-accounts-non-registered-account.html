Non-Registered Investment Accounts Explained

A non registered account, usually written non-registered, is an ordinary taxable investment account with no contribution limit, no tax deduction and no tax shelter. Interest is taxed in full at your marginal rate, Canadian dividends get a gross-up and a dividend tax credit, and only half of a capital gain is taxed. At $90,000 of taxable income in Ontario in 2026, $1,000 of interest costs $296.50 in tax, $1,000 of eligible dividends costs $63.90 and a $1,000 capital gain costs $148.25. This guide explains each rule, how to track your adjusted cost base and when a taxable account makes sense.

At a glance

What it is. A taxable investment account in Canada, such as a cash account, margin account or joint account at a broker or bank. Anyone can open one, and there is no limit on how much you put in.

No deduction, no shelter. Contributions are not deductible, and the income and gains the account earns are taxed each year on your return. Taking out money you put in is not taxed; selling an investment to raise cash can create a taxable gain or a loss.

Interest. Fully taxable at your marginal rate, the same as salary. $1,000 of interest costs $296.50 at $90,000 of income in Ontario in 2026.

Canadian dividends. Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and other than eligible dividends by 15%, then federal and provincial dividend tax credits reduce the tax. $1,000 of eligible dividends costs $63.90 at $90,000 in Ontario.

Capital gains. Half of a capital gain is included in income. A $1,000 gain costs $148.25 at $90,000 in Ontario. Half of a capital loss can offset taxable capital gains, carried back three years or forward indefinitely.

US dividends. 15% US tax is withheld with a W-8BEN on file (30% without one). In a non-registered account you can usually claim a foreign tax credit for it, which you cannot do in a TFSA.

1. What a Non Registered Account Is

The plain definition. A non-registered account is any investment account that is not registered with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) as a TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, RESP, RRIF or other plan. It has no special tax rules of its own. Whatever it earns is taxed in the normal way, in the year it is earned or realized, on the return of the person who owns the money.

Common types. At an online broker or bank you will usually see a cash account, where you pay in full for what you buy; a margin account, where the broker can lend you money against your holdings; and a joint account held by two people. Companies can open a corporate account, which is taxed in the company's hands. All of them are non-registered.

No limits, no deadlines. There is no contribution room, no annual limit, no age limit and no deadline. You can add money or take it out at any time, as often as you like. That makes a taxable account the natural place for savings once your registered room is full.

What is and is not taxed. Putting money in has no tax effect, and taking out cash you contributed is not taxed. What is taxed is the income the money earns, interest, dividends and fund distributions, plus any gain when you sell an investment for more than it cost. A loss on a sale can reduce your taxable gains.

The same investments as a TFSA. A taxable account can hold cash, GICs, stocks, ETFs, bonds and mutual funds, and some brokers also offer options and short selling in margin accounts. Investment choice is rarely what separates it from a TFSA. The tax is.

2. How Interest Is Taxed

Fully taxable. Interest from savings accounts, GICs, bonds and similar investments is added to your income in full and taxed at your marginal rate, the same rate as your next dollar of salary. There is no gross-up, no credit and no inclusion rate. That makes interest the most heavily taxed kind of investment income in a non-registered account.

A worked GIC. This example is hypothetical. A $20,000 GIC pays an example rate of 4% for one year, so it earns $800 ($20,000 x 4%). For someone in Ontario with $90,000 of other taxable income, the 2026 tax on that $800 is $237.20, a combined marginal rate of 29.65%. They keep $562.80 ($800 - $237.20). Held in a TFSA, the same $800 would be tax-free.

Taxed as it is earned. Interest is reported in the year it is earned or paid to you. For a compound GIC or other investment that runs longer than a year and pays all its interest at maturity, the interest generally has to be reported each year as it builds up, not only at the end. Your institution's tax slips show the amount for each year.

Tax slips. Your bank or broker sends a slip each year showing interest paid to you. Interest under the slip threshold, or interest from a source that does not issue slips, is still taxable and must be reported. Please keep your year-end statements so you can check every amount.

Interest on cash balances. Cash sitting in a brokerage account may earn interest, and that interest is taxable in a non-registered account. Our pages on high-interest savings accounts and GIC rates compare current rates, and the GIC calculator shows maturity values.

Where interest fits best. Because interest is taxed so heavily, many investors who hold both a TFSA or RRSP and a taxable account keep interest-paying investments inside the registered accounts and hold more tax-favoured investments, such as Canadian dividend payers or growth investments, in the taxable account. Section 9 covers this idea, often called asset location.

3. How Canadian Dividends Are Taxed

Two kinds of Canadian dividends. Dividends from Canadian corporations are either eligible dividends, usually paid by large public companies out of income taxed at the general corporate rate, or other than eligible dividends, usually paid by small private companies. Your tax slip says which is which.

The gross-up. You do not pay tax on the dividend you received. You pay tax on a grossed-up amount: the actual eligible dividend times 1.38, or the actual other than eligible dividend times 1.15. The grossed-up amount is what goes into your income.

The dividend tax credit. You then subtract dividend tax credits. The federal credit is 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount for eligible dividends and 9.0301% for other than eligible dividends. Provinces add their own credit. The table lists the 2026 provincial rates. For Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan and the three territories they are the rates on CRA's 2025 provincial worksheets (line 61520); CRA publishes the 2026 worksheets in January 2027, and none of the six 2026 budgets changed personal income tax.

Other than eligible dividends. The same $1,000 as an other than eligible dividend is grossed up to $1,150 and costs $202.79 at that income in Ontario: $235.75 federal tax less a $103.85 federal credit, plus $105.23 Ontario tax less a $34.34 Ontario credit.

Why low incomes can pay less than zero. At lower incomes the dividend tax credit can be larger than the tax on the dividend itself, so it also reduces tax on your other income. That is why the tables in section 8 show a negative figure for eligible dividends at $45,000 in Ontario and British Columbia. The credit cannot create a refund on its own, but it can wipe out tax you would otherwise pay on salary or pension income.

The gross-up raises net income. Because the grossed-up amount is what goes into income, $1,000 of eligible dividends adds $1,380 to your net income. For retirees, that can matter for income-tested benefits such as Old Age Security, whose recovery tax takes 15% of net income above $95,323 for the 2026 income year.

4. How Capital Gains Are Taxed

Half is taxed. When you sell an investment for more than its adjusted cost base (ACB) plus selling costs, the difference is a capital gain. One half of the gain, the taxable capital gain, is added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate. The other half is tax-free. The inclusion rate is one half for 2026, as it has been since 2001; the proposed increase to two thirds was cancelled.

Only when you sell. A gain is taxed when it is realized, usually when you sell. A share that has doubled but has not been sold creates no tax yet. That deferral is one of the biggest advantages of growth investments in a taxable account: you choose the year the tax falls due.

A worked gain. This example is hypothetical. Someone in Ontario with $90,000 of other taxable income sells shares for $25,000 that had an ACB of $15,000. The gain is $10,000 ($25,000 - $15,000), and the taxable capital gain is $5,000 ($10,000 x 1/2). The 2026 tax is $1,484.30, slightly more than $1,482.50 ($5,000 x 29.65%) because the Ontario surtax starts within that $5,000. They keep $8,515.70 of the gain ($10,000 - $1,484.30).

Fund distributions. Mutual funds and ETFs can pay out capital gains they realized inside the fund. These are taxable to you in the year paid, even if they are reinvested automatically. A reinvested distribution adds to your ACB, so you are not taxed on it again when you sell. Section 6 shows how.

Business income instead of gains. If you trade very often, with short holding periods, CRA may treat your profits as business income, fully taxable, rather than capital gains. There is no fixed number of trades that decides it; it depends on your conduct. Most buy-and-hold investors report capital gains.

Moving shares to a TFSA or RRSP. Transferring an investment in kind from a taxable account into a TFSA or RRSP is treated as selling it at its current value. A gain on that transfer is taxable. A loss on a transfer into a registered account is not allowed, so it is usually better to sell a losing investment in the taxable account first, claim the loss, and contribute the cash. 5. Capital Losses and the Superficial Loss Rule

Losses offset gains. If you sell for less than your ACB, you have a capital loss. Half of it, the allowable capital loss, is used to reduce taxable capital gains in the same year. It cannot be deducted against salary, interest or other income.

Carry back three years, forward indefinitely. A net capital loss you cannot use this year can be carried back to any of the three previous years to recover tax paid on gains, or carried forward with no time limit to offset future gains. Our guide to capital losses in Canada explains how to apply them.

The superficial loss rule. A loss is denied if you, or a person affiliated with you such as your spouse or common-law partner, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after the sale. The denied loss can usually be added to the ACB of the shares you bought back, so it is deferred rather than lost.

A worked superficial loss. This example is hypothetical. You bought 100 shares for $4,000 and sell them for $3,000, a $1,000 loss. Two weeks later you buy back 100 shares of the same company for $3,100 and still hold them 30 days after the sale. The $1,000 loss is denied. The ACB of the new shares becomes $4,100 ($3,100 + $1,000), so when you eventually sell them, the gain will be $1,000 smaller or the loss $1,000 larger.

Tax-loss selling. Selling a losing investment before year end to realize a loss that offsets gains is common in taxable accounts. It only works if you avoid the superficial loss rule, for example by waiting more than 30 days to buy back, or buying something that is not identical property. Our page on the superficial loss rule in Canada covers the details.

No losses in registered accounts. A loss inside a TFSA or RRSP has no tax value, because gains there are not taxed either. Only a non-registered account produces deductible capital losses, which is one of its few tax advantages.

6. Adjusted Cost Base: How to Track It

What ACB means. Your adjusted cost base is what an investment cost you for tax purposes: the price you paid plus commissions and other buying costs, adjusted over time for reinvested distributions, returns of capital and similar events. Your gain or loss on a sale is the proceeds, less selling costs, minus the ACB of what you sold.

Average cost for identical shares. When you buy the same security more than once, you do not pick which lot you sold. The ACB of identical property is averaged: total ACB divided by total units gives the ACB per unit, and that average is used for every sale.

A worked ACB record. This example is hypothetical. You buy 100 shares at $50 with a $10 commission, then 50 shares at $60 with a $10 commission. Later, $120 of dividends is reinvested in 2 more shares. You then sell 76 shares at $70 each, paying a $10 commission.

The gain on that sale. Proceeds are $5,310 (76 x $70 = $5,320, less the $10 commission). The ACB of the 76 shares sold is $4,070 ($8,140 x 76 / 152). The capital gain is $1,240 ($5,310 - $4,070), and the taxable capital gain is $620. At $90,000 of other income in Ontario, the 2026 tax is $183.83.

Events that change ACB. Reinvested dividends and distributions add to ACB, because you were already taxed on them. A return of capital, a payout that is not income, reduces ACB. Reinvested capital gains distributions from funds, sometimes shown only on year-end tax slips, add to ACB. Missing these adjustments is the most common reason investors pay tax twice on the same money.

Who keeps the record. Many brokers show a book value on statements, but it may not match tax ACB, for example after an account transfer or a fund's year-end adjustments. CRA expects you to report the correct figure. Please keep your own spreadsheet or record of every purchase, sale, reinvestment and adjustment, for every holding, across all your taxable accounts.

7. US and Foreign Investments

US withholding. US dividends paid to a Canadian resident are subject to US tax at source. Under the Canada-US tax treaty the rate is capped at 15%, as long as your broker has a valid Form W-8BEN on file. Without it, the US rate is 30%. A broker usually asks for the W-8BEN when you open an account that can hold US securities.

The foreign tax credit. In a non-registered account you report the full, gross foreign dividend as income and claim a foreign tax credit for the US tax withheld. The federal credit is claimed on Form T2209 (line 40500), and outside Quebec the provincial part on Form T2036. The credit is the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. Our guide to the foreign tax credit for US dividends shows the forms in detail.

No dividend tax credit on foreign dividends. The gross-up and dividend tax credit apply only to dividends from Canadian corporations. US and other foreign dividends are taxed like interest, in full at your marginal rate.

A worked US dividend. This example is hypothetical. US shares in a non-registered account pay $1,000 of dividends to someone in Ontario with $90,000 of other income. The US withholds $150 ($1,000 x 15%). The Canadian tax on $1,000 of foreign income at that income is $296.50. The foreign tax credit is $150, the lesser of $150 and $296.50, so $146.50 of Canadian tax remains. Total tax: $296.50, leaving $703.50.

9. When a Taxable Investment Account Makes Sense

When registered room is full. The most common reason to open a non-registered account is that your TFSA, RRSP and, if you are buying a first home, FHSA room is used up. Money beyond that has nowhere else to go, and investing it in a taxable account is still better than leaving it idle. Please check your room first: our TFSA contribution limit page lists every annual limit, our overview of investment accounts in Canada covers the RRSP, FHSA and RESP, and our ranking of the best TFSA accounts compares where to hold a TFSA.

For short-term money you will add and remove often. TFSA room returns only on January 1 of the year after a withdrawal, so frequent in-and-out money can cause over-contributions in a TFSA. A taxable account has no room to manage. Our page on the TFSA over-contribution penalty explains the trap, and our guide to TFSA withdrawal rules covers when room comes back.

For active trading and strategies registered accounts do not allow. Short selling and margin borrowing are available only in non-registered margin accounts. Frequent trading inside a TFSA risks the gains being taxed as business income. Many active traders keep that activity in a taxable account.

To use capital losses. Only a taxable account produces capital losses you can deduct against gains. Investors with large gains elsewhere, for example from a property or business sale, sometimes value that.

Asset location: what to hold where. If you have both registered and taxable accounts, where you hold each investment changes your total tax. A common general approach is to fill the TFSA and RRSP first with the most heavily taxed income, such as interest, and to hold investments that produce eligible Canadian dividends or long-term capital gains in the taxable account, where they are taxed more lightly. US dividend payers tend to fit an RRSP best because of the treaty exemption. These are general rules, not a recommendation for any investment.

When it does not fit. If you still have unused TFSA or RRSP room, a taxable account usually costs you tax that a registered account would avoid. Our TFSA vs non-registered account comparison works out that cost at several incomes.

11. Joint Accounts, Spouses and Attribution

Joint accounts. Many brokers and banks offer joint non-registered accounts for spouses, partners or family members. A joint account is convenient, but for tax the income and gains generally belong to each owner in proportion to the money each one put in, not simply split in half.

Attribution between spouses. If you give or lend money to your spouse or common-law partner to invest, the income and capital gains it earns can be taxed back to you under the attribution rules. Simply moving money to the lower-income partner's taxable account does not shift the tax. Please get advice before setting up any income-splitting arrangement.

A TFSA for the spouse instead. One common general approach is for the higher-income partner to give the other partner money to fund their own TFSA. Income earned inside a TFSA is not taxed, so there is nothing to attribute while it stays there. Our TFSA guide explains the account.

Accounts for children. Accounts opened for a minor child are complex for tax, and the rules on who is taxed on interest, dividends and gains depend on whose money it is. For saving toward a child's education, the RESP adds government grants that no taxable account offers.

Corporate accounts. A company can hold its own non-registered investment account. Income in it is taxed in the company's hands under corporate rules, not on your personal return. Our list of the best corporate investment accounts in Canada compares platforms, and our list of the best joint investment accounts covers joint options.

Deposit and investor protection. At a CIPF member dealer, cash and margin accounts fall under the general accounts group, covered up to $1,000,000 combined if the firm fails and client property is missing. CIPF does not cover market losses. Eligible deposits at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per category, with joint deposits a separate category from deposits in one name.

12. Opening One and Where

Cash or margin. A cash account is the simpler choice: you pay in full for every purchase. A margin account lets you borrow against your holdings, which adds interest cost and the risk of a margin call when prices fall. Interest on money borrowed to earn investment income in a taxable account may be deductible; please keep records of what the borrowed money was used for. Our ranking of the lowest margin rates in Canada compares rates.

What you need. Expect to give your SIN, because the institution reports your investment income to CRA on tax slips, plus photo ID, your address, your employment details and your investing experience. A broker also asks you to sign a W-8BEN if the account can hold US securities.

Where to open one. Every major online broker and bank-owned brokerage offers non-registered accounts, usually in the same app as their TFSA and RRSP. Our ranking of the best online brokers in Canada follows our published broker rating methodology. Reviews such as the Questrade review, the Wealthsimple Trade review and the Interactive Brokers Canada review cover pricing in detail, and our page on how WealthScale makes money explains our business model.

Robo-advisors. Most robo-advisors offer a taxable account alongside a TFSA and RRSP. Our list of the best robo-advisors in Canada compares them.

Transfers in kind. Moving a taxable account to another broker by direct transfer in kind keeps your holdings and their ACB, with no sale and no tax. Please send your new broker your ACB records, because book values do not always transfer correctly. Our guide on how to transfer an investment account covers the process.

13. Common Mistakes With Non-Registered Accounts

Investing in a taxable account while TFSA room sits unused. Every dollar of income in the taxable account is taxed; in a TFSA it would not be. Fill registered room first unless you have a specific reason not to.

Not tracking ACB. Relying only on a broker's book value can mean reporting the wrong gain, especially after transfers, reinvested distributions or returns of capital. Keep your own record.

Forgetting reinvested distributions. Reinvested dividends and fund distributions are taxed in the year paid and raise your ACB. Missing the ACB increase means paying tax on the same money again when you sell.

Triggering a superficial loss. Selling for a loss and buying the same security back within 30 days, or having your spouse buy it, denies the loss for now.

Moving a losing investment into a TFSA or RRSP. A transfer in kind into a registered account is treated as a sale, and a loss on it is not allowed. Sell in the taxable account first, then contribute cash.

Ignoring the foreign tax credit. US tax withheld in a taxable account is usually creditable. Not claiming it on Form T2209, and Form T2036 for the provincial part outside Quebec, means paying tax twice on the same dividends.

Holding interest-heavy investments in the taxable account and dividend stocks in the TFSA. When you have both, the reverse arrangement usually leaves less tax to pay. Our editorial standards explain how we check the tax rules on this page.

What You Need and What You Get

What you need to run a non-registered account well

  • Your SIN and photo ID to open the account
  • A signed W-8BEN if you will hold US securities
  • A record of every purchase, with commissions
  • A record of reinvested dividends and distributions
  • Your year-end tax slips from each institution
  • Your capital loss carry-forward balance from past returns
  • Knowledge of your marginal rate for each kind of income
  • A plan for which investments sit in which account

What you get from a non-registered account

  • No contribution limit and no deadline
  • Money in and out at any time, with no room to manage
  • Only half of capital gains taxed, and only when realized
  • Gross-up and dividend tax credit on Canadian dividends
  • Deductible capital losses, carried back 3 years or forward indefinitely
  • A foreign tax credit for US tax withheld
  • Access to margin and short selling in a margin account
  • CIPF coverage within the $1,000,000 general accounts limit at member dealers

How It Works, Step by Step

  1. Use registered room first. Check your TFSA, RRSP and FHSA room with the TFSA calculator and your notice of assessment.
  2. Open the account. Choose cash or margin, individual or joint, and sign a W-8BEN if you will hold US securities.
  3. Decide what to hold where. Keep heavily taxed income in registered accounts where you can.
  4. Record every transaction. Purchases, commissions, reinvestments and returns of capital all change ACB.
  5. Report income each year. Use your tax slips for interest, dividends and distributions, and report gains and losses on sales.
  6. Claim credits. Apply the dividend tax credit, the foreign tax credit and any capital losses carried forward.
  7. Review before year end. Consider tax-loss selling, keeping clear of the superficial loss rule.

10 Strategies to Cut Tax in a Non-Registered Account

1. Fill registered room first

A TFSA shelters all income, and an RRSP defers it. Money that fits in registered room is usually taxed less there. A taxable account works best for money that has nowhere else to go once that room is used.

2. Put interest inside registered accounts

Interest is taxed at your full marginal rate in a taxable account, the heaviest treatment of any investment income. If you hold both kinds of account, keeping interest-paying investments in the TFSA or RRSP removes the most tax per dollar of room.

3. Let gains build

Capital gains are taxed only when realized, and only half is included in income. Holding growth investments for the long term defers the tax and lets you choose the year it falls due, such as a year with lower income.

4. Realize gains in low-income years

A sabbatical, a year between jobs or the first years of retirement before pensions start can mean a lower marginal rate. Selling appreciated investments then can cut the tax on the same gain.

5. Harvest losses carefully

Selling a losing investment to offset gains is useful, but buying it back within 30 days, or having your spouse buy it, triggers the superficial loss rule. Wait at least 31 days or choose a holding that is not identical property.

6. Keep a full ACB record

Every reinvested dividend, distribution and return of capital changes ACB. An accurate record stops you paying tax twice on the same money and is the best defence if CRA asks how you worked out a gain.

7. Claim the foreign tax credit

US dividends in a taxable account have 15% withheld with a W-8BEN on file. Reporting the gross dividend and claiming the credit means the US tax is not a cost on top of Canadian tax.

8. Sell losers before moving shares to a TFSA

A transfer in kind to a registered account counts as a sale, and losses on it are denied. Selling a losing holding in the taxable account first, then contributing the cash, keeps the loss.

9. Watch income-tested benefits

Interest, grossed-up dividends and taxable gains all raise net income, which can trigger the OAS recovery tax above $95,323 for the 2026 income year. Retirees near that threshold may draw on a TFSA instead of selling taxable investments.

10. Use a spouse's TFSA rather than their taxable account

Money given to a spouse to invest in a taxable account can be taxed back to you under the attribution rules. Funding the spouse's own TFSA instead keeps the income tax-free while it stays in the account.

Illustrative Example: One Year in a Taxable Account

This example is hypothetical.

Marc lives in British Columbia and has $130,000 of taxable income from his job. His TFSA and RRSP are full, so he invests extra savings in a non-registered account.

  1. Interest. A bond fund in the account pays $1,600 of interest in 2026. At his income the tax is $612.64 ($1,600 x 38.29%).
  2. Eligible dividends. Canadian shares pay $2,400 of eligible dividends, grossed up to $3,312 ($2,400 x 1.38). After the federal and B.C. dividend tax credits, the tax is $373.27, about 15.55% of the dividend.
  3. A capital gain. He sells shares for a $5,000 gain. Half, $2,500, is taxable, and the tax is $957.25 ($5,000 x 19.145%).
  4. Total. His $9,000 of investment income ($1,600 + $2,400 + $5,000) costs $1,943.16 in 2026 tax ($612.64 + $373.27 + $957.25). He keeps $7,056.84 ($9,000 - $1,943.16).
  5. What the numbers show. The $1,600 of interest costs more tax than the $2,400 of dividends. Had his TFSA held the bond fund and the taxable account held more of the dividend shares, his yearly tax would usually be lower for the same investments.
  6. Record keeping. He updates the ACB of the shares he still holds, adds any reinvested distributions, and keeps his year-end tax slips with his records.

All three figures are worked out as extra tax on top of his $130,000 salary, using 2026 federal and British Columbia brackets. Our capital gains tax calculator and dividend tax calculator work out your own figures.

Non-Registered Account FAQ

What is a non registered account in Canada?

It is an ordinary taxable investment account, such as a cash, margin or joint account at a broker or bank, that is not registered as a TFSA, RRSP or other plan. There is no contribution limit and no deduction. Interest, dividends and realized capital gains are taxed each year on the owner's return, and capital losses can offset gains.

How is interest taxed in a non-registered account?

Interest is added to your income in full and taxed at your marginal rate, like salary. There is no gross-up, credit or inclusion rate. At $90,000 of taxable income in Ontario, $1,000 of interest costs $296.50 in 2026 tax. Interest on multi-year compound investments is generally reported each year as it builds up, not only at maturity.

How are Canadian dividends taxed outside a TFSA or RRSP?

Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and other than eligible dividends by 15%. The grossed-up amount is taxed, then federal and provincial dividend tax credits reduce the tax. At $90,000 in Ontario, $1,000 of eligible dividends costs $63.90 in 2026 tax and $1,000 of other than eligible dividends costs $202.79.

How much of a capital gain is taxed?

One half. The taxable half is added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate, and the other half is tax-free. The 2026 inclusion rate is one half, and the proposed increase to two thirds was cancelled. A $10,000 gain at $90,000 of income in Ontario costs $1,484.30. Gains are taxed only when you sell.

What is the adjusted cost base?

The adjusted cost base, or ACB, is what an investment cost you for tax purposes: the purchase price plus commissions, adjusted for reinvested distributions, which raise it, and returns of capital, which lower it. For identical shares bought at different times, the ACB is averaged across all units. Your gain is the sale proceeds, less selling costs, minus the ACB.

Can I deduct investment losses in a non-registered account?

Yes, against capital gains. Half of a capital loss, the allowable capital loss, reduces taxable capital gains in the same year. Unused net capital losses can be carried back three years or forward with no time limit. They cannot be deducted against salary or interest. The superficial loss rule denies a loss if you buy back identical property within 30 days.

Is a non-registered account better than a TFSA?

For tax, a TFSA is almost always better, because nothing earned inside it is taxed. A non-registered account makes sense once TFSA and RRSP room is full, for money you move in and out often, for margin or short selling, and when you want deductible capital losses. Many investors use both, keeping heavily taxed income in the TFSA.

Do I pay tax when I withdraw from a non-registered account?

Not on the withdrawal itself. Taking out cash you contributed or cash already in the account is not taxed. But if you sell an investment to raise the cash, any gain on the sale is a capital gain, half of which is taxable. Income earned in the account is taxed in the year it is earned, whether you withdraw it or not.

How are US dividends taxed in a non-registered account?

The US withholds 15% with a W-8BEN on file, or 30% without one. You report the gross dividend as income, taxed at your full marginal rate with no dividend tax credit, and claim a foreign tax credit on Form T2209, plus Form T2036 outside Quebec. The credit is the lesser of the US tax and the Canadian tax on that income.

Should I hold dividend stocks or bonds in my taxable account?

As a general rule, investors who have both registered and taxable accounts often keep interest-paying investments, the most heavily taxed, in the TFSA or RRSP, and hold Canadian dividend payers or long-term growth investments in the taxable account, where they are taxed more lightly. This is general information about tax treatment, not a recommendation of any investment.

Can I move shares from my non-registered account to my TFSA?

Yes, as a contribution in kind, up to your TFSA room. The move is treated as selling the shares at their current value. A gain is taxable in the taxable account, and a loss on the transfer is not allowed. Selling a losing holding first and contributing the cash keeps the loss available.

Is a joint non-registered account taxed 50/50?

Not automatically. Income and gains in a joint account are generally taxed to each owner in proportion to the money each one contributed. If one spouse supplied all the money, the attribution rules can tax all the income to that spouse. Please keep records of who contributed what, and get advice before using a joint account to split income.

Work out the tax on your next sale or dividend, then compare where your money is taxed least.
