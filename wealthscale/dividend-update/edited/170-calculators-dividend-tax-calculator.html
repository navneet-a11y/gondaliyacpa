At a glance

What the calculator answers. How much 2026 federal and provincial tax you pay on Canadian dividends received in a non-registered account, given your other taxable income, and how much of each dividend you keep.

What you need. Your province on December 31, 2026, your other taxable income for the year, and the actual amounts of eligible and other than eligible dividends from your slips or brokerage statements.

The 2026 figures it uses. A 38% gross-up and a 15.0198% federal credit for eligible dividends; a 15% gross-up and a 9.0301% federal credit for other than eligible dividends; each confirmed province's 2026 credit; and the 2026 federal and provincial brackets and basic personal amounts.

Provinces covered. All 13: Ontario, British Columbia, Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut.

The method. Tax on dividends equals tax on your other income plus the grossed-up dividends, minus the dividend tax credits, less tax on your other income alone. A negative result means the credits also cut the tax on your other income.

1. How to Use the Dividend Tax Calculator

Province. Pick the province where you lived on December 31, 2026. That province's credit and brackets apply to all your dividends for the year, even those paid before you moved.

Other taxable income. Enter your 2026 taxable income from everything except these dividends: salary, pensions, interest, rent, self-employment profit and the taxable half of any capital gains, minus deductions such as RRSP and FHSA contributions. The calculator adds the dividends on top, so this figure decides which brackets the dividends fall into. If you have not finished the year, last year's taxable income is a reasonable starting point.

Eligible dividends. Enter the actual amount received, not the taxable amount. Your slip shows both; use the smaller figure, labelled as the actual amount. Most dividends from Canadian public companies, including those paid through Canadian equity ETFs and mutual funds, are eligible dividends.

Other than eligible dividends. Enter the actual amount of these too. They usually come from a private corporation, such as your own small business. The two boxes can be used together, for example by a business owner who also holds bank shares.

What the results mean. The headline is the extra tax caused by the dividends. Below it, the grossed-up amount is what is added to your taxable and net income, the effective rate is the extra tax divided by the dividends, and "you keep" is the dividends minus the extra tax. If the headline is negative, the dividends lowered your total tax.

Change one thing at a time. Moving your other income up or down by $10,000 shows how quickly the rate on dividends changes near a bracket line. For a full return including salary, gains and RRSP deductions, use the income tax calculator in section 7 or the income tax calculator page.

2. The Rules Behind the Numbers

Reading the combined columns. Each figure is the federal plus provincial credit per $100 received: for Ontario eligible dividends, $138 x (15.0198% + 10%) = $34.53. That is the tax the credits remove, before the tax on the $138 itself. For Quebec residents the federal credit is worth 16.5% less in practice, because the Quebec abatement is worked out after it. New Brunswick and Newfoundland and Labrador publish their rates without saying what they apply to; the calculator applies them to the grossed-up dividend, as the CRA's provincial forms do.

Province-specific items. For Ontario, the calculator includes the surtax and the Ontario Health Premium; Ontario works out its surtax before subtracting its dividend credit, which raises Ontario's rates above about $95,000 of taxable income. For British Columbia it includes the low-income tax reduction, and for Quebec the 16.5% federal abatement.

What is left out. Credits other than the basic personal amounts, minimum tax, the OAS recovery tax, Quebec's prescription drug insurance premium and any foreign dividends are not included. Credits cannot push your tax below zero, so very large negative results are capped by the tax you actually owe on other income. Our dividend tax credit guide explains each rule in full.

3. Worked Examples

This example is hypothetical. Three people use the dividend tax calculator for 2026.

  1. Maya, Ontario, $75,000 of other income, $8,000 of eligible dividends. The grossed-up amount is $11,040 ($8,000 x 1.38), all taxed at 20.5% federal and 9.15% Ontario: $11,040 x 29.65% = $3,273.36. Credits: federal $11,040 x 15.0198% = $1,658.19 and Ontario $11,040 x 10% = $1,104.00. Tax: $3,273.36 - $1,658.19 - $1,104.00 = $511.17, or 6.39%. She keeps $7,488.83. As interest, the same $8,000 would cost $2,372.00 ($8,000 x 29.65%).
  2. Ben, British Columbia, retired with $40,000 of pension income and $20,000 of eligible dividends. Tax on his pension alone is $4,620.33. With the dividends added, his total tax falls by $919.59 to $3,700.74, so the dividends carry an effective rate of minus 4.60%. The credits on $27,600 of grossed-up dividends are larger than the tax on that amount at his rates, and the excess shelters part of the tax on his pension.
  3. Sophie, Quebec, $120,000 of salary and $15,000 of other than eligible dividends from her own corporation. The grossed-up amount is $17,250. Federal: $17,250 x 26% = $4,485.00, minus the $1,557.69 credit, equals $2,927.31, reduced by the 16.5% Quebec abatement to $2,444.30. Quebec: $12,245 x 24% + $5,005 x 25.75% = $2,938.80 + $1,288.79 = $4,227.59, minus the $589.95 credit, equals $3,637.64. Total $6,081.94, or 40.55% of the dividend.

Each figure is what the calculator returns for the same inputs, using 2026 brackets, basic personal amounts and the confirmed 2026 credits. Other credits, such as the age amount and the pension income amount, are left out, so a retiree's own figures can differ.

What the examples show. Maya's dividends cost less than a third of what interest would, because her income sits where Ontario's credit and the federal credit cover most of the tax. Ben's dividends lower his tax, which only works because he has tax on other income for the extra credit to absorb. Sophie pays the most because other than eligible dividends get a smaller credit and her income is in Quebec's top two brackets.

Why Ontario's rates climb so fast. Ontario's surtax adds 20% of Ontario tax over $5,818 and 36% of Ontario tax over $7,446, and it is measured before the Ontario dividend tax credit comes off. Grossed-up dividends therefore count fully toward the surtax thresholds, which are crossed at about $95,000 and $112,000 of taxable income. Between $100,000 and $120,000 the rate on eligible dividends almost triples, from 8.92% to 25.38%.

The top Ontario rates. Above $258,482 of taxable income, eligible dividends are taxed at 39.34% and other than eligible dividends at 47.74%. The federal part is 24.81% and 27.57%; the rest is Ontario tax including the 56% surtax on Ontario tax at that level.

2027 change for business owners. Ontario's credit on other than eligible dividends falls from 2.9863% to 1.9863% of the grossed-up amount in 2027, adding $115 of Ontario tax per $10,000 of such dividends for anyone with enough Ontario tax to use the credit. What limits each figure. In British Columbia, New Brunswick, Alberta, Saskatchewan and the three territories, federal tax is the limit: at $80,755 of eligible dividends, the $111,441.90 grossed-up amount produces federal tax that the federal credit and basic personal amount just cover. Who this matters to. Early retirees living on a non-registered portfolio, adult children who receive dividends on shares they genuinely own, and business owners deciding how to pay themselves in a low-income year. Please remember that dividends paid by a private family company can be caught by the tax on split income rules.

8. Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the Territories

Six jurisdictions added. Alberta's dividend tax credit is 8.12% of the grossed-up amount for eligible dividends and 2.18% for other than eligible dividends, the rates on CRA's Worksheet AB428 (line 61520). The same worksheets give 11% and 2.519% for Saskatchewan (SK428), 8% and 0.7835% for Manitoba (MB428), 12.02% and 0.67% for Yukon (YT428), 11.5% and 6% for the Northwest Territories (NT428) and 5.51% and 2.61% for Nunavut (NU428). The calculator and the income tax calculator now use these rates.

Why the 2025 worksheets. CRA publishes the 2026 worksheets in January 2027. None of the six 2026 budgets changed personal income tax, so the 2025 worksheet rates are the 2026 rates. The federal side is the same everywhere: the 38% and 15% gross-ups and the federal credits in section 2 apply in every province and territory.

Where else they appear. The same rates are used in the tables on this page, the dividend tax credit guide and the province pages for Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut.

At filing time. Certified tax software applies the official provincial rates when you file, whatever the status on this page. Our ranking of the best tax software and our guide on how to file taxes online cover that step.

9. Special Cases the Calculator Does Not Show

OAS recovery tax. The calculator shows income tax only. If you receive OAS and your net income is above $95,323 for 2026, each $10,000 of eligible dividends also costs $2,070 of recovery tax ($13,800 x 15%). The OAS clawback page has its own calculator.

Registered accounts. The calculator is for non-registered accounts only. Leave out anything paid inside a registered plan such as a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA, since it is not taxed as it is paid; see our explainer on non-registered accounts.

US and foreign dividends. They get no gross-up and no credit. Add them to your other income instead, and see the foreign tax credit guide for the 15% US treaty withholding.

Mutual fund and ETF distributions. Only the Canadian dividend part of a distribution goes in the calculator. Interest and foreign income belong in other income, and capital gains distributions are half taxable.

Negative results. A negative figure only helps if you have enough tax on other income for the credit to reduce. If your tax is already zero, the extra credit is lost, since the dividend tax credit is non-refundable and cannot be carried forward.

Dividend Tax Calculator FAQ

How does the dividend tax calculator work?

It calculates your 2026 tax twice: once on your other income, and once on your other income plus the grossed-up dividends minus the federal and provincial dividend tax credits. The difference is the tax on your dividends. It uses 2026 brackets, basic personal amounts, the 38% and 15% gross-ups and each confirmed province's credit.

Which provinces does the dividend tax calculator cover?

All 13 provinces and territories. For Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut it uses the credit rates on CRA's 2025 worksheets (line 61520); CRA publishes the 2026 worksheets in January 2027, and none of those six 2026 budgets changed personal income tax.

Should I enter the actual or the taxable amount of my dividends?

Enter the actual amount you received. The calculator applies the gross-up itself: 38% for eligible dividends and 15% for other than eligible dividends. If you enter the taxable amount from your slip by mistake, the dividends are grossed up twice and the result will not match your return.

What is the dividend tax rate in Ontario for 2026?

It depends on your income. On eligible dividends, Ontario's combined 2026 rate is minus 8.24% at $50,000 of taxable income, 6.39% at $75,000, 27.53% at $150,000 and 39.34% at the top. On other than eligible dividends it is 8.09%, 20.28%, 37.90% and 47.74% at the same levels.

What does the calculator show for $10,000 of eligible dividends?

At $75,000 of other income, the 2026 tax is lowest in British Columbia ($162.87), followed by Yukon ($339.51) and the Northwest Territories ($356.07), and is $1,638.88 in Quebec. At $250,000 it runs from $2,286.08 in Yukon to $4,030.00 in Newfoundland and Labrador. Section 4 lists all 13 provinces and territories at six incomes.

Why is my dividend tax result negative?

At lower incomes the dividend tax credits can be larger than the tax on the grossed-up dividend. The extra credit reduces the tax on your other income, so your total tax falls. It only helps if you have tax on other income to reduce, because the credit is non-refundable and cannot create a refund.

How much in dividends can I receive tax-free in Canada?

With no other income and only the basic personal amounts, a resident of British Columbia, New Brunswick, Alberta, Saskatchewan or any of the three territories can receive $80,755 of eligible dividends with no 2026 income tax, a Quebec resident $53,310 and an Ontario resident $14,492, where the Health Premium sets the limit. Minimum tax and benefit effects are not included.

Does the calculator include the OAS clawback?

No. It shows income tax only. If your 2026 net income is above $95,323 and you receive OAS, add 15% of the grossed-up dividend: $2,070 for each $10,000 of eligible dividends and $1,725 for each $10,000 of other than eligible dividends, until your OAS is fully recovered.

Can I use the calculator for US dividends?

No. US and other foreign dividends get no gross-up and no dividend tax credit; they are taxed like interest. Enter them as other income in the income tax calculator instead. US tax withheld at the 15% treaty rate in a non-registered account can usually be claimed as a foreign tax credit.

How accurate is the dividend tax calculator?

It uses the confirmed 2026 gross-ups, credits, brackets and basic personal amounts, checked on October 23, 2026, and matches the tables on this page. It leaves out other credits, minimum tax and benefit clawbacks, so treat it as a close estimate. Our editorial standards explain how figures are checked.
