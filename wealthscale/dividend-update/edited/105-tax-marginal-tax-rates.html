Marginal Tax Rates in Canada 2026 by Province

The marginal tax rate Canada residents pay is the combined federal and provincial tax on their next dollar of income, and in 2026 the top rate ranges from 44.50% in Nunavut to 54.80% in Newfoundland and Labrador. At $100,000 of taxable income it is 30.50% in Alberta, 31.48% in Ontario and 36.12% in Quebec. Capital gains are taxed at half your ordinary rate, and eligible dividends at much less. At a glance

What a marginal rate is. The marginal tax rate is the share of your next dollar of taxable income that goes to federal and provincial income tax. It is the rate that decides what a raise, a bonus or an RRSP deduction is worth.

Top rates for 2026. The highest combined rate on ordinary income is 54.80% in Newfoundland and Labrador, over $1,141,275. Nova Scotia reaches 54.00%, Ontario 53.53%, British Columbia 53.50% and Quebec 53.31%. Nunavut's top rate is the lowest at 44.50%.

Capital gains. Half of a capital gain is taxable in 2026, so the marginal rate on a gain is half the ordinary rate: from 22.25% to 27.40% at the top.

Dividends. Eligible dividends are taxed at the lowest rates of all because of the gross-up and dividend tax credit. Rates are shown for all 13 provinces and territories.

Rates move inside brackets. Phase-outs of basic personal amounts, the Ontario surtax and Health Premium and low-income tax reductions in Ontario and British Columbia all change the rate at points that are not bracket thresholds.

What is included. Every rate uses 2026 brackets, basic personal amounts, the Ontario surtax, Health Premium and tax reduction, the B.C. tax reduction and the 16.5% Quebec abatement. CPP and EI contributions are not included.

1. What a Marginal Tax Rate in Canada Is

The rate on the next dollar. Canada taxes income in brackets, federally and in every province. Your marginal rate is the federal rate for your bracket plus the provincial rate for your provincial bracket, adjusted for any surtax, premium, credit phase-out or abatement that changes with income. It answers one question: if your taxable income rose by $100, how much of that $100 would go to income tax?

Marginal versus average. Your average rate is total tax divided by income. It is always lower than your marginal rate once you are past the first bracket, because the early dollars are taxed at lower rates or not at all. This example is hypothetical. An Ontario resident with $80,000 of taxable income pays $15,497.25 of income tax in 2026, an average of 19.37%, but the next dollar is taxed at 29.65%.

Why the marginal rate matters more for decisions. A raise, a bonus, overtime, a rental profit, an RRSP or FHSA deduction and a pension split all change income at the margin. Each is worth, or costs, your marginal rate. The average rate tells you what share of your whole income goes to tax, which helps for budgeting but not for decisions about the next dollar.

Different income, different rate. The same person can face several marginal rates at once: one on salary or interest (ordinary income), one on capital gains and two on Canadian dividends. Interest, salary, rent, RRSP and RRIF withdrawals and foreign dividends are all ordinary income. The income tax calculator Canada 2026 shows all four rates for your income.

Combined means federal plus provincial. The federal part is the same in every province except Quebec, where the 16.5% abatement lowers it. The provincial part is what makes rates differ so much across the country. Our guide to federal tax brackets 2026 covers the federal layer on its own, and the Canadian income tax guide explains how the two fit together.

Where the top starts later. Alberta's 15% bracket starts at $370,220, Manitoba's top rate applies once its basic personal amount is fully phased out at $400,000, Yukon's 15% bracket starts at $500,000 and Newfoundland and Labrador's 21.8% bracket starts at $1,141,275. Manitoba's top rate falls at $400,000. Between $200,000 and $400,000 of net income, Manitoba phases out its $15,780 basic personal amount, which adds 0.85 points to the marginal rate. Once the amount is gone, the rate drops from 51.25% to 50.40%.

Top rates on investment income. At the top, capital gains are taxed at half the ordinary rate. Eligible dividends range from 28.33% in the Northwest Territories to 46.20% in Newfoundland and Labrador at the top, while other than eligible dividends range from 36.82% to 49.99%.

The highest rate is not always at the top. In Ontario, the marginal rate is 73.26% for the $600 between $200,000 and $200,600, and 54.65% between $72,000 and $72,600, because the Health Premium phases in there. Section 7 explains why.

Ontario marginal tax rates

Dividend rates use Ontario's 10% and 2.9863% credits. The Ontario tax brackets page and our Ontario Health Premium guide explain each step.

CRA's 2026 figures describe the lifetime capital gains exemption deduction limit as half of the $1,275,000 exemption "since ½ of the capital gain is taxable", and the proposed increase to a two-thirds inclusion rate was cancelled. 7. Why Rates Jump Inside a Bracket

Basic personal amount phase-outs. The federal basic personal amount shrinks by $1,623 between $181,440 and $258,482 of net income, adding 0.29 points to the federal rate in that band. Yukon's amount follows the federal one. Manitoba's $15,780 amount phases out between $200,000 and $400,000, adding 0.85 points.

The Ontario surtax. Ontario charges a surtax of 20% of basic Ontario tax over $5,818 and a further 36% over $7,446. For someone with only the basic personal amount, those thresholds are reached at $94,901 and $111,810 of taxable income. Above both, every Ontario bracket rate is effectively multiplied by 1.56, so the 13.16% top Ontario rate becomes 20.53%.

The Ontario Health Premium. The premium rises in steps from $0 at $20,000 of taxable income to $900 at $200,600. Each step phases in at 6% or 25% of income over a short band, which is why Ontario shows bands at 44.05% ($48,000 to $48,600), 54.65% ($72,000 to $72,600) and 73.26% ($200,000 to $200,600).

Low-income tax reductions. Ontario's tax reduction is twice the $300 basic amount, minus basic Ontario tax plus surtax. It wipes out Ontario tax up to $18,930 for a person with only the basic personal amount, then shrinks, doubling the Ontario rate to 10.1% until it is used up at $24,870. British Columbia's $690 reduction shrinks by 3.56% of income over $25,570 and is gone at $44,952.

The Quebec abatement. The 16.5% abatement lowers every federal rate for Quebec residents to 83.5% of its headline value, which is why Quebec's combined rates contain figures such as 36.12% (17.1175% federal plus 19% Quebec).

Other credits shift the edges. These tables assume only the basic personal amount. Credits such as CPP, EI, the Canada employment amount and the age amount move the point where tax starts, and in Ontario they also move the surtax thresholds. The calculators use the same assumption.

8. How to Use Your Marginal Rate for RRSP Decisions

The deduction is worth your marginal rate. An RRSP deduction lowers taxable income, so it saves tax at the rate on the dollars it removes. A deduction that stays inside one band saves exactly that band's rate. A larger one can cross into a lower band, so the last dollars save less than the first.

Manitoba is settled. The Income Tax Act (Manitoba) sets the brackets at $47,000 and $100,000 for 2024 and every later year, with no indexation, and CRA's 2026 payroll tables use the same figures. A CRA rates page shows $47,564 and $101,200. Those equal the legal thresholds raised by 1.2%, which does not match the Act. The law governs, so our tables and calculators use $47,000 and $100,000.

Dividend credits for the last six. For Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut, the dividend tax credits are the rates on CRA's 2025 provincial worksheets (line 61520), as a percent of the grossed-up dividend: Alberta 8.12% eligible and 2.18% other than eligible, Saskatchewan 11% and 2.519%, Manitoba 8% and 0.7835%, Yukon 12.02% and 0.67%, the Northwest Territories 11.5% and 6%, and Nunavut 5.51% and 2.61%. 11. Common Marginal Tax Rate Mistakes

Applying the top rate to all income. Someone at $150,000 in Ontario faces 44.97% on the next dollar, but their average rate is far lower. Use the marginal rate only for changes at the margin.

Ignoring the province. The same $100,000 income faces 27.50% in Nunavut and 38.00% in Nova Scotia. Use your own province's table, not a national figure.

Using the ordinary rate for a capital gain. Only half of a gain is taxable, so the rate on a gain is half the ordinary rate. Using the full rate doubles the tax you expect on a sale.

Forgetting the Ontario spikes. A bonus that pushes income from $71,900 to $72,700 in Ontario crosses the 54.65% band. The extra cost is only $150 of Health Premium, but it surprises people who expected 29.65%.

Assuming a deduction saves one flat rate. A $30,000 RRSP deduction from $120,000 in Alberta crosses from 36.00% into the 30.50% band. Please work out the saving on the whole amount with the RRSP calculator.

Treating withholding as the rate. RRSP withdrawals have 10%, 20% or 30% withheld outside Quebec, but the tax owed is set by your marginal rate when you file. A $10,000 withdrawal at a 43.41% rate leaves about $2,341 more to pay at tax time than the $2,000 withheld.

12. Strategies That Use Your Marginal Rate

These strategies are general and rule-based, not advice for your situation.

1. Deduct in your highest-rate years

An RRSP or FHSA deduction is worth more at a higher marginal rate. Carrying a deduction forward to a year with a higher rate can raise the saving by 10 points or more.

2. Avoid the Ontario premium bands

If your taxable income in Ontario sits just above $48,000, $72,000 or $200,000, a small RRSP deduction can save 44.05%, 54.65% or 73.26% on the dollars inside the band.

3. Hold interest in a TFSA

Interest is taxed at the full ordinary rate, so it gains the most from a TFSA. Eligible dividends and capital gains are taxed at lower rates outside one.

4. Split income in retirement

Moving up to half of eligible pension income to a spouse in a lower bracket can cut the household's marginal rate and the OAS recovery tax. See pension income splitting.

5. Time capital gains

Selling a gain in a year when your other income is low, such as the year after retirement, taxes the taxable half at a lower marginal rate.

6. Use the spousal RRSP

The higher earner deducts at today's high rate, and the withdrawals are later taxed to the lower-income spouse at a lower rate, subject to the three-year attribution rule. See the spousal RRSP guide.

7. Count benefit reductions

Families getting the CCB or the grocery benefit face higher effective rates. Deductions save both tax and benefit reduction for them.

8. Watch RRSP withdrawal timing

Withdraw in years when your marginal rate is low, for example between retirement and the start of CPP, OAS and RRIF minimums.

Illustrative Examples: Three Marginal Tax Rates

This example is hypothetical. Three people use the tables above to make a decision about their next dollar.

  1. Chen, Ontario, $73,000 of taxable income. Chen is $400 above the $72,600 band, so his marginal rate is 29.65%. A $1,000 RRSP deduction brings him to $72,000, through the 54.65% band. It saves $446.50: $400 x 29.65% ($118.60) plus $600 x 54.65% ($327.90).
  2. Amara, Alberta, $120,000 of taxable income, selling shares with a $20,000 gain. Half the gain, $10,000, is taxable, raising her income to $130,000, all inside the 36.00% band. Tax on the gain is $3,600 ($10,000 x 36.00%), or 18.00% of the gain.
  3. Luc, Quebec, $60,000 of taxable income. His combined rate is 36.12%: 17.1175% federal after the abatement plus 19% Quebec. A $2,000 raise adds $722.35 of tax ($2,000 x 36.1175%) and leaves him $1,277.65 more after income tax.

Marginal Tax Rate FAQ

What is a marginal tax rate in Canada?

Your marginal tax rate is the combined federal and provincial tax on your next dollar of taxable income. It depends on your province and your income. In 2026, an Ontario resident with $80,000 of taxable income pays 29.65% on the next dollar, while the average rate on all of that income is much lower, because lower brackets tax the earlier dollars at lower rates.

What is the top marginal tax rate in Canada for 2026?

The highest combined rate is 54.80% in Newfoundland and Labrador on taxable income over $1,141,275. Nova Scotia's top rate is 54.00%, Ontario's 53.53% and British Columbia's 53.50%. Nunavut has the lowest top rate at 44.50%. In most provinces the top rate starts at $258,482, where the federal 33% bracket begins.

What is my marginal tax rate in Ontario at $100,000?

At $100,000 of taxable income in Ontario, the 2026 combined marginal rate on ordinary income is 31.48%: 20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario tax raised by the 20% Ontario surtax. The rate on capital gains is 15.74%, on eligible dividends 8.92% and on other than eligible dividends 22.38%.

How is the marginal tax rate on capital gains worked out?

Only half of a capital gain is added to taxable income in 2026, so the marginal rate on a capital gain is half the rate on ordinary income at the same income. In Alberta at $100,000, the ordinary rate is 30.50% and the capital gains rate is 15.25%. Losses offset gains before the half is taken.

Why can the marginal tax rate on eligible dividends be negative?

Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and then earn federal and provincial dividend tax credits. At lower incomes, the credits on an extra dollar of dividends are larger than the tax on the grossed-up amount, so the extra credit also reduces tax on your other income. Credits cannot push total tax below zero.

How are the provincial dividend tax credits confirmed?

Our 2026 dividend rates use each province's own dividend tax credit, and we publish a rate only once that credit is confirmed from an official source. All 13 provinces and territories are now confirmed. For Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the three territories, we use the rates on CRA's 2025 provincial worksheets (line 61520); CRA publishes the 2026 worksheets in January 2027, and none of those six 2026 budgets changed personal income tax.

How do I use my marginal rate for an RRSP contribution?

An RRSP deduction saves tax at your marginal rate, so a higher rate now means a bigger saving. Compare it with the rate you expect when you withdraw in retirement. A $5,000 deduction at $100,000 of income saves $1,574.00 in Ontario and $1,525.00 in Alberta in 2026. A large deduction can cross into lower brackets, so check the whole amount.

Is my marginal tax rate the rate I pay on all my income?

No. The marginal rate applies only to the next dollar. Your average rate, total tax divided by income, is always lower once you are past the first bracket. At $100,000 in Ontario, the marginal rate is 31.48% but total tax of $21,520.55 is 21.52% of income. Use the marginal rate for decisions and the average rate for budgeting.

Why does Ontario show a 73.26% marginal rate just above $200,000?

The Ontario Health Premium rises from $750 to $900 as taxable income goes from $200,000 to $200,600, which adds 25% to the marginal rate in that $600 band. Together with 48.26% of federal and Ontario tax, that gives 73.26%. Similar short spikes happen at $48,000 and $72,000. Above each band the rate drops back.

Which Manitoba tax brackets are correct for 2026?

Manitoba's 2026 brackets are 10.8% up to $47,000, 12.75% from $47,000 to $100,000 and 17.4% over $100,000. The Income Tax Act (Manitoba) fixes those thresholds for 2024 and later years with no indexation. A CRA rates page shows $47,564 and $101,200, which do not match the Act. Our tables use the figures in the law.

Do marginal tax rates include CPP and EI?

No. The rates on this page are income tax only: federal and provincial tax, the Ontario surtax and Health Premium, the Quebec abatement and the Ontario and B.C. low-income tax reductions. CPP contributions of 5.95% up to $74,600 and EI premiums of 1.63% up to $68,900 are payroll deductions on top for employees below those ceilings.

Can benefit reductions raise my effective marginal rate?

Yes. Income-tested benefits shrink as income rises, which works like an extra tax. For an Ontario family with two children and $60,000 of family net income, the Canada Child Benefit falls by 13.5% and the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit by 5% of extra income, so the effective rate is 48.15% instead of 29.65%.
