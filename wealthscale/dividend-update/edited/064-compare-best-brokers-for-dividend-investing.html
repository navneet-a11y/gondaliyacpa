2. DRIP Broker Canada Guide: How a Broker DRIP Works

What a broker DRIP does. A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) at a broker uses each cash dividend to buy more of the same stock or ETF in the same account, instead of leaving the dividend as cash. You do not place an order, and at most brokers on this page there is no commission on the reinvestment. Over time, the extra shares pay dividends of their own, which is how reinvestment compounds.

Broker plans and company plans. Some companies run their own reinvestment plans through a transfer agent, which you join as a registered shareholder. This page covers the broker kind, which works inside your TFSA, RRSP or other brokerage account and needs no separate enrolment with each company. Inside a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF or other registered account, reinvestment has no tax effect at the time.

3. Whole-Share vs Fractional Reinvestment

One dividend, two outcomes. This example is hypothetical. Suppose you own 200 shares that pay $0.50 a share each quarter, and the share price is $45 on the payment date. The dividend is 200 x $0.50 = $100. A whole-share DRIP buys 2 shares (2 x $45 = $90) and leaves $100 minus $90 = $10 as cash. A fractional DRIP buys $100 / $45 = 2.222 shares and leaves nothing behind.

When a whole-share DRIP buys nothing. If a dividend is smaller than one share's price, a whole-share DRIP cannot buy anything. With 50 of the same shares, the quarterly dividend is 50 x $0.50 = $25, less than the $45 share price, so the whole $25 stays as cash. Small positions and high-priced shares are where fractional reinvestment helps most.

7. US Dividend Withholding Tax by Account Type

The two rates. The United States taxes dividends that US companies pay to Canadian residents. Under the Canada-US tax treaty, the rate is 15% when your broker has a valid Form W-8BEN on file. Without it, the US rate on US-source income paid to a foreign person is 30%. The tax is taken before the dividend reaches your account, so a DRIP reinvests only what is left.

The retirement exemption. Article XXI(2) of the treaty exempts dividends earned by arrangements operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits that are generally tax-exempt at home. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs are such arrangements, so dividends on US-listed securities held directly in them are exempt once your broker has the account documented. That is why the account you use for US-listed dividend payers matters so much.

Accounts that are not exempt. A TFSA, FHSA or RESP is not a pension or retirement arrangement, so the exemption does not apply: 15% is withheld, or 30% without a W-8BEN. These accounts pay no Canadian tax on that income, so there is nothing to claim a credit against, and the tax withheld is a permanent cost.

Non-registered accounts. In a cash or margin account, 15% is also withheld, but you report the gross dividend on your return and claim a foreign tax credit equal to the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. The federal credit goes on Form T2209 (line 40500), and outside Quebec the provincial part on Form T2036. US dividends get no gross-up or Canadian dividend tax credit, so they are taxed at your rate for ordinary income.

9. Which Account Suits Each Kind of Dividend

Canadian dividends in a TFSA. Canadian dividends in a TFSA are not taxed when paid or withdrawn, so the full dividend can be reinvested. The trade-off is that the dividend tax credit goes unused, because there is no tax for it to reduce. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and someone eligible since 2009 who has never contributed has $109,000 of room.

Canadian eligible dividends in a non-registered account. Outside registered accounts, eligible dividends get the gross-up and credit, which section 8 shows can leave a low effective rate: 6.39% at $60,000 of other income in Ontario, against 29.65% on the same amount of interest. That makes a non-registered account a reasonable home for Canadian eligible dividends once registered room is full. The grossed-up amount still counts in your net income, which matters for income-tested benefits.

The OAS effect. Old Age Security is reduced by 15% of net income above $95,323 for the 2026 income year, until it is fully recovered at $155,320 for those aged 65 to 74. Because of the gross-up, $5,000 of eligible dividends adds $6,900 to net income. For someone already above the threshold, that means $6,900 x 15% = $1,035.00 more in OAS recovery tax. Dividends earned in a TFSA do not count as income, so retirees near the threshold may prefer to hold dividend payers there.

Canadian dividends in an RRSP or RRIF. Dividends inside an RRSP or RRIF are not taxed when paid, but every withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income, and the dividend tax credit is lost. On the section 8 figures, eligible dividends held in a non-registered account can be taxed more lightly than the same dividends withdrawn later from an RRSP. Whether that matters depends on your tax rate now and in retirement; our TFSA vs RRSP comparison works through the trade-off.

13. Common Dividend Investing Mistakes

No W-8BEN on file. Without it, 30% of each US dividend is withheld instead of 15%. On US$1,000 of dividends, that is US$300 instead of US$150, and in a TFSA none of it comes back.

Expecting the RRSP exemption in a TFSA or FHSA. Only retirement arrangements (RRSP, RRIF, LIRA and LIF) are exempt from US withholding. The 15% withheld in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP is never recovered.

Leaving reinvested dividends out of your cost base. In a non-registered account, every reinvested dividend is taxed when paid and is also a purchase. If you do not add it to your adjusted cost base, your gain looks larger when you sell, and you pay tax on the same dollars twice.

Selling at a loss while the DRIP is on. A loss is denied as a superficial loss if you, or a person affiliated with you such as your spouse, acquire identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after. A DRIP purchase counts as an acquisition, so a reinvested dividend in that window can deny all or part of the loss. The denied loss can usually be added to the cost base of the shares you still hold. Switching the DRIP off for that holding before a tax-loss sale avoids the problem.

Assuming the DRIP is on. At National Bank and TD you must ask for it, and at Qtrade you enrol yourself. At Wealthsimple the switch covers the whole account, so holdings you wanted paid in cash will reinvest too. Please check the first dividend after you set it up.

Re-contributing a TFSA withdrawal too soon. A withdrawal is added back to TFSA room on January 1 of the next year. Putting it back in the same year without room creates an excess, taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess in each month it remains.

Ignoring account fees on a small account. A $100 yearly fee on a $5,000 account takes 2.0% of the balance every year. Check the balance and age waivers in section 5 before you open the account.

10 Dividend Investing Strategies That Cut Tax and Costs

1. Hold US-listed payers in a retirement account

US dividends on US-listed shares held directly in an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF are exempt from US withholding. The same dividends lose 15% in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP, with no way to recover it.

2. File the W-8BEN on every account

The form cuts US withholding from 30% to 15% wherever the retirement exemption does not apply. Check it on each account that holds US shares, including one you rarely use.

3. Use the TFSA for Canadian dividends you want to compound

Canadian dividends in a TFSA are never taxed and do not count as income, so the whole payment can be reinvested. The shelter is worth more at higher incomes: in Ontario, $5,000 of eligible dividends outside a TFSA is taxed at 25.38% with $120,000 of other income, against 6.39% at $60,000.

4. Keep eligible dividends outside once room is full

The gross-up and credit leave $5,000 of eligible dividends taxed at 6.39% with $60,000 of other income in Ontario, against 29.65% on interest. A non-registered account can suit Canadian eligible dividends after registered room is used.

5. Prefer fractional reinvestment for small positions

A whole-share DRIP reinvests nothing when a dividend is smaller than one share's price. If your positions are small or your shares are high-priced, a broker that reinvests fractionally keeps every dollar working.

6. Switch the DRIP off before a tax-loss sale

A reinvested dividend within 30 days before or after a sale at a loss can make the loss superficial. Turn reinvestment off for that holding first, in a non-registered account.

7. Record every reinvestment in your cost base

Each reinvested dividend in a non-registered account is taxed and is also a purchase. Adding it to your adjusted cost base stops you paying tax twice when you sell.

8. Watch the OAS threshold in retirement

The gross-up raises net income, and OAS is reduced by 15% above $95,323 for 2026. Near that line, dividends earned in a TFSA can protect your OAS.

9. Take RRIF dividends in cash

A RRIF must pay a minimum each year: 5.28% at age 71, or $7,920 on $150,000. Cash dividends inside the RRIF can fund part of it without selling shares.

10. Meet fee waivers or move

National Bank waives its $100 fee at $20,000, and TD waives its quarterly fee at $15,000 or with $100 a month of pre-authorized contributions. If you cannot meet a waiver, a broker without the fee may cost less, and several reimburse transfer fees.

Illustrative Example: Placing Canadian and US Dividends in Ontario

This example is hypothetical. It uses 2026 federal and Ontario tax rates and published prices as of October 22, 2026, and ignores growth.

  1. The investor. Ana, 45, lives in Ontario and has $60,000 of employment income. Her holdings pay $4,000 a year of Canadian eligible dividends and US$1,000 a year of dividends on US-listed shares. Her broker has her Form W-8BEN.
  2. Option A: Canadian payers in a non-registered account, US payers in her TFSA. The gross-up is $4,000 x 1.38 = $5,520. Federal tax is $5,520 x 20.5% = $1,131.60 minus the credit of $5,520 x 15.0198% = $829.09, or $302.51. Ontario tax is $5,520 x 9.15% = $505.08 minus $5,520 x 10.0% = $552.00, or minus $46.92. Canadian tax: $302.51 minus $46.92 = $255.59. US tax in the TFSA: US$1,000 x 15% = US$150.00, not recoverable.
  3. Option B: Canadian payers in her TFSA, US payers in her RRSP. Canadian tax on the dividends: $0. US tax: $0, because US-listed shares held directly in an RRSP are exempt.
  4. The yearly difference. Option A costs $255.59 of Canadian tax and US$150.00 of US tax a year; Option B costs neither. Over 10 years at the same amounts, that is $255.59 x 10 = $2,555.90 and US$150.00 x 10 = US$1,500.00.
  5. If the $4,000 were interest. At her 29.65% combined rate, $4,000 x 29.65% = $1,186.00 of tax, which shows how much the dividend tax credit is already saving her in Option A.
  6. The room check. Option B works only if Ana has enough TFSA and RRSP room to hold those shares. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and her RRSP room depends on her earned income and past contributions.
  7. The broker cost. At Questrade, both accounts reinvest fractionally once fractional trading is on, with no transaction fees, and new purchases cost $0. At TD, reinvestment is whole shares only, with no commission, and each new purchase costs $9.99.

For Ana, where each holding sits saves more each year than which broker she uses. Both choices compound over time.

Are reinvested dividends taxable?

Yes, in a non-registered account. A reinvested dividend is taxed in the year it is paid, as if you had received the cash, and it appears on your tax slip. Each reinvestment also raises your adjusted cost base, so keep a record to avoid paying tax twice when you sell. In a TFSA, RRSP or RRIF there is no tax when the dividend is paid.

How much US withholding tax do Canadians pay on US dividends?

US withholding tax is 15% under the Canada-US tax treaty when your broker has your Form W-8BEN, and 30% without it. On US$1,000 of US dividends, that is US$150 or US$300. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs are exempt on US-listed securities held directly, once the broker has the account documented.

Do I pay US withholding tax on US dividends in a TFSA?

Yes. A TFSA is not a retirement arrangement under the treaty, so 15% is withheld with a W-8BEN on file, and that tax cannot be recovered because the TFSA pays no Canadian tax to credit it against. The same applies to an FHSA and an RESP. US$2,000 of US dividends a year in a TFSA loses US$300.

How does the dividend tax credit work in Canada?

Eligible dividends from Canadian corporations are grossed up by 38% and other than eligible dividends by 15%, and the grossed-up amount is added to your income. Federal and provincial credits are then subtracted: federally, 15.0198% of the grossed-up eligible amount and 9.0301% of the other kind. The credit applies only outside registered accounts and not to foreign dividends.
