1. Saskatchewan Tax Brackets 2026 and Rates

Three brackets, the fewest in Canada. Saskatchewan and Manitoba are the only provinces with just three income tax brackets. Alberta has six, British Columbia seven and Newfoundland and Labrador eight. With three steps, most Saskatchewan earners face a single provincial rate on every extra dollar, which makes planning simpler.

How to read the table. Each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its bracket. Someone with $80,000 of taxable income pays 10.5% on the first $54,532 ($5,725.86) and 12.5% on the remaining $25,468 ($3,183.50), for $8,909.36 before credits. The Saskatchewan basic personal amount credit of $2,140.01 then comes off, which leaves $6,769.35 of provincial tax. The last two columns show the tax on a whole bracket and the running total at its upper edge, before any credit.

Indexation for 2026. Saskatchewan raised its bracket thresholds by its 2.0% indexation rate for 2026, the same rate the federal government used. Indexation keeps a raise that only matches inflation from pushing you into a higher bracket. The rates themselves (10.5%, 12.5% and 14.5%) did not change.

Who pays Saskatchewan rates. The province where you live on December 31, 2026 taxes your whole year's income. If you moved to Regina from Calgary in October, you file as a Saskatchewan resident for 2026 and these brackets apply to all of your income, including what you earned in Alberta. Income tax deducted at source in another province is still credited against your final bill.

Taxable income, not salary. The brackets apply to taxable income: employment, self-employment, interest, rental income, the taxable half of capital gains and pensions, minus deductions such as RRSP and FHSA contributions, the deduction for enhanced CPP contributions and union dues. That is why the income tax calculator asks for deductions separately. A worker earning $82,000 who deducts a $6,000 RRSP contribution has $76,000 of taxable income before any other deductions.

5. Saskatchewan Basic Personal Amount 2026 and Credits

$20,381 for 2026. The Saskatchewan basic personal amount is $20,381 for 2026, up $890 from $19,491 in 2025. Every resident can claim it in full; Saskatchewan does not reduce it at higher incomes. The credit equals the amount times the lowest provincial rate: $20,381 x 10.5% = $2,140.005, or $2,140.01 rounded.

Why it rose faster than prices. The Saskatchewan Affordability Act adds an extra $500 to the basic personal amount each year from 2025 to 2028, on top of normal indexation. For 2026 the province applied its 2.0% indexation rate and then the $500. A Saskatchewan resident with income above the amount saves $52.50 a year from the $500 alone ($500 x 10.5%), and more as the extra steps build to 2028.

Second highest in Canada. Only Alberta ($22,769) has a larger provincial basic personal amount. Ontario's is $12,989 and Nova Scotia's $11,932. The gap matters most at modest incomes: a Saskatchewan resident pays no provincial tax on the first $20,381, while an Ontario resident starts paying provincial tax above $12,989 (before Ontario's own tax reduction).

Payroll credits for CPP and EI. In CRA's 2026 payroll formulas, base CPP contributions (up to $3,519.45) and EI premiums (up to $1,123.07) earn a Saskatchewan credit at 10.5%, worth up to $487.46 a year, alongside a federal credit at 14% worth up to $649.95. The Canada employment amount ($1,501, worth $210.14 federally) is a federal credit only; Saskatchewan has no provincial version in the payroll tables.

Other credits. Federal 2026 amounts include the age amount ($9,208, reduced above net income of $46,432 and gone at $107,819), the pension income amount (up to $2,000), the disability amount ($10,341) and medical expenses above the lesser of 3% of net income and $2,890. Saskatchewan has its own set of non-refundable credits on its provincial form. Apart from the basic personal amount, we have not yet verified their 2026 Saskatchewan amounts, so this page does not quote them and the calculator leaves them out.

6. Surtaxes, Premiums and Abatements in Saskatchewan

No surtax. Saskatchewan has no provincial surtax for 2026. In CRA's 2026 payroll formulas, a provincial surtax applies only to Ontario residents, where it adds 20% of basic Ontario tax over $5,818 and 36% of basic Ontario tax over $7,446. Saskatchewan tax is simply the bracket tax minus credits.

No health premium. Saskatchewan does not charge a provincial health premium through the tax system. Ontario's Health Premium, up to $900 a year, is the only one built into the 2026 payroll formulas, and our Ontario Health Premium page covers it.

No abatement. The 16.5% federal abatement applies only to Quebec residents, who file a separate Quebec return. Saskatchewan residents pay full federal tax and claim no abatement.

No payroll tax reduction. CRA's payroll formulas include a provincial tax reduction only for Ontario and British Columbia. The tables and calculators on this page therefore apply none for Saskatchewan. If you have a low income, your final Saskatchewan tax on your return can differ from these tables because of provincial credits that are not built in.

What that means for your rate. With no add-ons, the Saskatchewan rate you see in section 1 is the rate you pay. That makes Saskatchewan's marginal rates easy to predict: 10.5%, 12.5% or 14.5% on top of the federal rate, with no hidden bumps from a surtax threshold. Ontario residents, by contrast, face a combined 43.41% just above $117,045 because the surtax lifts the provincial rate.

9. Capital Gains Tax in Saskatchewan

Enter your other income, sale proceeds and cost to see the tax on a capital gain in Saskatchewan.

Half the gain is taxed. For 2026, half of a net capital gain is added to taxable income and taxed at your marginal rate. The other half is tax-free. A Saskatchewan resident therefore pays from 12.25% on a gain (in the 24.5% band) to 23.75% (above $258,482).

Worked example. This example is hypothetical. Dana, a Saskatoon resident with $120,000 of other taxable income, sells shares in a non-registered account for a $30,000 gain. The taxable half is $15,000 ($30,000 x 50%). Her income rises from $120,000 to $135,000, all inside the 38.5% band, so the tax is $15,000 x 38.5% = $5,775.00, an effective 19.25% of the gain. If she had $150,000 of other income and a $50,000 gain, the $25,000 taxable half would cross the $155,805 line: ($5,805 x 38.5%) + ($19,195 x 40.5%) = $2,234.925 + $7,773.975 = $10,008.90.

Losses and exemptions. A net capital loss can be carried back three years or forward with no time limit, but only against taxable capital gains; see our guide to capital losses. A loss is denied if you or an affiliated person buy the same property within 30 days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after, under the superficial loss rule. Gains on qualified small business shares and qualified farm or fishing property may qualify for the 2026 lifetime capital gains exemption of $1,275,000.

Inside registered accounts. Gains inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA are not taxed when they happen. The capital gains tax calculator handles a single sale with carried-forward losses for any province.

11. Ways to Lower Your Saskatchewan Tax

These are general, rule-based approaches. Which ones fit depends on your income, family and plans.

1. Deduct RRSP contributions in the 33% band

Between $58,523 and $117,045 of taxable income, every $1,000 of RRSP deduction saves $330 in Saskatchewan. Your 2026 room is 18% of 2025 earned income up to $33,810, plus unused room shown on your notice of assessment.

2. Save the deduction for a higher year

You can contribute this year and deduct later. A deduction claimed at $50,000 saves 24.5%; the same deduction in a year above $155,805 saves at least 40.5%. That makes carrying it forward worth considering before a promotion or a large sale.

3. Open an FHSA before you buy

Each year an FHSA adds $8,000 of room you can deduct, to a lifetime maximum of $40,000. In Saskatchewan the deduction saves at the same rates as an RRSP, and a qualifying withdrawal for a first home is tax-free. See our FHSA guide.

4. Put taxable savings in a TFSA

Interest is taxed at your full marginal rate, 33% at middle incomes in Saskatchewan. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and Saskatchewan residents can open one at 18. Income and gains inside never appear on your return. Our TFSA guide covers the rules.

5. Use a spousal RRSP

If one partner earns $130,000 and the other $35,000, the higher earner can contribute to a spousal RRSP, deduct at 38.5% and have the money withdrawn later in the lower earner's hands, subject to the three-year attribution rule.

6. Split pension income at 65

Couples can split eligible pension income on their returns. In Saskatchewan, shifting a dollar of pension income from a spouse taxed at 33% to one taxed at 24.5% saves 8.5 cents. Our pension income splitting guide explains who qualifies.

7. Harvest losses against gains

A loss realized in the same year is netted against your gain before half of it is taxed. Unused losses carry back three years or forward indefinitely. Wait out the 30-day superficial loss window before buying the same investment back.

8. Watch benefit and clawback lines

Deductions lower net income, which feeds the Canada Child Benefit, the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit and the OAS recovery tax (15% of net income above $95,323 for 2026). A deduction in those ranges can be worth more than the tax rates alone suggest.

Saskatchewan Tax Brackets 2026 FAQ

What are the Saskatchewan tax brackets for 2026?

Saskatchewan has three 2026 brackets: 10.5% on taxable income up to $54,532, 12.5% on income over $54,532 up to $155,805, and 14.5% on income over $155,805. The thresholds were indexed by 2.0% for 2026. The federal brackets, from 14% on the first $58,523 up to 33% above $258,482, apply on top of these Saskatchewan rates.

What is the Saskatchewan basic personal amount for 2026?

It is $20,381, up from $19,491 in 2025. The increase combines 2.0% indexation with an extra $500 a year from 2025 to 2028 under the Saskatchewan Affordability Act. The credit is worth $2,140.01 of tax, so a resident whose only credit is this amount pays no Saskatchewan tax on income up to $20,381.

How much tax do I pay on $60,000 in Saskatchewan?

On $60,000 of taxable income, 2026 tax is $10,462.08: $6,192.73 federal and $4,269.35 Saskatchewan, an average rate of 17.44%. An employee earning a $60,000 salary pays less income tax, $9,140.68, because CPP and EI credits, the enhanced CPP deduction and the Canada employment amount apply.

What is the top marginal tax rate in Saskatchewan?

The top combined rate for 2026 is 47.5% on taxable income over $258,482: 33% federal plus 14.5% Saskatchewan. Between $181,440 and $258,482 the rate is 43.79%, which includes the effect of the shrinking federal basic personal amount. Capital gains at the top are taxed at 23.75%.

Does Saskatchewan have a surtax or health premium?

No. Saskatchewan has no provincial surtax and no health premium for 2026. In CRA's 2026 payroll formulas, a surtax and an additional premium apply only in Ontario. Saskatchewan residents also get no federal abatement, which is available only to Quebec residents. Saskatchewan tax is the bracket tax minus credits.

What is the take-home pay on $80,000 in Saskatchewan?

An employee on an $80,000 salary takes home $59,021.87 in 2026, or $2,270.07 every two weeks. Deductions are CPP of $4,230.45, CPP2 of $216.00, EI of $1,123.07, federal tax of $9,242.60 and Saskatchewan tax of $6,166.02. This assumes one employer and no other income or credits.

How much does a $5,000 RRSP contribution save in Saskatchewan?

It depends on your income. In Saskatchewan, a $5,000 deduction is worth $1,225.00 to someone with $45,000 of taxable income, $1,421.01 at $60,000, $1,650.00 at $100,000 and $2,008.90 at $160,000. The saving is your marginal rate on the income the deduction removes, so it rises as income moves into higher brackets.

How are capital gains taxed in Saskatchewan?

Half of a 2026 net capital gain is added to taxable income and taxed at your combined marginal rate. That gives an effective rate on the full gain of 12.25% in the 24.5% band, 16.5% in the 33% band, 19.25% in the 38.5% band and 23.75% above $258,482. Gains earned inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA are sheltered from this tax.

Can I enter dividends for Saskatchewan in the calculator?

Yes. Saskatchewan's dividend tax credit is 11% of the grossed-up amount for eligible dividends and 2.519% for other than eligible dividends, the rates on CRA's Worksheet SK428 (line 61520). CRA publishes the 2026 worksheet in January 2027; Saskatchewan's 2026 budget made no change to personal income tax. The calculator applies these rates with the federal gross-up and credit.

Is Saskatchewan income tax lower than Alberta or Manitoba?

Saskatchewan tax is lower than Manitoba's at every income in our table and higher than Alberta's. At $60,000 of taxable income, a Saskatchewan resident owes $10,462.08 for 2026, against $9,171.21 in Alberta and $11,221.99 in Manitoba. Saskatchewan's top combined rate of 47.5% is below Manitoba's 50.4% and just under Alberta's 48%.

Which province taxes me if I moved to or from Saskatchewan in 2026?

The province where you lived on December 31, 2026 taxes your income for the whole year. If you moved to Saskatchewan in the fall, Saskatchewan brackets apply to all your 2026 income. If you left Saskatchewan before December 31, your new province's rates apply. Tax already withheld in either province counts toward your final bill.

Do I pay Saskatchewan tax on income below the basic personal amount?

No Saskatchewan tax is payable on taxable income up to $20,381 if the basic personal amount is your only credit, because the $2,140.01 credit cancels the 10.5% tax on that income. Federal tax starts sooner, above $16,452. Workers also earn credits for CPP and EI, so the point where tax begins is a little higher for them.
