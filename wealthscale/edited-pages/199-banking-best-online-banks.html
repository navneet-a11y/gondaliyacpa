2. Digital Banks Canada: Banks vs Fintechs

Not every app is a bank. Digital banks in Canada fall into two groups. Some are banks, or trade names of banks, built around an app; their deposits are covered by CDIC directly. Others are fintech companies that are not CDIC members and rely on member institutions instead. Both can be good choices, but the protection works differently.

What trust coverage means. When a fintech places your money in trust deposits at CDIC members, CDIC coverage can reach you through those members, subject to CDIC's rules for trust deposits. Using up to 10 members is how Wealthsimple gets to $1,000,000 combined. KOHO covers only funds you have opted in to earn interest. Neo does not name its member.

Sister brands share limits. Because Simplii is a CIBC trade name, money at Simplii and CIBC in your own name shares one $100,000 limit. Tangerine Bank is a member in its own right, separate from Scotiabank. Section 9 explains the categories.

Protected differently. Wealthsimple Savings is covered by CIPF, the investor protection fund, and is not CDIC-insured. Read CIPF vs CDIC and is Wealthsimple safe for the details.

Pairing a chequing account with a savings specialist. A common setup is a no-fee chequing account for pay and bills plus a higher-rate savings account elsewhere. Moving money between them takes a transfer, so keep a few days of spending in chequing.

9. Deposit Insurance at Online Banks

The CDIC rule. At each CDIC member, you are insured for up to $100,000 in each insured category, with interest counted inside that limit. The categories run in parallel: an account in your own name, a joint account, a trust account, and eligible deposits in an RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, RESP, RDSP or FHSA. Insurance applies to deposits such as GICs and term deposits; investments such as stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds and crypto are outside it.

Enter your balances in the calculator below, category by category, to see how much of them CDIC would cover at a single member.

Members. Equitable Bank (EQ Bank), Tangerine Bank, President's Choice Bank and Manulife Bank of Canada are members in their own right. Simplii is covered through CIBC, so Simplii and CIBC deposits in one name share a single limit.

Fintechs. Wealthsimple Chequing reaches up to $1,000,000 of combined CDIC coverage through trust deposits at up to 10 members, while Wealthsimple Savings relies on CIPF, up to $1,000,000. KOHO's trust coverage applies only to funds you opt in to earn interest. Neo's chequing funds are eligible through an unnamed member, and it did not state who protects Neo Savings.

Credit unions. Online credit union brands such as Saven, Hubert and Outlook are covered by provincial deposit insurance instead of CDIC.

Spreading a large balance. If you hold more than $100,000 in one name, spread it across unrelated members or across categories such as a TFSA. Two brands of the same bank, such as Simplii and CIBC, do not double your coverage.

13. How to Switch to an Online Bank

Open first, close last. Open the online account, confirm you can log in, deposit and send an e-Transfer, and keep your old account open until every payment has moved.

Move your pay. Give your employer the new institution, transit and account numbers. Your pay may land in the old account for one more cycle. Benefits paid by direct deposit need to be updated separately.

Move pre-authorized payments. List every automatic debit from the last two statements, such as rent, insurance, phone and loans, and update each one with the payee. Confirm the change took effect before you close the old account.

Watch the waiver month. A big-bank fee waiver that depends on your balance can be lost the day the balance falls short, which brings back the monthly fee. Move the last of the money right after the final payment clears.

Registered accounts. Moving a TFSA or RRSP between institutions uses a transfer form, not a withdrawal. See how to transfer an investment account.

14. Common Online Banking Mistakes

Assuming every app is CDIC-insured. Wealthsimple Savings is covered by CIPF, not CDIC; KOHO covers only opted-in funds; and Neo does not name its member.

Missing the direct-deposit rule. EQ Bank pays 1.00% instead of 2.75% if your pay goes elsewhere: $100 instead of $275 a year on $10,000.

Splitting money between sister brands for coverage. Simplii and CIBC share one CDIC limit, so splitting between them does not add coverage.

Dropping below a balance rule. Manulife charges $1.00 per transaction below $1,000, and KOHO Essential costs $7 a month without a qualifying deposit.

Ignoring new fees. Neo's e-Transfer, ATM and NSF fees start January 1, 2027 for anyone without Start, Build or Grow.

Closing the old account too soon. If an automatic payment hits a closed or empty account, you can face a returned-payment charge from the bank and a late fee from whoever you were paying.

Illustrative Example: Moving Chequing and Savings Online

This example is hypothetical. Sofia keeps $3,000 in a TD Unlimited Chequing Account and a $20,000 emergency fund in a TD ePremium Savings Account. Because her chequing balance stays below $4,000, she pays the $17.95 monthly fee. She considers moving both to EQ Bank, with her pay direct deposited.

  1. Chequing fees today. 12 x $17.95 = $215.40 a year.
  2. Interest today. TD Unlimited posts 0.000%, and TD ePremium Savings pays 0.450% on balances of $10,000 or more: $20,000 x 0.450% = $90.00.
  3. Net result today. $90.00 - $215.40 = -$125.40 a year.
  4. Chequing at EQ Bank. $3,000 x 2.75% = $82.50 a year, with no monthly fee.
  5. Savings at EQ Bank. In the 30 Day Notice Savings Account, $20,000 x 2.75% = $550.00 a year. She keeps one month of expenses in chequing, since notice savings needs 30 days' notice.
  6. Net result after the move. $82.50 + $550.00 = $632.50 a year, a gain of $757.90 ($632.50 + $125.40) before tax.
  7. Protection. Her $23,000 at EQ Bank in her own name is within CDIC's $100,000 limit for that category at Equitable Bank.

Interest is taxable, so her after-tax gain is smaller, but the fee saving of $215.40 is not taxed. If Sofia prefers to keep her emergency fund out of a notice account, Wealthsimple Savings at 2.5% would pay $500.00 on $20,000, covered by CIPF rather than CDIC.
