1. How We Ordered These Youth Accounts

Fee first. The plan for this page sorts youth accounts by fee, then by features. All six cost $0 a month for a child or teen, so the fee test is a six-way tie. We do not break ties with a score invented for this page; our published chequing scores cover adult accounts, not youth versions. Our general rules for rating providers are on our methodology page.

Then features, one at a time. Three feature tests come next, in a set order. The first test that tells two accounts apart decides which ranks higher:

  1. Unlimited transactions with no-fee Interac e-Transfers at every age the bank's youth accounts cover, as published. This is pass or fail; accounts that pass rank above accounts that do not, and test 2 orders each group.
  2. The bank's own fee at other banks' ATMs in Canada for teens, judged at the weakest published term for any teen age. Best to worst: waived at every other ATM; one withdrawal a month with no fee or with the fee rebated; a fee on every other-bank withdrawal; a fee, or ATM terms, not published.
  3. How long the $0 lasts, by age: to 25 beats to 23, which beats to the 19th birthday.

How the order came out. RBC, CIBC, TD and Scotiabank pass test 1. BMO does not, because its Kids Account for under-13s has no published transaction or e-Transfer terms, and neither does National Bank, because The Minimalist caps children under 14 at 18 transactions a month. Among the four that pass, test 2 gives RBC first (fee waived), CIBC second (one withdrawal rebated), TD third ($2.00 on every withdrawal, published) and Scotiabank fourth (no published fee from 16). Between the two that miss test 1, BMO's one free withdrawal a month beats National Bank's unpublished terms on The Connected. No tie reached test 3.

What the order leaves out. Offers never move an account, and only one youth offer in our check runs past November 4, 2026. Branch access, apps and parental tools are not part of the order. Youth chequing accounts pay little or nothing. Advantage Banking is on RBC's list of accounts with no interest, the Preferred Package earns none at Scotiabank, and TD shows no rate for its student account even though it says interest is worked out on every dollar. Leo's Young Savers did not publish a rate either. For money a child will save for months or years, a savings account at the same bank, or an RESP for education, is usually a better home. A simple saving habit. A child who saves $20 a month from age 8 to 18 deposits $2,400 (120 x $20). At an example rate of 2% a year, compounded monthly, that grows to $2,654.39, of which $254.39 is interest. The rate here is an example to show how compounding works, not a rate any youth account published.

Goals make it real. Use the savings goal calculator at the top of this page for a specific target. A teen who wants a $600 phone in 10 months needs to save $60 a month ($600 / 10). Our compound interest calculator and savings interest calculator pages explain the maths in more detail.

Interest and tax. Interest is taxable income. Where the money came from can affect who reports the interest, so please keep simple records of gifts from parents and relatives and of the child's own earnings, such as babysitting or part-time pay.

10. Saving Beyond the Bank Account: RESP and TFSA

RESP for education. A Registered Education Savings Plan is built for a child's education savings and attracts government grants. Our RESP guide explains the contribution limit and grant rules, and the RESP calculator projects the grants.

Canada Learning Bond. Children from lower-income families can receive the Canada Learning Bond in an RESP, even if the family puts in no money of its own. Our Canada Learning Bond guide gives the amounts and income limits, and our page on RESP grants covers the other grants.

TFSA at 18. TFSA room starts in the year a resident turns 18, even where a teen must wait until 19 to open one because that is the age of majority, and a valid SIN is needed to open it. Our TFSA guide, the TFSA contribution limit table and the TFSA calculator help a new adult start.

Family or individual RESP. If you have more than one child, a family plan can cover them all. See family vs individual RESP before you open one. Families who receive the Canada Child Benefit can read our Canada Child Benefit guide for amounts and payment dates.

11. Deposit Protection for a Child's Account

Who is covered. All six banks on this list (RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, CIBC and National Bank) belong to the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation. A child's eligible deposits at any of them are insured to $100,000 per category, interest included.

Categories matter for families. Money in the child's own name is one category. A parent's account held in trust for the child is another, joint deposits are a third, and deposits inside an RESP, TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, RDSP or FHSA each form their own. So a child's own account, an in-trust account and an RESP deposit at the same bank each get their own $100,000 limit.

What CDIC covers. Deposits such as chequing, savings, GICs and term deposits. An RESP that holds investments, such as funds or shares, gets no CDIC protection for those holdings. Read CIPF vs CDIC for how investments are protected.

Credit unions. A credit union or caisse regulated by its province is insured by that province's plan rather than by CDIC.

12. How to Choose the Best Bank Account for Kids in Canada

Choose for the next ten years, not the next month. The best bank account for kids in Canada for your family depends on your child's age, the ATMs near you and how long you want the account to stay at $0 a month. Work through these steps.

  1. Check the opening age. CIBC lets parents open online up to 11; RBC, BMO and TD need a branch for young children.
  2. Pick a bank you will keep. CIBC and Scotiabank use one youth account to 25; RBC switches at 13; BMO's discount ends at 19.
  3. Check the teen ATM rule. RBC waives its fee at other banks' ATMs for teens; TD charges $2.00 each.
  4. Confirm e-Transfers. Every teen account here includes no-fee e-Transfers, but National Bank's The Minimalist counts them in its 18-transaction cap, and BMO did not list them for its Kids Account.
  5. Add a savings home. Open a savings account or RESP for money that will sit, since youth chequing accounts pay little or no interest.
  6. Diary the end date. Note when youth pricing ends and plan the move to an adult account.

Same bank as a parent. Banking where a parent banks can make transfers and branch visits simpler, but it is not required, since e-Transfers between banks have no fee on these accounts.

13. Common Mistakes With Kids' and Teens' Accounts

Forgetting the 19th birthday at BMO. The Performance Plan costs $17.95 a month from then. Six years to 25 at full price is $1,292.40.

Searching for a discontinued account. Scotiabank's Getting There Savings for Youth was discontinued on May 26, 2025, though its page is still live.

Counting on an offer a minor cannot use. Some bank offers need the age of majority. Check the age rule before you open an account for a child because of a bonus.

Paying ATM fees by habit. Two other-bank withdrawals a month cost $48 a year at TD.

Leaving savings in chequing. Youth chequing accounts pay little or nothing. Savings belong in a savings account or RESP.

Missing the TFSA start. A teen gets a full year of TFSA room in the year they turn 18, even if they cannot open one until 19 in their province.

10 Strategies for a Child's First Bank Account

1. Choose an account that lasts

CIBC Smart Start and Scotiabank's youth package stay at $0 to 25. Fewer changes mean fewer chances to start paying a fee by accident.

2. Match ATMs to your neighbourhood

RBC waives its fee at other banks' ATMs for teens. If the nearby machines are TD's, TD's account costs nothing to use them.

3. Pay allowance by e-Transfer

Every teen account here sends and receives e-Transfers with no fee. Regular deposits teach budgeting and let a teen track spending.

4. Pair chequing with savings

Keep spending money in the youth account and savings in a savings account, so a child sees the two jobs money does.

5. Set a goal with a date

A $600 phone in 10 months is $60 a month. A concrete target makes saving easier than "save more".

6. Show compounding early

$20 a month from 8 to 18 is $2,400 deposited; at an example 2% it becomes $2,654.39. Small habits started early add up.

7. Open an RESP for education money

Government grants and, for eligible lower-income families, the Canada Learning Bond make an RESP the natural home for education savings.

8. Plan the move at 19 or 25

Set a reminder a few months before youth pricing ends to choose the next account together.

9. Start a TFSA at 18

TFSA room starts in the year a resident turns 18, and unused room carries forward, so a teen who opens later does not lose it.

10. Keep records of gifts

Keep simple notes on money from parents and relatives and on a child's own earnings. Tax rules can depend on where the money came from.

Illustrative Example: A Youth Account From 8 to 25

This example is hypothetical. Ava is 8 and lives in Ontario. Her parents want one account that lasts, plus a habit of saving $20 a month. They compare CIBC Smart Start with BMO's Kids Account and teen Performance Plan.

  1. Opening. CIBC lets her parents open Smart Start online because she is under 12. BMO needs a branch visit with a parent and ID.
  2. Fees from 8 to 18. Both cost $0 a month: CIBC's Smart Start, and BMO's Kids Account then its teen Performance Plan.
  3. Saving. She saves $20 a month for 120 months, depositing $2,400 (120 x $20). In a savings account at an example rate of 2% a year, compounded monthly, that grows to $2,654.39, including $254.39 of interest.
  4. Turning 18. In Ontario the age of majority is 18, so she can open a TFSA that year once she has a SIN, and her room starts that year.
  5. From 19 to 25. CIBC Smart Start stays at $0 until 25. BMO's discount ends on her 19th birthday; if she is not a full-time student and does not keep $4,000 every day, the Performance Plan costs 6 x $215.40 = $1,292.40 over those six years.
  6. ATMs as a teen. If she takes cash at other banks' ATMs twice a month from 13 to 18, BMO charges $2 for the second each month: $24 a year, or $144 over 6 years (6 x $24). CIBC rebates one a month and did not publish its fee for the second.

For Ava, CIBC's single account to 25 avoids the BMO fee from 19, which is worth up to $1,292.40 if she is not in full-time study. RBC, ranked first for its ATM waiver, would also be $0 to 24.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best bank account for kids in Canada in 2026?

On our fee-then-features order, RBC comes first: Leo's Young Savers Account is $0 for ages 0 to 12, and RBC Advantage Banking for students is $0 from 13 (14 in Quebec) to 24, with RBC's fee waived at other banks' ATMs in Canada. CIBC Smart Start, $0 until 25, is second.

At what age can a child open a bank account in Canada?

From birth, with a parent. RBC's Leo's Young Savers covers ages 0 to 12 and CIBC lets parents open Smart Start online up to age 11. Banks need a parent or guardian for young children: RBC under 13, BMO under 13 (14 in Quebec), TD under 14, and Scotiabank under 12 and at 12 and 13.

Can a teen open a bank account without a parent?

Sometimes. TD lets teens apply online from 14, and Scotiabank from 16; at Scotiabank, teens aged 14 and 15 may not need a parent if they meet the ID requirements. RBC lets under-18s apply for its student account in the RBC Mobile app. CIBC asks teens aged 14 and 15 to open at a banking centre.

Are kids' bank accounts free?

Each of the six charges $0 a month, yet a few costs can still apply: $2.00 per withdrawal at another bank's ATM with TD, $2 after one free withdrawal a month with BMO and with Scotiabank under 16, and $1 per e-Transfer at RBC after 999 in a month. Regular fees start when youth pricing ends.

What is the best teen bank account in Canada?

For teens who use other banks' ATMs, RBC Advantage Banking for students, because RBC waives its own access fee at non-RBC ATMs in Canada. For one account that lasts to 25, CIBC Smart Start. BMO's teen plan gives the full Performance Plan at $0 for ages 13 to 18, but the discount ends on the 19th birthday.

When does a youth bank account start charging fees?

BMO's teen discount ends on the 19th birthday, TD's no-fee period at 23, and RBC, CIBC, Scotiabank and National Bank at 25. RBC, Scotiabank and TD extend $0 pricing for full-time students. After that, fees range from $3.95 on National Bank's The Minimalist to $17.95 on BMO's Performance Plan.

Do kids' bank accounts pay interest?

Little or nothing. RBC puts Advantage Banking on its no-interest list, Scotiabank's Preferred Package earns none, TD gives no rate for its student account, and Leo's Young Savers did not publish one. Savings that will sit for a while earn more in a savings account or RESP.

Can my child get a debit card?

Yes, on most youth accounts. RBC offers the Virtual Visa Debit from age 14 for online shopping, and CIBC's offer terms refer to Visa Debit purchases on Smart Start. The other banks did not describe their youth cards on the pages we checked, so please ask when you open.

Is my child's money insured?

Yes, at the six big banks, which are all CDIC members. Eligible deposits are insured up to $100,000 per insured category at each member. A child's own account, a parent's account held in trust for the child and an RESP deposit are separate categories, each insured up to $100,000.

What happened to Scotiabank's Getting There Savings account?

Scotiabank ended Getting There Savings for Youth on May 26, 2025. Holders were moved to the Preferred Package for Students and Youth, a chequing package that costs $0 under 25 and includes unlimited debit and e-Transfers. If your child held the old account, check a recent statement for the current account name and terms.

Are there welcome offers for kids' bank accounts?

Few. As checked October 23, 2026, the only youth-account offer that runs past November 4, 2026 is CIBC's $125 cash for a new Smart Start account, with no end date stated. It needs 5 eligible Visa Debit purchases within 60 days, and its terms did not say whether minors qualify, so ask CIBC first.

Should I open an RESP or a bank account for my child?

They do different jobs. A bank account is for spending and short-term saving. An RESP is for education savings and attracts government grants, and children in eligible lower-income families can receive the Canada Learning Bond with no family contributions. You can use both: one account for spending and one plan for school.

When can my teen open a TFSA?

At 18 if they are a resident with a valid SIN, or at 19 where that is the age of majority. Room starts in the year they turn 18 and carries forward, so a teen who opens at 19 keeps the room from the year before. Our TFSA guide lists the current limit.
