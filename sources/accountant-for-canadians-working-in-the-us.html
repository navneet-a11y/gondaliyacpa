Canadians Working in the US — Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I pay tax in both countries if I work in the United States?
You file in both, usually. You should not pay twice on the same dollar, and the whole point of a properly prepared pair of returns is to make sure you do not. The United States can tax employment income earned there, which is why tax comes off your paycheque. Canada taxes your worldwide income as long as you are resident here, under ITA subsection 2(1), which includes those same American wages. Relief comes through the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126, computed by country and by income type against the American tax you actually ended up paying rather than the amount withheld during the year. Treaty Article XV allocates employment income between the two countries and can affect how much each may tax, driven by where the duties were performed. We state no day count, threshold or exemption outcome here because those are specific to your facts. The failure we see most is not double taxation by design — it is a credit claimed as a round figure that nobody could support.
Which American return do I file — Form 1040-NR or Form 1040?
It depends on whether you are treated as a resident of the United States for tax purposes, which turns on the substantial presence test, and a year in which your pattern changed can be a dual-status year with part of it on each basis. We name the test and the forms so you know what the question is. We do not tell you the answer, because it depends on a day count across more than one year and because that determination properly belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States who will sign the return. What we do is make sure the day-count evidence exists so the question can actually be answered rather than guessed, and we frame it clearly for whoever prepares that return. One practical point worth knowing: the answer is not always the one that produces the lowest American tax in isolation, because the interaction with your Canadian return and the foreign tax credit matters too, which is exactly why the two sides need to talk.
Why does the day count matter so much?
Because it is the fact everything else is built on, and almost nobody records it. Treaty Article XV allocates your employment income according to where the duties were performed. The substantial presence test on the American side counts days of presence. Both of those are questions about your calendar, and when a tax authority asks, you either have a contemporaneous record or you have an argument. A record kept as you go, supported by travel documents, border crossings, timesheets, rosters, calendars and assignment letters, is believed. A spreadsheet assembled two years later from memory and a bank statement is not worth the same thing, even when it happens to be accurate. This is why we set up the record in the first month and review it quarterly rather than asking you for it at year-end. It is unglamorous and it is the highest-value habit available to anyone in your position.
How do I claim credit for the American tax taken off my pay?
Through the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126, claimed on your Canadian return with the supporting computation behind it. Two details decide whether it actually works. First, the credit is based on the American tax you finally paid, not the amount withheld from your paycheque during the year — and those two figures are frequently different, often because too much was withheld. That means your American return has to be prepared before the Canadian credit can be computed properly, which is why the sequence matters. Second, the credit is computed by country and by income type rather than as one lump figure, so employment income, investment income and any self-employment are handled separately. A single round number claimed as a foreign tax credit is the most common error we inherit on these files, and it is almost always wrong in the client's own favour — meaning they paid more Canadian tax than they needed to.
Do I pay into both FICA and CPP?
That is exactly the question the Canada–United States totalization agreement exists to address, and the answer is specific to your arrangement rather than general. FICA is the American social security and medicare withholding; CPP is the Canadian contribution. Without coordination, a cross-border worker can find both being taken from the same earnings. The agreement coordinates which system a person contributes to, and certificates of coverage exist within it as the documentation of that position. We name the agreement and the certificate so you know the mechanism is there and worth asking about, usually through your employer's payroll function in the first instance. We state no rate, no wage base, no threshold and no outcome, because the determination depends on the facts of your employment and because getting that wrong in either direction has real consequences for your eventual benefits. What we will do is read your pay stubs and tell you what is actually being deducted, which is often the first time anyone has looked.
What about state tax? I had money withheld for a state too.
That is real, it is a separate system from American federal tax, and it is the part we are most emphatic about not answering. States have their own rules about residence, about sourcing income to work performed within their borders, and about who must file. A person can have their federal position in good order and still have a state return to file, or state tax over-withheld and sitting unrecovered. We will not tell you which state, what creates an obligation, what any rate or threshold is, or whether a return is required, because we are not licensed to answer it and a guess from a Canadian accountant is worth less than nothing when it is wrong. Raise it specifically with a preparer licensed in the United States, because a federal-only review will not surface it. What we do on our side is make sure every dollar of American tax you actually pay, state and federal, is identified and documented so it is available for the ITA section 126 credit rather than lost.
Am I still a Canadian resident if I work in the United States?
Probably, if your home and family remain here, but it is a question of fact rather than a matter of choice or paperwork. Canadian residency is determined on residential ties: a dwelling here, a spouse or common-law partner here, dependants here, with secondary ties also weighed, and CRA's view set out in Income Tax Folio S5-F1-C1. A daily commuter who sleeps in Ontario every night is in a very different position from someone who moved their household south and kept a cottage. Where the position is genuinely unclear, we can request a determination from CRA on Form NR73, which is worth doing when an assumption would otherwise sit underneath several years of filings. We do not assert your status on a web page. If you do cease to be resident, that is a different and significant event — ITA subsection 128.1(4) deems a disposition of most of your property on departure, and our page for green card holders in Canada works through those mechanics.
I work remotely from Canada for an American company. What applies?
This is the fastest-growing version of this question and the one most often handled wrongly, usually by the payroll department rather than by anyone advising you. The starting point is that your duties are being performed in Canada, not in the United States, and where the work is physically done is the fact that matters most. Despite that, American tax is frequently withheld from such salaries simply because the payer is American and the payroll system defaults that way. Where that has happened, the recovery route is filing the correct American return, not asking the employer to reverse it, and we coordinate that with a preparer licensed there. Two further points. Your American bank account will quietly accumulate toward the Form T1135 cost threshold, which catches people out. And whether the arrangement creates an obligation for your employer in Canada is a real question but it is theirs, not yours — we flag it and send it to their counsel.
How is my 401(k) or IRA treated in Canada?
It is a genuine question rather than a formality, and the answer depends on the plan and on what you do with it. Article XVIII of the Canada–United States Tax Convention deals with pensions, and we identify it as the governing provision without asserting what it produces for your plan. On the Canadian side, ITA paragraph 60(j) is the provision under which a transfer from a foreign plan into an RRSP may be available, and the conditions attached to it are specific enough that we will not characterise your situation from a web page. A Roth account raises its own separate question, including whether an election is available in respect of it, which is a conversation for your American preparer. The practical advice is simpler than the law: do not withdraw from one of these accounts before someone has looked at both sides, because a withdrawal handled badly can produce tax in both countries with usable credit in neither, and that is not recoverable afterwards.
Are my stock options and RSUs taxed in both countries?
Equity that vests over a period during which you worked in both countries raises a sourcing question on both sides, and it is one of the few areas where the two systems most reliably produce a mess if nobody looks early. The question is what portion of the benefit relates to services performed in each country, and both tax systems have their own approach to answering it. We will not give you a sourcing formula, a percentage or an outcome, because the analysis depends on the grant terms, the vesting pattern and your actual work location over the whole period, and because the American side of it belongs to a preparer licensed there. What we will do is flag it before the income is reported rather than after, make sure the grant documents and your day-count record are both on file, and make sure that whatever is finally reported on each return is consistent with the other. Raised early this is manageable; discovered on an assessment it rarely is.
I have an American bank account now. Do I have to report it?
Possibly, and this one catches cross-border workers more than almost anything else, because the account exists only to receive a salary and never feels like a foreign investment. On the Canadian side, Form T1135 is required where your specified foreign property costs more than $100,000, and the test is on cost amount rather than market value. A few years of salary accumulating in an American account, plus a brokerage account opened alongside it, reaches that point more quietly than people expect. The filing carries its own penalty regime whether or not any tax was owing. On the American side, FinCEN Form 114 and Form 8938 are the account and asset reporting forms; we name them so you know to raise them, and we state no threshold or penalty for either. We build the list from actual statements, file for each year required, and where years were missed we bring them forward on Form RC199.
I have been doing this for years and never filed properly. How bad is it?
Often much better than people fear, and sometimes it ends in a refund. The reason is specific to this situation: the usual problem on these files is not unreported income, because the American wages were generally reported on the Canadian return. It is that the foreign tax credit was never properly computed, so the client paid Canadian tax on income that already bore American tax. Recomputing the credit for the years still open frequently produces money back rather than a liability. Where there is genuine exposure — unfiled years, or missed Form T1135 filings — we reconstruct from American pay records, bank statements and Canadian slips and bring the Canadian side forward on Form RC199, which when accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief. Late filing otherwise costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month up to twelve months, so the oldest year goes first.
