Green Card Holders in Canada — Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do green card holders have to file US tax returns while living in Canada?
Yes. A lawful permanent resident is taxed by the United States as a resident on worldwide income and files a Form 1040 wherever they live, with relief for Canadian tax claimed on Form 1116 or, for earned income, Form 2555. Living in Canada does not end that by itself. Account and asset reporting is a separate obligation again, through FinCEN Form 114 and Form 8938. We state no filing threshold, exclusion amount or penalty figure for any of those forms on this page, because those numbers move and the current answer belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States. The practical trap on these files is not the American return at all. It is that Canada is simultaneously taxing you on worldwide income as a resident under ITA subsection 2(1), and that the two returns have to be prepared in a sequence that lets each claim the relief it is entitled to.
Does living in Canada affect my green card?
That is an immigration question, not a tax question, and we do not answer it. We are Ontario CPAs. What we will not do is the thing that makes this area dangerous, which is answer it anyway because you asked us and we were in the room. Put it to immigration counsel, get the answer in writing, and bring it back to us so the tax work is built on something real. What we will say is why it matters to the tax work. Several positions that look purely technical on a return — most obviously asserting treaty residence in Canada under Article IV and disclosing that on Form 8833 — are positions on your record about where you reside. Whether that has any consequence for your status is counsel's call. We flag the interaction, we refuse to guess at the consequence in either direction, and we do not take a treaty position for you until you have had that conversation.
Can I claim treaty residence in Canada, and should I?
The mechanism is real: Article IV of the Canada–United States Tax Convention decides residence for treaty purposes and contains a tie-breaker for exactly the situation you are in, where the United States treats you as resident because of your status and Canada treats you as resident because of your residential ties. A treaty-based position on an American return is disclosed on Form 8833. That is the mechanism, and we will frame it for your facts. Whether you should take it is a different question with two parts we separate deliberately. The tax part depends on your actual ties and income and we work it through with you and with a preparer licensed in the United States. The other part is the one nobody wants to say out loud: it is a formal assertion about where you reside, made by someone holding permanent residence. Immigration counsel should look at that before you file, not after. We assert no tie-breaker outcome and no immigration consequence.
What happens for tax if I give up my green card?
Surrendering status engages the expatriation rules, and that is a genuinely technical regime rather than a form you sign. The pieces to know the names of are these. Form 8854 is the expatriation statement. The long-term resident concept is what brings a green card holder within the regime at all. There are tests that determine whether someone is treated as a covered expatriate, and there are consequences that follow if they are. Form I-407 is the immigration instrument for abandoning the status itself. We name all of that so you know what to ask about and who to ask. We state no test threshold, no net-worth figure, no tax-liability figure, no year count and no outcome, because every one of those is specific, several move, and a wrong number here would be worse than silence. The determination belongs to a preparer or counsel licensed in the United States. Our job is the Canadian side and the records the analysis will need.
Should I keep the green card or surrender it?
We will not tell you, and you should be wary of anyone who answers that quickly. It is not a tax decision alone. It involves where you expect to live, family, work, how you feel about returning, and immigration consequences that are not ours to assess, alongside a tax regime whose thresholds we deliberately do not quote. What we can do is make the comparison real instead of anecdotal. We cost the Canadian side of each branch: what your position looks like continuing as you are, and what it looks like if the status goes and, separately, if you later leave Canada and trigger the ITA subsection 128.1(4) deemed disposition here. We assemble the asset and cost-base records both analyses depend on, while the statements still exist. Then you take a documented comparison to a US-licensed preparer and to immigration counsel and make the decision with real figures in front of you, which is more than most people in your position ever get.
What is departure tax, and does it apply to me if I leave Canada?
If you cease to be resident in Canada, ITA subsection 128.1(4) deems you to have disposed of most of your property at fair market value on the date of departure, so unrealised gains are taxed even though you have sold nothing. On a portfolio built over years that can be a substantial bill in a year when you are spending heavily on the move. Certain property is excluded, including Canadian real property, registered plans and some pension interests, and we identify what is actually caught rather than applying the rule to everything you own. Form T1161 lists the properties you held on emigration where the total exceeds $25,000, and Form T1243 reports the deemed disposition. The provision that makes this manageable is ITA subsection 220(4.5), which lets you elect on Form T1244 to defer payment with acceptable security instead of paying in the year you leave. Planning the departure date and the disposition sequence is worth real money here.
What is Form T1135 and why do people in my position keep missing it?
Form T1135 is a Canadian filing, required where your specified foreign property costs more than $100,000, and permanent residents living here miss it as reliably as any group we see. The reason is not complexity. It is that the property in question is usually an American bank account, a brokerage account, a retirement account or a house you kept, and none of that feels foreign to its owner. To CRA it is foreign property, the test is on cost amount rather than market value, and the filing carries its own penalty regime whether or not any tax was owing in the year. Where you hold an interest in a foreign affiliate, Form T1134 can apply as well, and an ordinary-looking American holding company or LLC interest sometimes pulls people into that without their knowing. We build the list from actual statements, file for each year required, and bring missed years forward on Form RC199.
Am I resident in Canada for tax purposes? I thought my status settled that.
It does not, and this is the misunderstanding that causes the most trouble on these files. Canadian residency is a question of fact determined on your residential ties, not on what immigration documents you hold. The primary ties are a dwelling here, a spouse or common-law partner here, and dependants here, with secondary ties also considered, and CRA's view is set out in Income Tax Folio S5-F1-C1. A person can hold a green card and be a factual resident of Canada at the same time without the slightest contradiction, which is precisely why the Article IV tie-breaker exists. We do not assert your residency status on a web page. We assemble the analysis for your facts, and where the position is genuinely unclear we can request a determination from CRA on Form NR74 if you are entering Canada or Form NR73 if you are leaving, which is worth doing when an assumption would otherwise sit under years of filings.
How do I avoid being taxed twice?
Through credits claimed properly on both sides, and through preparing the two returns in a defined sequence. On the Canadian side you are taxed on worldwide income as a resident under ITA subsection 2(1), and relief for American tax comes through the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126, computed by country and by income type rather than as one estimated figure. On the American side relief comes through Form 1116 or Form 2555. The common failure is not a rule problem: it is two preparers who never exchange a number, each claiming relief for tax paid in the other country on a basis the other return does not support. Both returns look fine on their own and the household pays more than it owed. We prepare the Canadian side, hand your American preparer the figures in the form they need with the income characterised and the tax identified, and review each return's relief against the other before anything is filed.

I am leaving Canada but keeping my condo here and renting it out. What changes?
Quite a lot, and it is mostly mechanical once it is set up properly. As a non-resident you are taxed in Canada only on Canadian-source income under ITA subsection 2(3), and rent from Canadian real property is squarely within that. The default is Part XIII withholding under ITA section 212 on the gross rent, remitted by your agent, with NR4 reporting. In most cases we elect under ITA section 216 instead, which lets you file a Canadian return on the net rental income and claim the interest, property tax, maintenance and capital cost allowance the return allows, which is usually a materially better result than withholding on gross rent. Keep the capital cost allowance position consistent across years rather than claiming it opportunistically. And when you eventually sell, the disposition engages the ITA section 116 clearance certificate process, which stalls a closing badly if nobody started it in time.
I have not filed in one country or the other for years. Where do I start?
With the Canadian side, because that is the part we can actually fix, and with the understanding that there are two cleanups handled by different people. We reconstruct and file your unfiled T1 years and any missed Form T1135 filings from investment statements, employment records and bank history, and bring you forward through a Voluntary Disclosures Program application on Form RC199, which when accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief. Late filing otherwise costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month up to twelve months, so the oldest year goes first. On the American side, routes back in exist — the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures and the delinquent FBAR and international information return submission procedures. We name them so you know they exist, assert nothing about eligibility or outcome, and coordinate timing with your American preparer, because the two disclosures interact.
