Revenue Earned Across the Season

  • Fees received before a session runs are a prepayment rather than revenue, so the deposits and full payments arriving from January to May are a liability until the week each camper actually attends.
  • Which produces a deferred revenue balance that peaks just before the season and empties across July and August, and it has to be computable from the registration records rather than estimated.
  • A camp that books registrations as revenue on receipt reports a spectacular spring and a loss-making autumn every single year, and the first person misled by that is the owner trying to plan from it.
  • Your cancellation terms are a liability measured against that balance, so we carry the expected refunds rather than treating each withdrawal as a surprise reduction in a month that has already closed.
  • And we report revenue and direct cost by session rather than for the summer as a whole, because the sessions almost never earn alike and most camps cannot say which weeks pay for which.
3

Child Care Expense Receipts for Parents

  • Here is an obligation that is not about your tax at all. Camp fees paid for the care of an eligible child can be child care expenses to the parent under ITA section 63, and your receipt is what the claim rests on.
  • So the receipt has to carry the camp's name, the child's name, the amount paid for care and the period it covers — and where the camp is run by an individual rather than a corporation, that individual's social insurance number.
  • Which catches sole proprietors out every February, because a receipt issued without the SIN is not usable as issued and the parent who cannot claim will be back asking you to reissue it.
  • We also split the care portion from anything that is not care. Merchandise, a tuck shop float, optional trips and equipment hire are not child care expenses, and a single bundled figure invites the question.
  • And we set the process up once so it runs from the registration records each February rather than being assembled by hand, which is how the wrong child, the wrong amount and the wrong year get certified.
4

Day Camp or Overnight Camp

  • These are two different things in a parent's hands, and a camp that runs both and issues one receipt has handed every family a problem it cannot see from its own side of the transaction.
  • Day camp fees are ordinary child care expenses, subject to the annual limit that applies to the child. Amounts for an overnight camp are subject to a separate periodic limit for each week of attendance.
  • We state no amount for either on this page, because both move and a figure taken from a website is how a parent ends up reassessed on a claim your receipt invited them to make.
  • What we do instead is make your records distinguish day attendance from overnight attendance at the point of registration, so the receipt can report the two separately without anyone reconstructing a season.
  • The same split matters to you rather than only to your families, because a residential session and a day session have different staffing, different food cost and different capital behind them.
5

Seasonal Staff and Payroll

  • A camp hires most of its workforce for ten weeks, much of it under twenty years old, and the payroll setup that suits a year-round employer gets this wrong in both directions.
  • Start with the one almost nobody raises: pensionable employment begins the month after an employee turns 18, so no CPP is withheld or matched for a counsellor who is still sixteen or seventeen.
  • EI has no equivalent age exemption, so those same under-18 staff are generally insurable while not yet pensionable, which is a distinction a single payroll switch set once in May will not make for you.
  • We set the remittance schedule for a compressed season rather than a flat year, file the T4 slips by the end of February, and deal with the WSIB position for staff working on a site with water and equipment.
  • And we work through who is genuinely an employee. Counsellors in training on a stipend, a visiting specialist paid an honorarium and a parent volunteer are three different answers, decided on the arrangement rather than the label.
6

Board and Lodging for Resident Staff

  • A residential camp houses and feeds its own staff, and board and lodging provided to an employee is generally a taxable benefit. That is the starting position, and most camps have never tested it.
  • There is relief that may apply. ITA paragraph 6(6)(a) excludes the value of board and lodging at a special work site or a remote work location, for a period of not less than 36 hours, on conditions.
  • The conditions are where it turns. A special work site requires that the duties kept the employee away from a principal place of residence they maintained elsewhere as a self-contained domestic establishment.
  • Which is a real question for a seventeen-year-old counsellor whose home is their parents' house, and a different question again for a year-round site manager or a director living on the property.
  • We will not tell you on a web page that your camp is a special work site. We test it on your facts, handle the Form TD4 declaration where it is right, and value the benefit properly where it is not.
7

The Site: Capital Property That Sits Idle

  • A camp owns more capital per dollar of revenue than almost any business we see, and it uses that capital for eight weeks a year. Cabins, a dining hall, docks, canoes, a tractor and a bus are capital property.
  • So their cost is recovered through capital cost allowance over time rather than deducted when bought, and different property sits in different classes depending on what it actually is.
  • Where you own the site, the purchase price has to be split between land and buildings, because one is depreciable and the other is not, and a split nobody documented is a problem on the eventual sale.
  • Where you lease it, the cabins and improvements you put up are leasehold improvements recovered against the lease rather than against how long a building made of timber will actually stand.
  • We state no capital cost allowance rate on this page, because the class an asset belongs in turns on what the asset is, and we maintain the register that makes the claim defensible rather than estimated.
8

Off-Season Costs: Repair or Improvement

  • Ten months of the year a camp spends money on a site nobody is using, and almost all of it arrives as one question: is this a repair you deduct now or an improvement you capitalise?
  • ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) bars the deduction of a capital outlay, so work that betters the property or creates something of enduring value is capital however urgently it needed doing.
  • Reshingling a cabin roof to the standard it was is generally a current repair. Replacing that cabin, winterising it, or adding a washroom block is not, and the invoice heading will not tell you which.
  • We make the call job by job and record the reasoning, because a camp that expenses an entire rebuild and a camp that capitalises every repair are both wrong, in opposite directions, by a lot.
  • And we time the work deliberately where you have the choice, because when an asset becomes available for use affects the year the claim starts and a project finished in September behaves differently from one finished in June.
9

GST/HST Registration and Characterisation

  • The camp exemption question is narrower than most operators hope, and it turns on who you are rather than on what you sell, which is unusual and worth understanding before you rely on it.
  • Schedule V, Part VI, section 12 exempts a recreational programme provided primarily to children 14 and under, but only where the supplier is a public sector body, and on conditions.
  • So a camp carried on as a for-profit business generally cannot reach it, while a municipal, non-profit or charitable camp may. We settle which you are on your facts rather than assuming either answer.
  • Where you are taxable, registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, with Ontario at 13%.
  • We run the Quick Method against the regular method on your figures against its $400,000 cap, and we apportion credits where tuck shop sales, merchandise and off-season facility rental sit beside exempt programming.
10

Cash Flow Across an Eleven-Month Gap

  • This is the problem that actually ends camps, and it is not a tax problem until it is. One earning window funds twelve months of site, insurance, debt service and off-season wages.
  • Which means the registration money arriving in February is doing two jobs at once in the owner's mind: it is next summer's unearned revenue and it is this winter's cash, and only one of those is true.
  • So we build the year as a cash plan against the deferred revenue balance, so you can see how much of the balance is genuinely yours to spend and how much is owed back if a session does not run.
  • We price on cost per camper per week rather than on what the camp down the road charges, because a fee that looked fine at ninety per cent occupancy stops working at seventy and nobody notices until October.
  • And we model the off-season uses properly where you want them — March break programming, facility rental, retreats and weddings — because each one changes your tax, your credits and your capital position.
11

Incorporation and Structure

  • Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates, and limited liability around children, water and equipment.
  • For a camp the retained-earnings point does real work, because the off-season is funded out of after-tax profit and the rate that profit bears decides whether the site gets maintained or deferred again.
  • There is a wrinkle specific to you: incorporating changes your child care receipts, because a corporation does not put a social insurance number on them while a sole proprietor must.
  • We model salary against dividends for the owners, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions, and on whether the income record will support borrowing against a site purchase.
  • Where the land is owned we look at whether it belongs in the operating company at all, and we publish no capital gains inclusion rate and no lifetime capital gains exemption amount, because selling a camp usually means selling land.

Haliburton Camp — Counsellor Accommodation Nobody Valued

The problem: A Haliburton residential camp housed and fed forty seasonal staff on site and had never recorded a taxable benefit for any of them. It also ran CPP on every employee regardless of age, including a large cohort of sixteen and seventeen year olds, so it had over-remitted employer contributions across several seasons while carrying an unquantified benefit exposure.

What we did: We tested the ITA paragraph 6(6)(a) conditions against each category of staff rather than as a group, handled the Form TD4 declarations where the special work site test was met, and valued the benefit where it was not. We corrected the payroll so CPP begins the month after an employee turns 18 while EI continues to apply, and pursued recovery of the over-remitted contributions.

The result:

  • Lodging position tested per staff category, not as one group
  • CPP corrected to start after age 18, EI left in place
  • Over-remitted employer contributions pursued for recovery

Eastern Ontario Day Camp — A Spectacular Spring, Every Year

The problem: An eastern Ontario day camp booked registrations as revenue when the payment arrived, so its statements showed enormous February-to-May income and losses for the rest of the year. Summer Camp Accounting & Tax FAQs

Are camp fees a child care expense for parents?
Often yes, and that makes your receipt a document somebody else's tax return depends on. Fees paid for the care of an eligible child can be child care expenses to the supporting person under ITA section 63, claimed generally by the lower-income supporting person and subject to annual limits that vary with the child's age and eligibility. You are not the claimant; you are the issuer of the receipt the claim rests on. That is worth understanding properly, because a receipt that cannot be used is a receipt you will be asked to reissue, usually in April, usually by several families at once. We deliberately state no limit, rate or weekly amount anywhere on this page — those figures move, and a number taken from a web page is how a parent ends up reassessed on a claim your paperwork invited them to make.
What has to go on a camp child care receipt?
Enough for the parent to support the claim: the name of the camp, the child's name, the amount paid for care, and the period the payment covers. Where the camp is operated by an individual rather than through a corporation, the receipt must also show that individual's social insurance number. The part most camps get wrong is the amount. What goes on the receipt is the amount paid for care — not merchandise, not a tuck shop float, not optional trips, not equipment hire, and not a cancelled session that was refunded. A single bundled figure covering all of it is the kind of thing that invites a question for the family rather than for you, which is a worse outcome than being asked yourself. We split the care portion at the point of registration so the receipt is right when it is generated.
Do I really have to put my SIN on the receipts?
If you operate as an individual rather than through a corporation, yes — a child care receipt from an individual provider is expected to show that provider's social insurance number, and a receipt issued without it is not usable as issued. Camps run as sole proprietorships discover this every February, typically from a parent whose claim was questioned. It is one of the practical arguments for incorporating that nobody raises in the usual incorporation conversation: a corporation issues receipts under its business number and the owner's SIN stays off several hundred pieces of paper circulating among families. That is not on its own a reason to incorporate, and we will say so — the small business deduction, the retained earnings that fund your off-season and the liability position matter far more. But it belongs in the decision, and we raise it.
Day camp and overnight camp — does the difference matter?
Very much, and it is the single most expensive thing most camps get wrong. In a parent's hands, day camp fees are ordinary child care expenses subject to the annual limit for that child. Amounts for an overnight camp are subject to a separate periodic limit for each week of attendance. So a camp running both and issuing one undifferentiated receipt has handed every family a figure they cannot correctly allocate, and the camp will never see the consequence because it lands on the parents' returns. We state neither amount here, for the reason given above. What we do instead is make your records distinguish day from overnight attendance at the point of registration, so the receipt reports the two separately without anyone reconstructing a season from a spreadsheet in February. The split also tells you something useful: residential and day sessions have quite different staffing, food and capital behind them.
When do I recognise registration money as revenue?
When the session runs, not when the payment arrives. Fees received before delivery are a prepayment, so the deposits and full payments landing between January and May are a liability until the week each camper actually attends. The unearned balance sits as deferred revenue in between, peaking just before the season and emptying across July and August. This is not bookkeeping neatness. A camp that books registrations on receipt shows enormous spring income and losses through the autumn every single year, and the person most misled by that pattern is the owner drawing against it and computing instalments from it. Your cancellation terms are a liability measured against the same balance, so we carry the expected refunds rather than treating each withdrawal as a surprise. And we report revenue and direct cost by session, because sessions rarely earn alike.
Do I deduct CPP from a sixteen-year-old counsellor?
No. Pensionable employment begins the month after the employee turns 18, so no CPP is withheld from a sixteen or seventeen year old and no employer contribution is matched for them either. For a camp whose counsellor roster is largely under 18 that is a real amount of money, and we have corrected payroll setups that had been over-remitting employer contributions for several seasons without anyone noticing. EI has no equivalent age exemption. Insurable employment does not turn on age, so those same under-18 staff are generally EI-insurable while not yet CPP-pensionable — a distinction a single payroll switch flipped once in May will not make for you. Watch the transition too: a counsellor who turns 18 mid-season becomes pensionable the following month, not on their birthday, and not at the start of the summer.
Is staff accommodation and food a taxable benefit?
Start from yes, then test whether relief applies. Board and lodging provided to an employee is generally a taxable benefit, which puts every residential camp in scope by default. The relief that may help is ITA paragraph 6(6)(a), which excludes the value of board and lodging at a special work site — where the duties required the employee to be away from their principal place of residence — or at a remote work location, for a period of not less than 36 hours, subject to conditions. The crucial condition is that the employee maintained a self-contained domestic establishment elsewhere as their principal place of residence, which is a genuine question for a seventeen-year-old whose home is their parents' house, and a different question again for a year-round site manager living on the property. We test it per category of staff, handle the Form TD4 where it is met, and value the benefit where it is not.
Do I charge HST on camp fees?
Probably, if you are a for-profit camp — and that surprises operators who have heard there is an exemption for children's programmes. There is, but it is narrower than it sounds and it turns on who the supplier is rather than on what is supplied. Schedule V, Part VI, section 12 exempts a supply of a recreational programme provided primarily to children 14 and under, but only where the supplier is a public sector body, and on conditions. So a municipal, non-profit or charitable camp may be able to reach it while a camp carried on as an ordinary business generally cannot. We settle which you are on your facts rather than assuming either answer, because the consequence runs both ways: charging tax you should not have charged is one problem and failing to charge tax you should have is a larger one. Where you are taxable, registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, with Ontario at 13%.
Can I claim input tax credits on camp purchases?
To the extent your supplies are taxable, yes — and if your programming is exempt, that is precisely where it hurts. Input tax credits are available only to the extent an input is acquired for use in a commercial activity, and making exempt supplies is not a commercial activity for this purpose. So a camp whose programming is exempt pays the HST on the cabins, the food, the equipment and the fuel without recovering it, and that is permanent cost rather than a timing difference. Most camps are mixed suppliers even so: tuck shop sales, branded merchandise, equipment rental and off-season facility rental for retreats or weddings generally sit on the taxable side and carry credits with them. The credits on shared inputs then have to be apportioned on a basis that is genuinely reasonable on your facts, documented when adopted, and applied the same way every period. A round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a sales tax auditor takes apart.
How do I account for cabins, docks and canoes?
As capital property, recovered over time. A camp owns more capital per dollar of revenue than almost any business we see and then uses it for eight weeks a year, which makes getting this right unusually consequential. Cabins, the dining hall, docks and waterfront structures, canoes and programme equipment, vehicles and a tractor are all capital property, so their cost is recovered through capital cost allowance rather than deducted when bought, with different property falling in different classes depending on what it actually is. Two points specific to a camp. Where you own the site, the purchase price must be split between land and buildings — one is depreciable and one is not — and a split nobody documented becomes a problem on the eventual sale. Where you lease it, what you build is a leasehold improvement recovered against the lease term rather than the building's physical life. We name no rate here; the class turns on the asset.
Off-season work: repair or improvement?
It depends on the job, and it is the question almost every off-season invoice raises. ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) bars the deduction of a capital outlay, so work that betters the property beyond its former condition or creates something of enduring value is capital however urgently it needed doing. Reshingling a cabin roof back to the standard it was is generally a current repair. Replacing that cabin, winterising it so it can be used in February, or adding a washroom block is not. The invoice heading will not tell you which, and a contractor's single spring bill routinely contains both. We make the call job by job and record the reasoning, because a camp that expenses an entire rebuild and a camp that capitalises every repair are both wrong in opposite directions. Where you have the choice we also time the work deliberately, since when an asset becomes available for use affects the year the claim starts.
What fiscal year end should a camp have?
Almost certainly not December 31, and this is one of the easiest wins available to a camp. A year end shortly after the season closes on a complete cycle: one full set of sessions delivered, the deferred revenue balance near its floor, the camper records fresh, and the compliance work landing in your quietest months rather than in the middle of the registration rush. A December or March year end does the opposite — it cuts straight through a registration cycle, leaving part-paid sessions to apportion and a large unearned balance to measure at exactly the point you can least afford the time. For an incorporated camp the choice is genuinely yours within the rules, so it is worth making once, deliberately, rather than inheriting whatever the incorporation paperwork defaulted to. We also use it as a planning lever on capital projects, since the timing of when an asset is available for use interacts with it.
