Accounting & Tax Services Tailored for Dormant Corporations

Real, practitioner-level CPA expertise for owners of inactive corporations: every overdue year filed, the accounts closed rather than abandoned, the registry position checked, and the keep-or-dissolve decision made once.

1

The Nil Return, Every Year

  • ITA subsection 150(1) requires every corporation resident in Canada to file a return of income for each tax year, within six months of the end of that year, whether or not it carried on a business.
  • There is no exemption for inactivity, and no threshold below which the obligation stops. A corporation with a bank account holding two hundred dollars has the same filing duty as one with staff.
  • A nil year is still a return with schedules: the GIFI information has to be there, the balance sheet still has to balance, and the year end has to match what the registry and the CRA both hold.
  • We also set the filing cycle properly so the next year does not drift. Most dormant files became dormant files because one missed deadline turned into a habit nobody revisited.
  • And we keep the six years of records ITA section 230 requires, including the two years after a dissolution during which the records of a dissolved corporation still have to be retained.
2

Catching Up the Unfiled Years

  • This is the work most dormant files actually need: a run of years, often four to nine, filed in order so that the balances carry forward correctly and the loss pool is established where there is one.
  • We start by confirming what the CRA actually holds on the account, because an owner's memory of which years were filed and the account's own record of it are frequently two different documents.
  • Then we work out the real first dormant year from when the business actually stopped, since the final trading year usually has figures in it and is the one most likely to be wrong.
  • Where a year has been assessed arbitrarily because nothing was filed, the filed return replaces that assessment, which is normally the fastest way to remove a figure the owner does not recognise.
  • We file them all rather than the recent ones only, because a gap in the middle of a run is what generates the next letter, and the cost of the whole set is far closer to the cost of one than owners expect.
3

Penalties, Demands and the Arithmetic Nobody Expects

  • The reason dormant years pile up is a real feature of the rules. The late filing penalty under ITA subsection 162(1) is 5% of the unpaid tax plus 1% per month, and on a nil balance it is nil.
  • So owners conclude, not unreasonably, that there is no consequence. But ITA subsection 162(7) imposes, where no other penalty applies, the greater of $100 and $25 for each day of default, to a maximum of 100 days.
  • A demand to file served under subsection 150(2) changes the position again, and it is usually the point at which an owner discovers the file exists, several years after it stopped being simple.
  • We do not state what any of that would be in your case. We name the provisions, file the returns, and work the numbers out on the actual account rather than on a web page.
  • Where relief is available we ask for it on Form RC4288 on the facts, which is a different and better conversation than asking for it after a collections letter has already arrived.
4

Voluntary Disclosure Before Contact

  • There is an important difference between a delay and an exposure. Years of nil returns are a delay. Unreported rent from the unit the shell still owns is an exposure, and it is treated differently.
  • The Voluntary Disclosures Program on Form RC199 is available before the CRA contacts you, and the whole value of it is in that sequence: disclose first, then file, rather than respond to a letter.
  • So we ask the uncomfortable question early. Was there any income in these years at all? Rent, interest, a single consulting invoice, a gain on a property sale nobody mentioned?
  • Where the answer is yes, we deal with it through the right channel and request relief from penalties and interest on Form RC4288 where the facts support it.
  • Where the answer is genuinely no, we say so plainly and simply file the outstanding years, because a run of nil returns does not need a disclosure programme designed for something else entirely.
5

The Ontario Annual Return and Default

  • This one has caught more owners than any other change in recent years. The Ontario Annual Return used to travel with the corporate tax return. Since 2021 it does not.
  • It is filed through the Ontario Business Registry as a separate filing, which means a corporation whose accountant files only the T2 can be in default of its registry obligations without anybody noticing.
  • The consequence is not cosmetic. Under the Ontario Business Corporations Act a corporation in default of its filing obligations may be dissolved by the Director for that default.
  • We check your registry position as part of the file, because an owner who finds out their company was dissolved three years ago usually finds out from a bank, a buyer or a lawyer rather than from us.
  • A dissolved Ontario corporation may apply to be revived, and property not distributed may have forfeited to the Crown in the meantime, so the sooner that is established the better.
6

Closing the GST/HST Account Properly

  • A GST/HST registrant must file a return for every reporting period, including periods with no activity, until the registration is cancelled. Stopping filing is not the same as closing the account.
  • That is why a dormant corporation can accumulate a run of missing returns on an account its owner regarded as finished years earlier, and why the first letter is often about HST rather than income tax.
  • A person who has ceased commercial activity generally must request cancellation of the registration rather than simply stop filing, so we file whatever is outstanding and then close the account deliberately.
  • Cancellation also has consequences where the corporation still holds property, so we work that through before the account is closed rather than discovering it in the period afterwards.
  • Then the payroll account, on the same principle: final T4 slips for any year remuneration was paid, remittances reconciled, and the account closed rather than left open indefinitely.
7

Director Liability While the Shell Lives

  • This is the part of a dormant file that is genuinely personal rather than corporate, and it is the reason we ask about outstanding remittances before anything else on the engagement.
  • ITA section 227.1 makes directors jointly and severally liable for amounts the corporation failed to deduct, withhold or remit, and ETA section 323 does the same for net tax under the sales tax rules.
  • In each case an assessment of a director cannot be made more than two years after the person last ceased to be a director, which is the only real limitation in the picture.
  • Which means keeping a dormant corporation alive, with yourself in office, is also keeping that two-year clock from starting. Owners are rarely told this and it is often decisive.
  • We identify any unremitted source deduction or net tax balance in the shell first, because that is the exposure that does not go away simply because the company stopped trading.
8

The Loss Pool and What Can Kill It

  • Sometimes the shell is worth keeping, and the reason is the losses. Non-capital losses carry forward twenty years and net capital losses carry forward indefinitely, which is a real asset.
  • We establish the pool from the filed returns rather than from an estimate, which is the first argument for filing the overdue years even where nothing was owing in any of them.
  • Then the risk: where control of the corporation is acquired, ITA subsection 249(4) deems a tax year to end, and subsection 111(5) restricts the non-capital losses to income from the same or a similar business.
  • So a plan that involves bringing somebody else into the shell, or selling it, needs that restriction modelled first, because the losses are exactly what the buyer thinks they are paying nothing for.
  • A dormant corporation can also still be associated with your active one, which means sharing the $500,000 business limit, and that is a live cost of keeping it rather than closing it.
9

What the Shell Still Holds

  • A corporation that holds something is not dormant in the way its owner usually means. A rental unit, a portfolio, a vehicle, even a bank account earning interest: each produces something to report.
  • So we inventory it properly: the bank balance, any property and its cost base, any shareholder loan, any intercompany balance, and anything that was transferred out without being recorded.
  • Shareholder loan balances are the most common complication, because they have to be dealt with before a dissolution rather than discovered during one, and they have their own rules.
  • Intercompany balances inside a group of companies are the second most common complication, and cleaning up three inactive subsidiaries usually means unwinding those balances first, in the right order.
  • Where there is property still held we flag the GST/HST consequence of cancelling the registration before anybody cancels it, because that sequence is an easy one to get the wrong way round.
10

Dissolve or Keep It Dormant

  • This is the decision the whole file exists to support, and we will not make it for you on a web page because it turns on things we need to see.
  • Keeping it costs the annual filings, the registry return, the risk of default, the association effect on your active company's business limit, and the directors' clock that does not start.
  • Closing it costs the final-year work, dealing with whatever the corporation still holds, and giving up the loss pool and the name, which may or may not matter to you.
  • So we price both sides on your actual numbers and tell you which we would do, which is a different thing from telling you what the rules are and leaving you to choose.
  • If dissolution is the answer, the articles and the registry filings are legal work and we coordinate with your counsel; we prepare and file the final returns and the record retention that follows.
11

Reviving and Restarting

  • Sometimes the point is the opposite of closing: the owner wants the company back. A dissolved Ontario corporation may apply to be revived, and a dormant one can simply be restarted.
  • A revival brings the missing years with it, so the filings that were not done while the company was dissolved still have to be dealt with, and we deal with them as part of the same exercise.
  • Restarting a dormant corporation is a good moment to fix the things that were never right: the year end, the share structure, the accounts that are open and the ones that are not.
  • We check whether the losses sitting in the shell will actually be available against the new activity, because the same or similar business test is not a formality where the plan is a different business.
  • And we re-open the programme accounts the new activity needs rather than assuming the old ones still work, which is the most common administrative snag on a restart.
12

CRA Representation on an Old File

  • When a demand to file or an arbitrary assessment arrives for a company nobody has thought about in years, we answer it with the filed returns rather than with correspondence about intentions.
  • When a director is assessed personally under ITA section 227.1 or ETA section 323, we deal with the amounts and the dates, including when the person actually ceased to hold office.
  • When an estate is dealing with a corporation the deceased had not touched, we reconstruct the position from the records that exist and file what is required before the estate can be closed.
  • When the loss pool is examined on a restart or a sale, we produce the returns that established it and the analysis of the same or similar business test done at the time.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment, and request relief on Form RC4288 where the history supports it, which on a dormant file it frequently does.

Dormant Corporation Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I have to file taxes for an inactive corporation?
Yes. ITA subsection 150(1) requires every corporation resident in Canada to file a return of income for each tax year, within six months of the end of that year, whether or not it carried on a business and whether or not any tax is payable. There is no exemption for inactivity and no threshold below which the obligation stops: a corporation whose only asset is a bank account with a small balance has the same filing duty as one with staff and premises. A nil year is still a real return, with the GIFI information and a balance sheet that balances. The practical reason this matters beyond compliance is that the return is what establishes your loss pool and keeps the corporation's position on record, so the years you file are the years you can later rely on.
What happens if I never filed my dormant years?
It is common and it is fixable, and the first step is finding out what the CRA actually holds rather than relying on memory, because the two rarely match. We then establish the real first dormant year from the date the business stopped, since the final trading year usually has figures in it and is the one most likely to be wrong. Where a year was assessed arbitrarily because nothing was filed, the filed return normally replaces that assessment, which is the fastest route to removing a number you do not recognise. We file the whole run rather than the recent years only: a gap in the middle is what generates the next letter, and the cost of the full set is much closer to the cost of one year than owners expect.
Is there a penalty on a nil return?
This is the arithmetic that creates the problem in the first place. The familiar late filing penalty under ITA subsection 162(1) is 5% of the unpaid tax at the due date plus 1% of that amount for each complete month the return is late, to a maximum of twelve months — and on a nil balance, five per cent of nothing is nothing. So owners conclude there is no consequence to not filing, which is understandable and incomplete. ITA subsection 162(7) imposes, where no other penalty is specified, a penalty equal to the greater of $100 and $25 for each day of default, to a maximum of 100 days. We do not state what that would be in your case; we name the provision and work the numbers out on the actual account.
What is a demand to file?
It is a notice served under ITA subsection 150(2) requiring a return to be filed, and in practice it is the point at which many owners discover that the dormant file exists at all. It changes the position: a corporation that has simply not filed is in a different place from one that has been asked in writing and still not filed. It is also frequently accompanied, or followed, by an assessment made without a return, which puts a figure on the account that bears no relation to the corporation's actual position. The right response is to file, not to correspond about intentions. We deal with the demand and the outstanding years together, which is both faster and cheaper than handling them in sequence.
Do I still need to file the Ontario Annual Return?
Yes, and this is the change that has caught more owners than any other in recent years. The Ontario Annual Return used to travel with the corporate tax return. Since 2021 it does not: it is filed separately through the Ontario Business Registry. Which means a corporation whose accountant files only the T2 can fall into default of its registry obligations with nobody noticing, including the accountant. The consequence is not cosmetic. Under the Ontario Business Corporations Act a corporation in default of its filing obligations may be dissolved by the Director for that default. We check the registry position as part of every dormant file, because owners usually learn about a dissolution from a bank or a lawyer rather than from their accountant.
Can my corporation really be dissolved for not filing?
Yes. Under the Ontario Business Corporations Act a corporation that is in default of its obligations may be dissolved by the Director for that default, and it happens without the owner being meaningfully involved. The discovery usually comes at the worst moment: a refinancing, a sale, or an estate, when a search shows the registered owner of a property or a bank account no longer exists as a corporation. A dissolved Ontario corporation may apply to be revived. Property that had not been distributed may have forfeited to the Crown in the meantime, so the sooner the position is established the better. The articles and registry filings for a revival are legal work; we prepare the tax returns for the intervening years and coordinate with your counsel.
Do I have to file nil HST returns, and how do I close the account?
A GST/HST registrant must file a return for every reporting period, including periods with no activity, until the registration is cancelled. Stopping filing is not the same as closing the account, which is why a dormant corporation can accumulate a run of missing sales tax returns on an account its owner regarded as finished years ago — and why the first letter is often about HST rather than income tax. A person who has ceased commercial activity generally must request cancellation rather than simply stop. So we file what is outstanding and then close the registration deliberately. One caution: cancellation has consequences where the corporation still holds property, so that is worked through before the account is closed rather than discovered in the period afterwards.
Am I still personally liable as a director?
For some amounts, yes, and this is the part of a dormant file that is genuinely personal. ITA section 227.1 makes directors jointly and severally liable for amounts the corporation failed to deduct, withhold or remit, and ETA section 323 does the same for net tax under the sales tax rules. In each case an assessment of a director cannot be made more than two years after the person last ceased to be a director. That limitation is the only real clock in the picture, and it has an implication owners are rarely told: keeping a dormant corporation alive with yourself in office keeps that two-year period from ever starting. We identify any unremitted source deduction or net tax balance in the shell first, because that is the exposure that does not disappear simply because the company stopped trading.
Can I keep my losses in a dormant company?
Often yes, and it is one of the better reasons to keep a shell alive. Non-capital losses carry forward twenty years and net capital losses carry forward indefinitely. The catch is that the pool has to be established, which means the overdue returns have to be filed even though nothing was owing in any of them — a loss that exists only in a spreadsheet is not a loss you can use. The risk is a change of control: where control of the corporation is acquired, ITA subsection 249(4) deems a tax year to end and subsection 111(5) restricts the non-capital losses to income from the same or a similar business carried on for profit. So any plan that brings somebody else into the shell needs that restriction modelled before, not after.
Does a dormant company affect my active company?
It can, and this is a real annual cost that owners rarely count. A dormant corporation can still be associated with your active one, and associated corporations must share the $500,000 business limit for the small business deduction. The group's taxable capital also affects that limit. So a company doing nothing at all can be taking up part of the room your trading company needs, which is one of the stronger arguments for closing a shell rather than leaving it. Whether two corporations are associated turns on the actual share ownership and control rather than on how the owner thinks of them, so we look at the structure rather than assume. It is also one of the things a buyer's advisers check when they look at a group.
Should I dissolve it or keep it dormant?
We will not answer that on a web page, because it turns on four things we need to see. Keeping it costs the annual corporate filing, the registry return, the risk of default, the effect of association on your active company's business limit, and the directors' two-year clock that does not start while you remain in office. Closing it costs the final-year work, dealing with whatever the corporation still holds, and giving up the loss pool and the name. For some owners the losses or the entity itself are worth real money; for others the shell is pure cost and has been for years. We price both sides on your actual numbers and tell you which we would do, which is a different thing from reciting the rules and leaving you to decide. The dissolution filings themselves are legal work, done with your counsel.
How long do I keep records after dissolving?
Records must generally be kept six years under ITA section 230, and the records of a corporation that has been dissolved must be retained for two years after the date of the dissolution. That second rule surprises people, because dissolution feels like the end of the obligation rather than the start of a new retention period. In practice it means the minute book, the filed returns, the final financial statements and the supporting records do not go in the recycling the week the company is struck. It matters most for an estate or for a former director, since they are the people most likely to be asked a question about a corporation that no longer exists. We tell you what to keep and for how long as part of the final-year work.
