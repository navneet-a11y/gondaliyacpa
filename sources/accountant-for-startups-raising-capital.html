Corporate Tax Filing at a Loss

  • A corporation must file a T2 for every tax year even with no revenue and no tax payable, within six months of its year end, and founders are often surprised that the obligation starts at incorporation.
  • The return is not a formality: it is the document that establishes and carries forward your non-capital losses, which run twenty years forward and three years back once there is income to apply them against.
  • Where control is acquired we file the short return that ITA subsection 249(4) requires, because the deemed year end creates a filing deadline nobody has in their calendar.
  • We claim the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit from the first year there is income to shelter, and set instalments only once the tax owing justifies them.
  • We keep the six years of records ITA section 230 requires, including the cap table history, because a question about a grant made in year one will be asked in year four by somebody else.
2

CCPC Status Before the Round

  • This is the first thing we look at, and it is the one most likely to be missed entirely. A Canadian-controlled private corporation is defined by who does not control it rather than by who does.
  • Under ITA subsection 125(7) it is a private corporation resident in Canada not controlled, directly or indirectly in any manner whatever, by non-residents, by public corporations, or by any combination of them.
  • That phrase "in any manner whatever" is why the shareholders' agreement matters as much as the share register: rights, vetoes and board composition can amount to control even where the percentages do not.
  • What status carries is substantial: the small business deduction, the enhanced refundable SR&ED credit available only to CCPCs, the option deferral under subsection 7(1.1), and your own exemption on qualifying shares.
  • So we price what ending it would cost on your own numbers and put that next to the valuation on offer, which is the comparison founders are rarely given before they choose a lead.
3

Acquisition of Control and the Loss Pool

  • For a company four years into development, the accumulated losses are often the most valuable thing on the balance sheet, and a financing can impair them without anyone intending it.
  • Where control of a corporation is acquired, ITA subsection 249(4) deems a tax year to end immediately before that time, which means a short return, a stub period close and an off-cycle deadline.
  • Then ITA subsection 111(5) restricts the non-capital losses carried forward: they become deductible only against income from the same or a similar business carried on by the corporation for profit.
  • Net capital losses carried forward are generally not available at all after an acquisition of control, which matters for a company that has written down investments or disposed of assets.
  • We quantify the pool and what the restriction would do to it, so a founder weighing two term sheets can see which one costs the loss carryforward and which one does not.
4

Share Capital, Not Revenue

  • Money received for newly issued shares is a capital transaction and not income, and we have corrected books where a seed round had been recorded as sales for an entire fiscal year.
  • So we record it properly: stated capital for the issued shares, contributed surplus where it belongs, and the share classes kept distinct in the ledger rather than pooled into one equity line.
  • Paid-up capital is a corporate tax concept with its own arithmetic, and it is not the same number as any shareholder's adjusted cost base. Both matter later, at different times, to different people.
  • We reconcile the ledger to the cap table and the minute book at every close, which is the reconciliation that takes weeks if nobody has done it and minutes if somebody has.
  • And we keep SAFEs, notes and options visible in the records rather than in a spreadsheet on a founder's laptop, because the spreadsheet is what diligence always finds first.
5

Financing Costs Under Paragraph 20(1)(e)

  • Raising money costs money: legal fees, accounting fees, agent fees, filing costs. Those amounts have their own rule, and expensing them in the year they are paid is not it.
  • ITA paragraph 20(1)(e) permits a deduction for expenses incurred in the course of issuing or selling shares, or borrowing money, spread over five years at twenty per cent a year.
  • Getting that right is worth real money to a company that has just spent a meaningful amount on a raise, and it is the kind of deduction that is simply lost when nobody identifies it.
  • We separate the costs of the financing from ordinary professional fees in the records as they are incurred, because reconstructing the split from invoices two years later is unreliable.
  • On the sales tax side, ETA section 185 generally treats a registrant that is not a financial institution as having acquired inputs relating to a financial supply in its commercial activity, so the tax is usually recoverable.
6

SAFEs, Convertible Notes and Bridges

  • Most early rounds now arrive as a SAFE or a convertible note, and the first question is the one the template does not answer: is this instrument debt or equity for tax purposes?
  • It turns on the terms rather than the title: whether there is a principal amount repayable, whether interest accrues, what happens on a failure to convert, and what rights the holder has meanwhile.
  • The answer drives the consequences. Interest accruing on debt raises deductibility questions under ITA paragraph 20(1)(c), and where the holder is a non-resident it raises Part XIII withholding questions on top of them.
  • We also look at what conversion does: the share capital created, the paid-up capital, and the effect on the cap table that the next round's investors will test against the CCPC definition.
  • We will not characterise your instrument on a web page. We read the actual document, tell you plainly how we see it, and record it consistently from that point rather than changing treatment later.
7

Stock Options Under Section 7

  • An option plan is a tax plan whether or not anybody treated it as one. ITA section 7 governs employee stock options, and the benefit generally arises on exercise rather than on grant.
  • That benefit is the fair market value of the shares at exercise less the amount paid for them, which is why an employee can face tax on shares they cannot sell and have not been paid for.
  • ITA subsection 7(1.1) defers that inclusion until the shares are disposed of where they are shares of a CCPC and the employee deals at arm's length with it, which is one more thing status is worth.
  • The ITA paragraph 110(1)(d) deduction has conditions of its own, and paragraph 110(1)(d.1) applies to CCPC shares held for at least two years, so the holding period is worth knowing before an exercise.
  • Section 7 applies to options granted to employees: a grant to an independent contractor sits outside it, which is a different and often worse answer, and we raise it before the grant rather than after.
8

Contractors, Employees and Payroll

  • Almost every startup pays its first few people as contractors, and almost every diligence list asks about it, because the payer carries the consequence of getting it wrong.
  • It is decided on the whole relationship: control over how the work is done, ownership of tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration into the business. The agreement's label does not settle it.
  • A full-time developer working your hours on your systems with no other clients is on the wrong side of most of those factors however the contract reads, and we say so rather than leaving it to be found.
  • Where people are employees we set payroll up properly from the first payment, with source deductions, remittances and T4 slips; where they are genuinely independent, fees for services go on a T4A.
  • Directors are personally liable for unremitted source deductions under ITA section 227.1, which for a founder-director makes an arrears balance a personal exposure rather than a company problem.
9

Non-Resident Investors and Withholding

  • A non-resident on the cap table changes more than the CCPC question. Payments out of the company to that investor can carry Canadian withholding, and the company is the one that has to withhold.
  • Dividends paid to a non-resident are subject to Part XIII withholding at the statutory twenty-five per cent rate, frequently reduced by an applicable treaty, which has to be established rather than assumed.
  • Interest can be subject to Part XIII where it is participating debt interest or is paid to a non-arm's-length non-resident, which is a live question on a convertible note held by a foreign fund.
  • The thin capitalization rules in ITA subsection 18(4) can deny part of the interest deduction on debt owed to specified non-residents, so a shareholder loan from a foreign parent is not a neutral choice.
  • And on a later exit, section 116 applies to a non-resident disposing of taxable Canadian property, which is a question worth knowing about years before anybody is selling.
10

GST/HST While Pre-Revenue

  • A company with no sales is not required to register, because the $30,000 small supplier threshold has not been crossed, and that is exactly why so many startups leave money on the table.
  • Registration is available voluntarily even below the threshold, and once registered a company may claim input tax credits under ETA section 169 on the inputs it acquires for use in its commercial activity.
  • For a pre-revenue company the burn is almost entirely taxable inputs: software, hardware, contractors, professional fees, rent. The tax on those is real cash and recovering it extends runway.
  • We run that decision properly rather than as a reflex, because registration brings filing obligations and the credits have to be supported by invoices rather than by card statements.
  • Once revenue starts, particularly software revenue sold across borders, the place of supply and zero-rating questions arrive together, and we deal with them before the first invoice rather than after the first return.
11

Statements, Catch-Up and the Data Room

  • A fund's diligence list is long, specific and arrives with a deadline. What comes back in the first week sets the tone for the rest of the negotiation, fairly or not.
  • We prepare compilation engagement financial statements and the supporting schedules, reconciled to the filed returns and to the cap table, so the three documents agree without a covering note.
  • Where corporate years, HST returns or slips are outstanding we bring them current first, and where there is an exposure rather than a delay the Voluntary Disclosures Program on Form RC199 is available before the CRA makes contact.
  • We flag what diligence will find before it finds it: the contractor who looks like an employee, the undocumented founder loan, the option granted without a board resolution, the remittance paid late.
  • Each of those becomes a representation, an indemnity or a price adjustment if the investor raises it first, and costs a fraction of that to deal with beforehand.
12

CRA Audit Resolution

  • When a contractor is reassessed as an employee, we deal with it on the classification factors and the real working arrangement, and with the payroll consequences that follow if the reassessment stands.
  • When a shareholder loan is questioned under ITA subsection 15(2), we produce the loan documentation and the repayment history, and we deal with the imputed interest question under section 80.4.
  • When the loss carryforward is reviewed after a change of control, we answer with the pool as it was established in the returns and the analysis of the same or similar business test done at the time.
  • When a credit claim or an expense allocation is examined, we produce the records as they were kept rather than reconstructing them, which is the difference between a query and a problem.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment, request relief on Form RC4288 where the facts support it, and keep the investors' reporting intact while it is resolved.

Startup Financing Accounting & Tax FAQs

Is money from investors taxable income?
No. Money received for newly issued shares is a capital transaction, not income: it increases stated capital and, where the price exceeds the nominal amount allocated to the shares, contributed surplus. We mention it because we have corrected books in which an entire seed round had been recorded as sales, which made the year look like a business with impossible margins and a strange cost base. The distinction matters beyond tidiness. Paid-up capital is a corporate tax concept with its own arithmetic and is not the same figure as any shareholder's adjusted cost base; both are relevant later, at different times, to different people. Getting the entries right at the time is also what makes the ledger reconcile to the cap table when an investor's diligence team asks for both.
Will my round end CCPC status, and what do I lose if it does?
It can, and this is the question we look at first. Under ITA subsection 125(7) a Canadian-controlled private corporation is a private corporation resident in Canada that is not controlled, directly or indirectly in any manner whatever, by non-resident persons, by public corporations, or by any combination of them. That last phrase is why the shareholders' agreement matters as much as the share register: board composition, veto rights and consent thresholds can amount to control where the percentages alone would not. What goes with the status is substantial: the small business deduction, the enhanced refundable SR&ED investment tax credit that only CCPCs can claim, the option deferral under subsection 7(1.1), and the shareholders' access to the exemption on qualified small business corporation shares. We will not conclude on a web page whether your round ends it; we read the actual documents and price the consequence.
What happens to my losses on a change of control?
Two things, and the second is the expensive one. First, where control of a corporation is acquired, ITA subsection 249(4) deems a tax year to end immediately before that time, so a short return is due and a stub-period close arrives off-cycle. Second, ITA subsection 111(5) restricts the non-capital losses carried forward: after the acquisition they are deductible only against income from the same or a similar business carried on by the corporation for profit. Net capital losses carried forward are generally not available at all. For a company four years into development, that loss pool is often the second most valuable thing it owns after its intellectual property, and nothing in a term sheet mentions it. We quantify the pool and model the restriction while the terms are still open.
Is a SAFE or a convertible note debt or equity for tax?
It depends on the instrument, and we will not answer it from the name on the cover page. The analysis turns on the terms: whether there is a principal amount repayable, whether interest accrues, what happens if conversion never occurs, what rights the holder has in the meantime, and what the parties can enforce. The characterisation then drives everything downstream. If it is debt, interest raises deductibility questions under ITA paragraph 20(1)(c), and where the holder is a non-resident it raises Part XIII withholding questions as well. On conversion there is share capital to record and paid-up capital to compute, and the resulting cap table is what the next round's investors will test against the CCPC definition. We read the document, tell you how we see it, and then record it consistently rather than changing treatment later.
Can I deduct the legal and accounting fees of a raise?
Yes, but not in the year you pay them, and this is a deduction that is routinely lost. ITA paragraph 20(1)(e) permits a deduction for expenses incurred in the course of issuing or selling shares, or borrowing money, spread over five years at 20% a year. For a company that has just spent a meaningful amount on legal and accounting work for a financing, that is real money, and it only gets claimed if somebody identifies the costs as financing costs rather than lumping them into professional fees. We separate them in the records as they are incurred, because splitting them out of invoices two years later is unreliable. On the sales tax side, ETA section 185 generally treats a registrant that is not a financial institution as having acquired inputs relating to a financial supply in the course of its commercial activity, so the GST/HST on those costs is usually recoverable.
How are employee stock options taxed?
Under ITA section 7, and the key point is that the tax event is generally the exercise, not the grant. The benefit is the fair market value of the shares at exercise less the amount paid for them, which is how an employee can face a tax bill on shares they cannot sell and have had no cash from. ITA subsection 7(1.1) defers that inclusion until the shares are disposed of where they are shares of a CCPC and the employee deals at arm's length with the corporation, which is one more thing CCPC status is worth. The paragraph 110(1)(d) deduction has its own conditions, and paragraph 110(1)(d.1) applies to CCPC shares held for at least two years, so the holding period is worth knowing before anybody exercises. We do not set the fair market value for a grant: that is a valuation, and it is a separate engagement.
Can I grant options to a contractor?
You can grant them, but the tax treatment is not the one you are expecting. ITA section 7 applies to options granted to employees; a grant to a genuinely independent contractor sits outside it, and the consequences — including the timing of any income inclusion and the availability of the paragraph 110(1)(d) deduction — are different and often worse for the recipient. This matters because early-stage companies routinely grant options to advisers, fractional help and developers engaged on contract, using the same plan document for everyone. It is worth raising before the grant rather than after, and it interacts with the classification question: if the person is an employee in substance, there are payroll consequences too. We look at both together rather than one at a time.
Our first developers were contractors. Is that a problem?
It is the single most common item on a startup diligence list, and the answer depends on facts rather than on the agreement. Employment or independent contract is decided on the whole relationship: control over how the work is done, ownership of tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration into the business. A full-time developer working your hours, on your systems, with no other clients, is on the wrong side of most of those factors however the contract reads. The exposure is the company's, because the payer carries the obligation to withhold, and ITA section 227.1 makes directors personally liable for unremitted source deductions, which for a founder-director is a personal exposure rather than a company problem. We review it honestly, set payroll up properly where it is needed, and issue T4A slips where the arrangement really is independent.
Do I have to file a T2 if we have no revenue?
Yes. A corporation must file a T2 for every tax year, within six months of its year end, even with no revenue and no tax payable. Founders are often told the opposite, or simply assume there is nothing to file until there is something to tax. The return is not a formality: it is the document that establishes and carries forward your non-capital losses, which run twenty years forward and three years back once there is income to apply them against. A company that has not filed its loss years has a loss pool that exists in its own spreadsheet and nowhere else, which is a problem both for an investor's diligence and for the company's own future tax bill. Unfiled years are also the easiest thing in a data room to find.
Should I register for HST before we have any revenue?
Often yes, and it is one of the few decisions at this stage that puts cash back into the company. Registration is not required until worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000, but it is available voluntarily below that, and once registered a company may claim input tax credits under ETA section 169 on inputs acquired for use in its commercial activity. For a pre-revenue company the burn is almost entirely taxable inputs — software, hardware, contractors, professional fees, rent — so the tax on them is real money and recovering it extends runway. It is a decision rather than a reflex: registration brings filing obligations, and credits must be supported by proper invoices rather than card statements. We run the arithmetic on your actual spending before advising either way.
Do I withhold tax on payments to a non-resident investor?
Possibly, and the obligation is the company's rather than the investor's. Dividends paid to a non-resident are subject to Part XIII withholding at the statutory 25% rate, frequently reduced by an applicable tax treaty, which has to be established with documentation rather than assumed from the investor's address. Interest can be subject to Part XIII where it is participating debt interest or is paid to a non-arm's-length non-resident, which is a live question on a convertible note held by a foreign fund. The thin capitalization rules in ITA subsection 18(4) can also deny part of the interest deduction on debt owed to specified non-residents. We do not state a treaty rate for a particular country on this page; we look at the actual investor and the actual payment.
What will investors ask for in diligence?
A long and specific list, usually with a short deadline. Expect financial statements and every filed corporate return, the loss balances, the cap table reconciled to the minute book, every SAFE and note, the option plan with each grant's date, price, vesting and board approval, the payroll and HST account positions, worker agreements and the slips issued, shareholder loans and their documentation, and major contracts. What they are really testing is whether there is a liability that should be priced or a representation that cannot be given. The predictable findings are the contractor who looks like an employee, the option granted without a resolution, the founder loan nobody documented, the late remittance and the unfiled year. Each becomes a representation, an indemnity or a price adjustment if the investor finds it first, and costs far less to fix beforehand.
