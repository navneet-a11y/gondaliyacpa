Cross-Border Expansion Accounting & Tax FAQs

Should I use a branch, a corporation or an LLC for my US expansion?
This is the question worth the most money and the one most often answered in an afternoon by whoever files the paperwork. We will not give you a general answer here, because there is not one: the right structure depends on where your profit arises, how you intend to bring it home, who owns the Canadian company, and whether American tax will actually be paid in the entity that earns the income. What we will do is model each option on the Canadian side before anything is formed, and flag the one specific trap that catches Canadian companies more than any other. CRA generally treats a US LLC as a corporation. The United States generally treats it as fiscally transparent, taxing its income in the hands of its members rather than in the entity. When the two countries disagree about who earned income and who paid tax on it, relief under ITA section 126 becomes very difficult to claim and tax can end up paid twice with no credit for it. The American side of the choice belongs to an adviser licensed there, and we coordinate with them rather than guessing.
How is a US LLC taxed in Canada?
CRA generally treats a US LLC as a corporation for Canadian tax purposes, which is the heart of the problem, because the United States generally treats the same entity as fiscally transparent. From Canada's point of view there is a foreign corporation that earned income. From the American point of view there is no separate taxpayer at all and the income belongs to the members. Those two characterisations do not fit together, and the consequence lands on the foreign tax credit: relief under ITA section 126 depends on the same person being taxed on the same income in both countries, and a hybrid mismatch can break that link. Treaty relief and the specific provisions that address hybrid entities are technical and fact-dependent, and we do not assert an outcome for your structure on a web page. We model it, document what each country is doing, and work with your American adviser on the position. If you have already formed an LLC, this is worth looking at now rather than at your next year-end.
Do I have a permanent establishment in the United States?
That is a question of fact under Article V of the Canada–United States Tax Convention, and it decides whether the United States can tax your business profits at all under Article VII. We frame it properly rather than answering it with a rule of thumb. The factors that matter are things like whether you have a fixed place of business there, whether someone habitually concludes contracts on your behalf, how long a project site persists, and what your people actually do when they are in the country. We state no day count, no threshold and no conclusion here, because every one of those is specific and several are beyond what we will assert on a public page. What we will tell you is that the answer changes as the expansion grows: a company selling remotely, a company with a salesperson travelling, and a company with an office and staff are three genuinely different positions, and the point to look at it is before the third one happens. A treaty-based position taken on an American return is disclosed on Form 8833.
What is transfer pricing and does it apply to my management fee?
Yes, it applies, and the management fee is usually the first charge an auditor asks about. ITA section 247 requires transactions between a Canadian company and a non-arm's-length non-resident to be priced as they would have been between parties dealing at arm's length. That reaches everything moving between your parent and your American subsidiary: goods, management fees, royalties, shared engineering and administrative cost, and intercompany interest. Two things matter in practice. First, the documentation is expected to exist contemporaneously, meaning prepared at the time rather than reconstructed when CRA asks, and the penalty regime treats the absence of documentation as its own problem. Second, non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents are reported on Form T106, which groups miss constantly because the charges felt internal rather than reportable. We will not quote you a method, a margin or a markup here. Those come out of a functional analysis of what each entity actually does, and a figure taken off a web page is exactly the kind of position that fails under review.
What do I have to file in Canada once I have a US subsidiary?
More than most companies expect, and the information returns carry penalties whether or not any tax is owing. Form T1134 reports your foreign affiliate annually. Form T106 reports non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. Form T1135 is required where specified foreign property costs more than $100,000, and it is missed constantly because owners think of the subsidiary as an operation rather than as property. Behind those sit ITA sections 91 and 95, which can attribute a foreign affiliate's passive income back to the Canadian parent as foreign accrual property income, and ITA section 247 with its documentation expectation. Your T2 itself changes too, because American-source revenue and the foreign tax credit under ITA section 126 now have to be computed by country and by income type rather than estimated. We set all of this up as an annual cycle rather than a year-end scramble, and where years have already been missed we bring them forward through a Voluntary Disclosures Program application on Form RC199.
How do I get money out of my US subsidiary and back into Canada?
Plan this before the subsidiary is profitable rather than after, because the route you build is hard to change once the cash is sitting there. The Canadian side has clear mechanics. Where value flows out of Canada, Part XIII tax under ITA section 212 applies to dividends, interest and royalties at the statutory rate, reduced by treaty where treaty relief is available, with NR4 reporting. Coming the other way, dividends from a foreign affiliate interact with the surplus account rules, which is a genuinely technical area where the computation matters more than the intuition. The American side — what the subsidiary pays before anything is distributed, and what applies on distribution — belongs to your American adviser, and we coordinate rather than assert. We also state no treaty-reduced rate for any payment on this page, because whether treaty relief is available at all is specific to the payment and the parties.
What about US state taxes? Everyone tells me those are the real problem.
They are often right, and this is the clearest line on the page. State and local tax — nexus, registration, apportionment, sales tax and franchise taxes — is real, it is separate from American federal tax, it varies substantially, and it surprises Canadian companies more than anything else in an expansion. A company can have no federal filing concern and a genuine state obligation at the same time. We will not tell you which states you have an obligation in, what creates nexus, what any rate or threshold is, or whether you need a sales tax registration, because we are not licensed to give you that answer and a figure from a Canadian accountant's web page is worth nothing when it is wrong. What we will do is tell you that it belongs on your list, early, and that it belongs with an American adviser. We then make sure the Canadian side of the group accounts for whatever tax you do pay there, so it is available for the foreign tax credit computation rather than lost in a general expense.
Do I charge HST on sales to American customers?
Not automatically outside the tax, which is the mistake. ETA Schedule VI Part V zero-rates certain exports, and whether a particular supply of yours falls within it depends on what was supplied and where it was supplied, not on where the customer's billing address happens to be. A physical good shipped out of Canada with export documentation behind it is a different case from a service performed in Canada for a customer who is abroad, and a digital service is different again. We review your supplies rather than applying a label, retain the documentation behind each zero-rated supply, and file your GST34 returns on that basis. The place of supply rules are also where we most often find errors on a file that has just started exporting, usually because somebody followed the invoice address. One practical note: if you are not yet registered, an expansion commonly pushes you past the $30,000 threshold over four consecutive calendar quarters in the same year it begins, so it is worth watching.
Can I deduct the interest on money I borrowed to fund the expansion?
Possibly, and two separate rules decide how much. Thin capitalisation under ITA subsection 18(4) restricts the deduction where a Canadian corporation is financed by debt from specified non-residents beyond a prescribed proportion of equity. The excessive interest and financing expenses limitation in ITA section 18.2 is a separate and more recent restriction that can limit net interest and financing expenses by reference to earnings. Both can apply to the same company. We do not state the ratio or the threshold in either rule on this page, because they are specific, they have transitional and exception mechanics, and a number quoted loosely is worse than no number at all. What we will say plainly is that a debt-funded expansion needs both rules looked at before the borrowing is structured, not at the year-end after the interest has been accrued and spent. Where intercompany debt is involved, the interest rate itself is also a transfer pricing question under ITA section 247.

My statements merge everything together. Does that actually matter for financing?
It matters more than most owners expect, and it is usually the reason a facility stalls. A lender funding an expansion is trying to answer three questions from your statements: how much of the revenue growth was real work rather than the exchange rate, how the group funds itself internally, and what the parent is actually carrying. Merged Canadian and American revenue answers none of them. A netted intercompany line hides the group's internal funding entirely, and statements that never state the translation basis leave the reader unable to tell growth from currency movement. A CSRS 4200 compilation engagement across two fiscal years fixes this: revenue separated by source, each intercompany balance shown explicitly rather than netted, the translation basis stated on the face of the statements, and the investment in the subsidiary presented consistently and tied to the Form T1134 filed. We have seen a declined facility turn into an approved one on presentation alone, with no change to the underlying business.
We expanded two years ago and filed none of this. How bad is it?
Common, fixable, and much better addressed now than after CRA writes. The exposure is mostly in the information returns rather than in tax: unfiled Form T1134 returns on the foreign affiliate, unfiled Form T106 returns on non-arm's-length transactions, and unfiled Form T1135 where specified foreign property cost more than $100,000 all carry their own penalty regimes regardless of whether tax was owing. Add undocumented intercompany charges under ITA section 247, and a foreign tax credit position that cannot be computed because nobody separated Canadian from American revenue.
