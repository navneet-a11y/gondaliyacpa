Snowbird Accounting & Tax FAQs

How many days can I spend in the United States?
We will not give you a number, and you should be careful with anyone who does casually, because there are two separate questions hiding in that one and they have different answers and different consequences. The immigration question — how long you are admitted for and what happens if you exceed it — is not a tax question at all. It belongs to immigration counsel and ultimately to the border authority, and the consequences of getting it wrong are not financial. The tax question is whether the substantial presence test treats you as a United States tax resident, which counts days of presence alone and takes no account of why you were there or what status you hold. That test looks across more than one year, which is precisely why a running record of days present matters. We frame the test and the closer connection exception for your American preparer; we state no day count anywhere on this page.
Do I have to report my Florida condo on Form T1135?
Probably not, and this is the single most useful thing on this page, because most snowbirds assume the opposite and some have been filing unnecessarily for years. The definition of specified foreign property in ITA subsection 233.3(1) excludes personal-use property. A vacation home that you use personally generally falls within that exclusion, so it is generally not a Form T1135 item at all. What does count, and what people overlook while worrying about the condo, is the rest of it: an American bank account, a brokerage account, and other specified foreign property are reportable on their own footing once the total cost exceeds $100,000, measured on cost amount rather than market value. Two cautions. Renting the property out puts the personal-use exclusion in question and we review it rather than assuming either way. And we reach that conclusion on your facts rather than from this page.
Is my US property subject to US estate tax?
It can be, and this is the exposure this group is almost never told about. A person who is not a United States resident but who dies owning United States situs assets — which include American real property, and can include certain American securities — may be within the US estate tax regime, even with no citizenship, no status and no American income. Relief is available under Article XXIX B of the Canada–United States Tax Convention, which includes a marital credit. That is the shape of it, and it is as far as we will go. We state no threshold, no exemption amount, no rate and no outcome, and we do not tell you whether any particular asset is US situs, because those figures move and the determination is specific. What we do is build the schedule of what you own and how it is titled, then put it in front of cross-border counsel while something can still be done about it.
What happens when I sell the property?
Two things, and one of them surprises people at the closing table. On the American side, a disposition of a United States real property interest by a foreign person is subject to FIRPTA withholding, which means an amount is held back from your proceeds at closing regardless of what the eventual tax turns out to be. Where the real liability is lower there is a withholding certificate application that can reduce it, and both the application and its outcome belong to your American preparer. On the Canadian side you report the same capital gain and claim the ITA section 126 foreign tax credit for the American tax finally paid, which means the American return generally has to be settled before the Canadian credit can be computed properly. The thing that decides how much tax you pay in both countries is your cost base, so find the purchase documents and the improvement records before you list, not after.
I rent the place out for part of the winter. What do I have to do?
Report it in both countries, and keep one record you probably are not keeping. The rent is American-source income and can require a Form 1040-NR, with an election available to be taxed on a net rather than gross basis; your American preparer handles that side and we do not assert which basis is better for you. The same net rental income is also reported on your Canadian return, because you remain resident here and are taxed on worldwide income under ITA subsection 2(1), with relief through the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126. The record is the split between the rental period and your own personal use, season by season. That split drives what is deductible in each country, nobody reconstructs it accurately from memory two years later, and it is also what puts the Form T1135 personal-use exclusion in question once rent is involved.
Am I still a Canadian resident if I spend five months in Arizona?
Almost certainly, if your home, your spouse and your life are still here, but it is a question of fact rather than a matter of arithmetic. Canadian residency turns on residential ties: a dwelling here, a spouse or common-law partner here, dependants here, with secondary ties also weighed, and CRA's view is set out in Income Tax Folio S5-F1-C1. A couple who keep their house in Ontario, return every spring and leave their doctor, their bank and their family here are in a very different position from someone who sold the house and kept a storage locker. The practical consequence of remaining resident is simple and worth being clear about: you are taxed here on worldwide income, so American rental income and American gains belong on your Canadian return with the foreign tax credit claimed. Where the position is genuinely unclear we can request a determination from CRA on Form NR73.
What is the closer connection exception and do I need Form 8840?
The closer connection exception is the mechanism that addresses being caught by the substantial presence test despite having your real life somewhere else, and Form 8840 is where it is claimed. That is the mechanism, and naming it is genuinely useful because a great many snowbirds have never heard of it. What we will not do is tell you whether you qualify, whether you need to file it, or what it produces, because the eligibility conditions are specific, the determination belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States, and a wrong answer here has real consequences. We also note that a treaty-based position is disclosed on Form 8833 and that Article IV governs residence under the treaty. What we do on our side is make sure the days-present record exists so that whoever answers the question can actually answer it rather than estimate.
Should I own the property jointly with my spouse, or in a trust?
We do not answer this, and we want to be clear that the reason is not caution for its own sake. How American property is held interacts with at least four things at once: the US estate tax exposure and the treaty relief available, probate in the American state where the property sits, whether your Canadian will reaches those assets at all, and the ordinary income tax treatment of rent and gains in both countries. Getting it right is a cross-border legal structuring question and it belongs to counsel qualified in both places. An accountant who answers it from a web page, or who recommends a structure because it worked for another client, is guessing with your estate. What we do is useful and concrete: we build the schedule of what you own and exactly how each item is currently titled, which is the document counsel needs before they can advise at all.
Do I need an ITIN?
Generally yes if you have to file in the United States, whether that is a rental return, a return after a sale, or a Form 8840 position, because an American filing needs an identifying number attached to it. The number is applied for on Form W-7. We name it so you know the step exists and can raise it early, and we assert nothing about the procedure, the documentation required, the timeline or the outcome, because that is a United States process and your American preparer runs it. The practical point is about sequencing rather than substance: people routinely discover the need for a number at the moment they are trying to meet a deadline, usually a closing, and that is the worst possible time. If an American filing is anywhere on your horizon, including a sale you are contemplating, raise it with your American preparer well ahead.
Does spending the winter away affect my provincial health coverage?
It can, and we do not answer it. Provincial health insurance has its own residency rules, they are set by the province rather than by any tax statute, they are not the same test as tax residency, and they have been changed more than once. We state no day requirement, no waiting period, no eligibility rule and no entitlement for any province, because the only reliable answer comes from the provincial authority itself and because an answer that was right a few years ago may not be right now. Call the ministry or health insurance plan in your province, ask specifically about extended absences, and get the answer in writing before you plan a longer stay. We mention it here only because it is one of the two or three things that genuinely change a snowbird's plans, and because the day count it depends on is not the same count the American tax test uses.
Will I end up paying tax twice on the rent or the sale?
You should not, and the mechanism that prevents it is the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126, claimed on your Canadian return for American tax actually paid on the same income. Two things make it work in practice. The first is sequence: the credit is based on the tax finally paid rather than on anything withheld along the way, so the American return generally needs to be settled before the Canadian credit can be computed properly, and that matters most in a year you sell. The second is support: the credit is computed by country and by income type, so rental income and a capital gain are handled separately rather than lumped into one figure. Where we most often find money on a snowbird file, it is not a planning idea at all. It is American tax that was genuinely paid on rental income over several years with no credit ever claimed against it here.
I have had the place for twenty years and never looked at any of this. Where do I start?
With the paperwork, not the filings, because on this file the records are the hard part and everything else follows from them. Start by finding the original purchase documents and whatever you have on improvements over the years, which is the cost base that will decide the tax on an eventual sale in both countries. Then write down what you own in the United States and exactly how each item is titled. Then start the days-present record from this season forward, imperfect as it will be for earlier years. From there we do the ordinary work: report any rental income for the years still open and claim the credits that were missed, settle the Form T1135 question on the actual definition rather than on an assumption, and bring anything genuinely unfiled forward on Form RC199, which when accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief.
