Filing Requirements and Deadlines

Deadlines

Understanding Corporate Tax Filing Deadlines for New Corporations

New corporations in Canada face a few important filing deadlines to keep in mind. The main one is the T2 Corporate Tax Return. You need to file it within six months after your corporation’s fiscal year ends. For example, if your company started on January 1st, your first T2 would usually be due by June 30th that same year.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: The example is wrong. The T2 is due six months after the fiscal year-end, not six months after the start date. A corporation that starts on January 1 with a December 31 year-end files its first T2 by June 30 of the following year. As written, a reader would think the return is due before the first year has ended. Please correct the example.

Here are some key dates to remember:

  • T2 Due Date: Six months after your fiscal year-end
  • Balance-Due Day: Usually two or three months after the fiscal year-end, depending on your corporate type

Missing these dates can cause penalties. So, knowing when to file is really important for any new business.

Defining the Fiscal Year and Its Impact on Filing Dates

Your fiscal year-end date affects when you have to file your taxes. You can pick a fiscal year that fits your business cycle. But, the first tax year can’t be longer than 53 weeks.

Here’s what that means:

  • If you pick December 31 as your fiscal year-end at incorporation, then all filings follow that date.
  • Your first tax period might be shorter or longer, but never over 53 weeks.

Choosing the right fiscal year-end makes it easier to plan and track your finances.

Our Actual Experience

The fiscal year-end is the one first-year choice that sets every later date. We usually suggest a year-end in a quieter month for the business, so the T2 and the year-end bookkeeping do not land in the busiest season. Changing the year-end later needs CRA approval, so please decide it before the first return, not after.

Adjustments to Deadlines for Weekends and Public Holidays

Deadlines can change if they land on weekends or public holidays. When this happens, you get until the next business day to file or pay.

This applies to:

  • T2 Corporate Tax Returns
  • Payroll remittances
  • Other payments like GST/HST

For example, if your balance-due day falls on a Saturday, pay by Monday noon at the latest to avoid late fees.

Worth Knowing

There is no noon cut-off in the CRA rule. A payment or return due on a Saturday, Sunday or public holiday is on time if CRA receives it on the next business day. Bank processing still takes time, so paying a few business days early is the safe habit. Please consider removing the word noon.

Knowing these rules helps you avoid surprises and extra charges.

Difference Between Filing Deadlines and Payment Deadlines

It’s easy to mix up filing deadlines and payment deadlines. But they are different things.

  • Filing Deadline: When you must send in forms like the T2 return. For most new corporations, this is six months after their fiscal year ends.
  • Payment Deadline: When you must pay any taxes owed. This could be two or three months after your fiscal year ends, depending on whether you're a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) or not.

Missing either deadline can lead to fines or interest charges from the CRA.

Getting these dates right helps keep your business in good standing with tax authorities during its first year.

Key Stat

Key Stat: For a December 31 year-end, a qualifying CCPC pays its balance of tax by March 31 and files its T2 by June 30. Interest runs from March 31, three months before the return is due.

Filing Your First T2 Return

T2 Return

If you just started a corporation in Canada, you’ll need to follow certain first year corporation filing requirements Canada. Filing your T2 corporate income tax return on time matters. Missing deadlines could bring penalties. So, you should learn the new corporation filing deadlines Canada. The first year corporate tax filing Canada involves steps that meet both federal and provincial rules under the Income Tax Act (ITA).

Mandatory Electronic Filing Requirements for New Corporations

Starting in 2026, all Canadian corporations must file their T2 returns electronically. This applies no matter how big or small your company is or where it’s located. It’s part of the new corporation compliance calendar Canada.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: Mandatory electronic filing did not start in 2026. It applies to tax years that start after 2023, so it already covers every new corporation. It is also not universal: insurance corporations, non-resident corporations, corporations reporting in a functional currency and a few others are exempt. Electronic filing speeds up processing and cuts down errors compared to paper forms. You have to use certified software or CRA-approved service providers to submit online. If you don’t comply, you risk penalties or delays.

It’s a good idea to sign up for a CRA My Business Account soon after incorporation. This lets you get notices and file securely online.

Required Forms and Publications for First-Year Filings

You’ll mainly need the T2 Corporation Income Tax Return form. Along with this, expect to complete:

  • Schedule 50: Info about your shareholders.
  • Schedule 24: Declaration if this is your first-time filing.
  • GIFI forms: These cover your financial statements.
  • Provincial schedules depending on your province (like Ontario’s Schedule ON428).
Risk Warning

Risk Warning: ON428 is the Ontario schedule on a personal T1 return, not a corporate one. An Ontario corporation calculates its provincial tax on Schedule 500 of the T2. Naming a personal form in a corporate filing guide published under a CPA firm's name is worth fixing before anyone checks it.

The CRA’s T4012 – T2 Corporation – Guide helps first-year filers understand what to do. It explains how to report income, even if you had no business activity or profit in that fiscal year.

Keep proper records supporting all figures reported. This follows ITA recordkeeping rules.

Step-by-Step Guide to Filing Your T2 Return

  1. Pick Fiscal Year-End: Choose your fiscal year-end within 53 weeks of starting your company. This sets your reporting period length.
  2. Prepare Financial Statements: Make sure statements follow GAAP standards since they back up GIFI info.
  3. Fill Out Schedules: Complete Schedule 50 with shareholder details, Schedule 24 if it applies, plus any provincial forms needed.
  4. Calculate Taxable Income & Credits: Deduct things like the small business deduction if you're eligible as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC). Check current rates for 2026.
  5. Use Certified Software: File electronically before your deadline—usually six months after fiscal year-end.
  6. Save Confirmation: Keep CRA receipt numbers as proof you filed on time.
  7. Plan Payment If Needed: Remember payment deadlines are different; usually due two or three months after fiscal year-end (see next section).

Following these steps helps you meet new corporation filing deadlines Canada smoothly and avoid problems with the CRA.

Worth Knowing

For a Canadian private corporation, the GAAP that applies is usually Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises (ASPE). A first-year corporation with no audit requirement normally has its statements prepared under a compilation engagement, which is enough to support the GIFI schedules.

Eligibility and Procedures for Extended Payment Deadlines

Your T2 return is due six months after your fiscal year ends but when you pay taxes depends on your situation:

  • If you're a CCPC with less than $500,000 active business limit and owe under $3,000 in taxes annually, payment is due three months after fiscal year-end.
  • Other corporations must pay within two months of fiscal year-end.
Risk Warning

Risk Warning: The $3,000 figure does not belong in the first bullet. It is the instalment threshold, not a condition for the three-month balance-due day. A corporation gets three months if it is a CCPC throughout the year, claims the small business deduction for the year or the previous year, and its taxable income for the previous year, with associated corporations, was not more than the $500,000 business limit. A CCPC owing $40,000 can still have three months. Please correct the first bullet.

If you can’t pay by the balance-due day, interest starts adding up right away. It’s best to talk to a CPA quickly who can check your case. Just know that getting extra time to pay isn’t automatic; you have to arrange it directly with CRA.

Tax Payments and Instalments

Payments

If your corporation just started in Canada, you need to know about corporate income tax payments. The first year corporation filing requirements Canada include sending in the T2 return and following instalment payment rules set by the CRA. These instalments help make sure you pay your taxes on time and avoid extra charges.

Determining Instalment Requirements for New Corporations

Corporations must pay instalments if their net tax owed goes over $3,000 in the current or previous year[1]. This rule is key when setting up a new corporation compliance calendar Canada because it adds more deadlines beyond just filing returns.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: The test is the other way round. A corporation does not have to pay instalments if its tax for either the current year or the previous year is $3,000 or less. Instalments are required only when both years are over $3,000. That is why the next paragraph is right that most new corporations start in their second year. Please change "the current or previous year" to "both the current and the previous year".

Usually, new corporations start paying instalments in their second tax year unless special cases apply. But sometimes, businesses might have to pay earlier. This depends on projected income or related companies’ past tax amounts.

The CRA checks your business number (BN) and past filings to decide. So, keep a close eye on new corporation filing deadlines Canada to avoid missing any instalment notices.

  • Instalments start if net tax payable > $3,000 per year.
  • First-year payments usually don’t happen but can vary.
  • Compliance calendars add quarterly or monthly payment dates.

Instalment Due Dates and Payment Schedules

The balance-due day for paying corporate income tax is different from the T2 return deadline.

  • Most corporations pay two months after fiscal year-end.
  • If your company is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), you have three months instead[2].

For example, if your fiscal year ends December 31 and you’re a CCPC, you have until March 31 to pay without penalties.

Once instalments kick in, they’re due monthly or quarterly depending on what CRA assigns. Knowing this helps you build a solid new corporation compliance calendar Canada that fits both filings and payments.

Worth Knowing

CRA does not assign quarterly instalments. A small CCPC can choose them if it meets CRA's conditions: taxable income and taxable capital within the limits, the small business deduction claimed, and a clean filing and payment record for the past 12 months. Every other corporation pays monthly, on the last day of each month.

Dates shown are examples; always check official info yearly.

Calculating Instalment Amounts and Avoiding Underpayments

If your estimated corporate tax over $3,000, instalments may start in your second tax year[1]. You can pick from three ways to figure out how much to pay:

  • No-calculation: Pay equal parts based on last year's taxes.
  • Prior-year amount: Use actual taxes paid last year.
  • Current-year estimate: Guess this year’s taxes but watch carefully.
Worth Knowing

CRA's three options differ slightly from the list above. Option 1 is one-twelfth of this year's estimated tax each month. Option 2 is one-twelfth of last year's tax. Option 3 bases the first two payments on the year before last and the other ten on last year. The no-calculation option is the amount on CRA's instalment reminders, which uses option 3. Please align the labels.

Paying less than needed can lead to interest even if you catch up later[3]. To avoid this:

  • Check taxable income often.
  • Change instalments as estimates change.
  • Get advice before finalizing amounts—things like small business deductions or dividends affect your tax owed.

Following these tips matches first year corporate tax filing Canada rules while helping cash flow stay smooth inside your new corporation compliance calendar Canada plan.

How to Make Payments on Time to Avoid Interest and Penalties

Paying late costs you interest every day after the balance-due day until full payment arrives[4]. You might also face penalties for repeated late payments. There’s no legal extension for these dates.

To keep on track:

  • Mark T2 filing dates and balance-due days separately.
  • Set reminders before each instalment deadline.
  • Pay online via “My Business Account” at CRA for fast processing.

Missing these can add costs that hurt your startup funds when you least want it.

Penalties and Interest

Penalties

When a corporation starts in Canada, it has to follow the first year corporation filing requirements Canada and meet new corporation filing deadlines Canada. Missing these can cause penalties and interest to pile up. This means the tax bill grows bigger. Knowing what happens if you file or pay late helps owners avoid these extra costs.

Interest Charges on Late or Insufficient Instalment Payments

Interest builds up every day on any unpaid tax after the due date. Usually, that date is two months after your fiscal year ends for most Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs). For other corporations, it’s three months.1 If instalments are required but paid late or not enough during the year, interest applies from each instalment due date until you pay in full.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: The two periods are reversed. A qualifying CCPC has three months and other corporations have two. Section 1 and the table in section 3 say it correctly, so the page contradicts itself on the date that starts the interest. Please swap them.

The CRA sets the interest rates based on government bond yields. They update these rates every quarter.2 A new corporation following its first year corporate tax filing Canada rules faces growing interest charges if instalments are missed according to the new corporation compliance calendar Canada.

Worth Knowing

The prescribed rate is set each quarter from the 90-day Treasury bill rate, not bond yields. CRA adds 4 percentage points on overdue tax, and the interest compounds daily.

Corporations should track their income closely. Guessing too low on instalments may cause surprise interest charges even when annual taxes get paid by the deadline.

Penalty Rates for Late Filing and Repeated Non-Compliance

Filing a T2 return late triggers immediate penalties under federal law. This happens even if you don’t owe taxes.3 The basic penalty is 5% of unpaid tax at the deadline plus 1% per month after that, up to 12 months. If you’ve been penalized before within three years, penalties go higher: 10% upfront plus 2% monthly up to 20 months.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: The penalty is a percentage of the tax unpaid at the filing deadline, so a corporation that owes nothing then has no late-filing penalty under this rule. The sentence saying it applies even with no tax owing contradicts the formula right after it. The rule is subsection 162(1), and the repeat penalty is 162(2), not 162(7) as footnote 3 says. File on time anyway, because a late return holds up refunds and can cost the dividend refund and SR&ED claims.

New businesses must watch new corporation filing deadlines Canada closely because one missed deadline can start these penalties. These fines add up on top of any interest owing.

If a company keeps missing deadlines, CRA may audit more often. That can make future filings harder and relief programs less accessible. Keep in mind that registry filings like Ontario annual returns have different deadlines than CRA filings.4

Options for Reducing or Avoiding Instalment Interest and Penalties

There’s no automatic forgiveness for late payments or filings. Still, some actions can reduce penalties:

  • Contact CRA quickly if you see a payment problem; voluntary disclosure programs might cut penalties but need expert advice.
  • Estimate income carefully early in your first year corporate tax filing Canada so instalments don’t fall short.
  • File electronically; this prevents delays that could count as late.
  • Work with a CPA to check your numbers and rules before sending forms—especially with tricky items like shareholder loans.
Worth Knowing

Relief from penalties and interest for a missed deadline is requested under the taxpayer relief provisions on Form RC4288, for reasons such as illness or a CRA error, within 10 calendar years. The Voluntary Disclosures Program is for correcting unreported income or errors, not for late filing on its own. Please consider naming the relief form here.

Compliance Calendar and Annual Filings

Calendar

GST/HST Filing Deadlines and Thresholds for New Businesses

Your new corporation needs to watch its sales closely. If your taxable supplies go over $30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters, you must register for GST/HST. That’s the small supplier threshold. You have 29 days from passing that limit to sign up with the CRA.

Worth Knowing

There is a second test. If taxable sales go over $30,000 in a single calendar quarter, you must charge GST/HST on the sale that takes you over, and register within 29 days of that sale.

Once registered, how often you file depends on how much you make annually:

  • If annual taxable supplies are $1,500,000 or less: file once a year.
  • Between $1,500,001 and $6 million: file quarterly.
  • Over $6 million: file every month.

Payroll Remittance Deadlines and Reporting Obligations (T4/T5 Slips)

Once you pay your first employee or dividends that need reporting slips, payroll rules kick in fast.

  • Payroll Remittances: You must send source deductions like CPP and EI premiums monthly by the 15th of the next month if you’re a regular remitter. Some remitters pay more often.
  • T4 Slips: These report employee earnings and are due by February 28 each year.
  • T5 Slips: Needed if dividends or investment income were paid; also due by February 28.

Missing deadlines can lead to penalties from CRA. In Ontario, WSIB coverage starts when hiring employees unless exempted3. Employer Health Tax applies once payroll hits certain limits.

Worth Knowing

An owner-manager who controls more than 40% of the voting shares is not insurable for EI, so no EI is deducted from their own salary. CPP still applies. T4 and T5 slips are due on the last day of February, which is February 29 in a leap year.

Corporate Filing Obligations Specific to Ontario Corporations

Ontario companies face some extra steps:

First-Year Filing FAQ

FAQ
What are the Key Numbers at a Glance for new corporations in Canada?+

New corporations must note key numbers such as the $30,000 GST/HST small supplier threshold and the $3,000 corporate tax instalment threshold. These trigger registration and payment obligations.

When is the Payroll Remittance Due Date for new Ontario corporations?+

Payroll remittances are generally due monthly by the 15th of the following month after salary payments start. Some businesses may require more frequent remittance.

What is the T4/T5 Slip Filing Deadline?+

T4 and T5 slips must be filed with CRA by February 28 each year. These report employee earnings and dividend payments, respectively.

How does the CBCA Annual Return Filing Window work?+

Federal corporations under CBCA must file annual returns within 60 days after their incorporation anniversary date to maintain good standing.

What is the Ontario Annual Return Filing Window?+

Ontario corporations have up to six months after their anniversary date to file an annual return via the Ontario Business Registry.

How do first-year compliance calendars differ across industries?+

First-year calendars vary by industry due to differences in revenue cycles, payroll needs, and regulatory filings. Professional advice ensures calendar accuracy.

What Does First-Year Compliance Cost and What Drives the Fee?+

Costs vary depending on business size, complexity of filings, and need for professional services. More complex filings typically increase fees.

What Are the Risks, Compliance Issues and Prevention Controls?+

Risks include missed deadlines, penalties, interest charges, and audits. Controls involve tracking deadlines closely, using CPA guidance, and electronic filing compliance.

People Also Ask

Quick Answers
How early should I start planning my first-year corporate tax filings?+

At incorporation. The fiscal year-end, the Ontario Initial Return, GST/HST registration and the payroll account are all settled in the first weeks, and each one fixes later dates.

What happens if I miss my Ontario Annual Return Filing Window?+

There is no government fee for the annual return, but the registry record falls out of date, and a corporation that keeps failing to file can eventually be dissolved. Please file it as soon as you notice it was missed.

Can I file my T2 return without professional help?+

Yes, using CRA-certified software. The return is still yours to get right, including the GIFI financial statements and schedules, and errors in a first return carry into the opening balances of the next one.

When will I know if I need to pay corporate tax instalments?+

Generally not in the first year. Instalments start once tax is over $3,000 in both the current and the previous year, so most new corporations first owe them in year two. CRA usually starts sending reminders once it has a prior-year return.

Glossary

Terms
  • CBCA: Canada Business Corporations Act governing federal corporations.
  • CCPC: Canadian-controlled private corporation eligible for special tax rates.
  • GST/HST Small Supplier Threshold: $30,000 annual taxable supplies triggering mandatory registration.
  • Instalments: Periodic corporate tax payments based on estimated taxes owed.
