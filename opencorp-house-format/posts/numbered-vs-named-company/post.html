Naming a Corporation in Canada: Overview

1

Naming Overview

The Basics

Starting a business in Canada means choosing a name for your corporation. You can pick either a numbered company or a named company. This choice matters because it affects how your brand looks, legal rules you follow, and how you run your business. Knowing the difference helps founders and solo owners who want to incorporate.

Types of Corporate Names: Numbered vs Named Corporation

In Canada, companies mostly have two types of names: numbered corporation Canada and named corporation Canada.

  • Numbered Corporations: These get a number as their official name, like 1234567 Ontario Inc. They are quick to set up since you skip some name approval steps.
  • Named Corporations: These pick a unique name that shows what the business does or its brand, such as “Tech Innovations Inc.” You must check if the name is free using a NUANS report before registering.

Your choice depends on how fast you want to register, whether you want privacy, and your plans for branding later.

Legal Requirements for Corporate Name Registration in Canada

Rules for naming differ if you register federally or provincially. In Ontario:

  • Numbered Companies: No NUANS report needed. But they must add “Inc.” or “Ltd.” at the end.
  • Named Companies: Need review for uniqueness and clarity by the registrar. A NUANS search proves no other similar businesses exist.

Both must follow laws from the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) or the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA). So, whether you choose a numbered company or named company, legal rules apply.

Language Options for Corporate Names: English, French, or Both

You can register your corporate name in English, French, or both languages.

  • If your market is mainly Quebec or bilingual clients across Canada, consider using both languages.
  • If you operate where one language is common—like most of Ontario—you can pick just English.

Your choice affects legal compliance and how customers see and access your business across different parts of Canada.

Knowing these basics about naming a corporation in Canada—whether it's a numbered company or named company—helps you start off right and stay within legal boundaries.

What Is a Numbered Company in Canada?

2

Numbered and Named Defined

Definitions

A numbered company in Canada is a corporation that uses a number instead of a regular business name. The government assigns this number when you file the paperwork. For example, in Ontario, you might see "1234567 Ontario Inc." and federally, it could be "12345678 Canada Inc."

Numbered companies don’t need to get their names approved first. The number itself acts as the legal name, so there’s no need for a NUANS report. The numbers get handed out in order when you submit your incorporation papers.

These companies work the same way as named corporations for taxes and legal stuff. They still have to follow all the rules and use words like “Inc.” or “Ltd.” at the end of their names.

You’d pick a numbered company or named company depending on your needs — maybe you want fast setup, less hassle, or privacy.

Worth Knowing

A numbered company can still trade under a brand. Register an operating name with Ontario for $60, valid five years, and show the corporation's legal name alongside it on contracts and invoices.

What Is a Named Corporation in Canada?

A named corporation picks its own unique business name. When you incorporate in Ontario or federally, you send your name choice for approval before registering.

Here are some naming rules:

  • You must add legal endings like Inc. or Ltd.
  • Some words can’t be used unless you get special permission
  • The name has to stand out so it doesn’t confuse people

Named corporations need a NUANS report to check if the name is taken or too close to others. This report is needed for both Ontario and federal setups but doesn’t guarantee approval.

Starting with a named corporation means your brand is clear from day one. But it takes more steps and time because of the name checks.

Key Differences Between Numbered and Named Corporations

Verdict: If you want quick setup and less cost, go numbered company. If branding early is key, then choose named corporation despite extra steps.

Understanding NUANS Name Search for Named Corporations

NUANS stands for Newly Upgraded Automated Name Search. It checks your proposed corporate name against existing ones across Canada. This helps avoid copying or confusion.

Here’s what to know about NUANS:

  • Ontario Incorporation: You must get an Ontario-focused NUANS report unless you’re using a numbered company.1
  • Federal Incorporation: Requires a national NUANS report covering all provinces.2
  • Validity: The report lasts 90 calendar days after issue.3 You’ll need a new one if it expires before filing.
  • Limitations: It checks many corporate and trademark databases,4 but doesn’t guarantee your name is fully unique or safe from unregistered names.

Make sure you check the current NUANS report fee (for example, in 2026) so you budget right when planning incorporation.

Verdict: Get an up-to-date NUANS report if picking a named corporation. Remember though—it just helps identify risks but never guarantees approval.

Incorporating a Numbered Company: Process and Advantages

3

Process, Costs and Branding

Process

When you incorporate a numbered company in Canada, you get a corporation with a unique number instead of a regular name. The process is quick and simple, which many like for privacy or ease.

A numbered corporation Canada gets its legal name from the Ontario Business Registry or Corporations Canada automatically. For example, an Ontario numbered company might be called “1234567 Ontario Inc.” Federally incorporated ones use formats like “12345678 Canada Inc.” You don’t need to run a NUANS report since the number makes it unique.

Here are some advantages of incorporating a numbered company:

  • Fast setup: You skip the wait for name approval.
  • Cheaper: No fees for NUANS reports or name conflicts.
  • Privacy: No business name shows on public records, although directors are still listed publicly.
  • Simple: Great for holding companies or businesses not focused on branding.

But there are some downsides. It’s harder to build brand recognition. Banks or platforms may prefer a known name. Many use registered business names alongside their numbered company for marketing.

Verdict: A numbered company fits founders who want quick setup and lower costs but may need more work for marketing later.

Incorporating a Named Company: Process and Advantages

When you incorporate a named corporation in Canada, you pick and register your own corporate name. This requires approval from provincial registries like Ontario Business Registry or federal bodies like Corporations Canada.

Named corporations must have unique, legal names that follow rules — like using “Limited” or “Inc.” properly. Usually, you must get a NUANS report to check if your chosen name conflicts with others across Canada (federally) or just Ontario.

Advantages of named corporations:

  • Brand identity: You can create a strong market presence.
  • Credibility: Customers, suppliers, banks recognize familiar names easier.
  • Trademark fit: Your corporate name can align with trademarks (though trademarks are separate).

Drawbacks include longer wait times due to manual reviews and extra costs for NUANS reports. Your name might get rejected if it’s too generic or confusing.

Many founders want brand control and accept these challenges because named corporations help in marketing more than numbered ones do.

Verdict: Named corporations work well for entrepreneurs aiming for strong brand presence but require careful naming plans.

Legal and Tax Treatment of Numbered and Named Corporations

4

Legal, Tax and Privacy

Legal and Tax

Numbered corporations Canada and named corporations Canada both have the same legal status. They are separate entities under Canadian corporate law. Depending on where you are, they fall under either the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) or the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA). Both types offer limited liability protection. They can own property, enter contracts, sue, be sued, and handle their own taxes.

From a tax viewpoint, there’s no difference between numbered company vs named company. Each needs a business number from the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). This is for corporate income tax filing (T2), GST/HST registration if needed, payroll deductions, and other federal tax duties. You might also have to file provincially based on your business location.

Choosing between numbered corporation Canada or named corporation Canada won’t change your eligibility for government programs or compliance rules. But it will affect your branding and how the public sees your company.

Privacy and Anonymity: Misconceptions About Numbered Companies

People often think that a numbered company hides who owns it better than a named company. That’s not true in Canadian law, including in Ontario.

Both numbered companies and named companies list their directors publicly. This happens through filings with Corporations Canada or provincial registries like the Ontario Business Registry. The name—whether it’s numbers like “1234567 Ontario Inc.” or words—does not protect privacy.

Numbered names don’t hide ownership details. They don’t free businesses from transparency laws. If you want privacy, talk to a lawyer instead of relying on a numbered company name alone.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: A numbered name does not keep you off the public record. Directors' names and addresses are searchable either way, and federal corporations also file individuals with significant control information with Corporations Canada. Please do not choose a numbered company for privacy alone.

When to Choose a Numbered Company for Your Business

The choice between numbered company vs named company depends mostly on what you want for your business. Think about how fast you need to set up, costs, branding goals, and rules you must follow.

Here’s when a numbered corporation Canada might make sense:

  • Fast Setup: No NUANS report is needed because the number format is pre-approved.
  • Lower Cost: You can save money since you don’t need a name search report.
  • Branding Flexibility: Good if you plan to use different trade names registered separately.
  • Less Noticeable: Some owners prefer numbers at first to stay low-key.
  • Banking & Contracts: Banks may accept numbered companies easily; though branded names might face fewer questions.

Numbered companies work well for holding companies, short projects that may rebrand later in 2026 [1], non-resident entrepreneurs wanting quick setup without name checks [2], or consultants using different operating names besides their official one.

Our Actual Experience

Most clients who choose a numbered company do it to file the same day and register an operating name afterwards. When they later want the brand as the legal name, articles of amendment change the name and keep the same incorporation date and business number.

When to Choose a Named Corporation for Your Business

Named corporation Canada is better when branding matters at every step—from marketing to invoices. It fits businesses focused on building identity and trust.

Reasons to pick a named company include:

  • Clear Branding: A unique legal name helps customers recognize your business everywhere.
  • Trademark Steps: Incorporating with your chosen name helps when applying for trademark registration later [3].
  • Customer Trust: People often see named corporations as more professional than ones with just numbers.
  • Regulated Fields: Some industries require specific words in their company names that only naming checks confirm [4].

Businesses selling directly to consumers like stores or restaurants benefit most here. Tech startups aiming nationwide presence under CBCA also need NUANS reports before approval [5].

So: go with “numbered” if speed and ease matter most. Pick “named” if building brand value over time is your goal. Regulated professionals have extra naming rules.

Converting and Protecting Your Name

Name Changes

If you have a numbered company in Canada, like "1234567 Ontario Inc.," you might want to change it to a named corporation. This means picking a proper name instead of just numbers. The process involves filing articles of amendment with the right government office.

Here’s what you need to do:

  1. Pick a corporate name that follows the rules.
  2. Run a NUANS report if your new name requires it.
  3. File Articles of Amendment with Ontario Business Registry or Corporations Canada.
  4. Pay fees, including the articles of amendment fee Ontario, which is $150.

Changing your company name updates public records but does not change your original incorporation date or business number. Online filings are usually processed the same day.

Changing from numbered to named gives you more branding options but you must follow naming rules and pay the fees.

Protecting Your Corporate Name: Strategies and Best Practices

If you own a named company Canada, protecting your name matters. Registering your corporate name is only part of it. You want real protection.

Here’s what to know:

  • Registering Your Corporate Name stops others from using the exact same name in your area.
  • NUANS Report Validity Period is 90 days. You must file your paperwork within this time.
  • Trademark Registration at CIPO offers stronger protection across Canada. The trademark application fee at CIPO is charged for the first class plus a lower fee for each additional class, with no HST, and it rises each January.

To protect your corporate name well:

  • Search thoroughly before registering.
  • Keep registrations up to date.
  • Watch for anyone using similar names.
  • Talk to trademark agents if expanding your brand.

Remember, just registering your corporate name doesn’t give full rights. Trademarks cover much more ground legally.

Differences Between Corporate Name, Trade Name, Brand Name, and Domain Name

It helps to know how these names differ:

  • Corporate Name: The official legal name on documents like tax forms and certificates.
  • Trade Name: Also called business or operating name. If it differs from the corporate name, you register it under Business Names Act Ontario. This costs $60 every five years.
  • Brand Name: This is the marketing name people recognize. Only trademarks protect brand names properly.
  • Domain Name: The website address online; registered separately from any government agency.

A quick note: Does a corporate name give you trademark rights? No! You get trademark rights by actually using the name in business and registering it with CIPO or through common law protections.
