Laws Governing Spouse Shareholders

The Law

When a spouse owns shares in a corporation Canada, several laws come into play. The main rules come from federal and provincial corporate and tax laws. For example, the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) set out who can own shares, what rights they have, and what must be reported.

On the tax side, the Income Tax Act (ITA) controls how dividends paid to a spouse shareholder corporation Canada get taxed. This includes attribution rules under sections 74.1 to 74.5 and the Tax on Split Income (TOSI) rules under section 120.4 ITA1. Just owning shares does not mean the taxes will be simple or favorable.

Also, common-law partners who live together for at least 12 months or have a child together count as spouses for shareholding purposes2. Family law affects ownership rights but does not change federal corporate or income tax laws.

Here’s what you need to keep in mind:

  • Corporate law governs share ownership rules
  • Tax law sets how dividends are taxed for spouses
  • Common-law partners usually qualify too
  • Family law impacts ownership but not tax or corporate rules

Understanding Tax on Split Income (TOSI) Rules Affecting Spouse Shareholders

3

TOSI and Spouse Shareholders

TOSI

In Canada, the Tax on Split Income (TOSI) rules apply when a spouse owns shares in a corporation. These rules kick in to stop people from shifting income to family members who pay less tax. So, if your spouse is owning shares in corporation Canada, TOSI could affect the dividends they get.

Even if your spouse holds voting or non-voting shares, the CRA can tax dividends at the top marginal rate under section 120.4 of the Income Tax Act. It’s not just about owning shares; it’s about who really benefits from them.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: A dividend caught by TOSI is taxed at the top rate from the first dollar. Please do not rely on your spouse's low income to keep the rate down.

You might wonder, "Can my spouse own shares in my corporation?" The answer is yes. But watch out—dividend payments can lead to high taxes unless some exceptions apply. Having spouse shares in Canadian corporation needs good planning because TOSI and attribution rules may override the tax benefits you'd expect.

The CRA looks closely at things like how involved your spouse is in the business, what kind of shares they hold, and where the money to buy those shares came from. Before adding your spouse as a shareholder, it’s smart to get advice from a CPA specialist.

TOSI Exemptions Relevant to Spouse Shareholders, Including Age and Labour Tests

Some exemptions help spouses avoid paying top tax rates on dividends under TOSI rules:

  • Age 65 Spousal Rule: If you, the business owner, are 65 or older in the year, dividends your spouse receives are excluded from TOSI to the extent they would have been excluded in your hands, without your spouse needing active work involvement2.
  • Labour Test / Reasonable Return: Your spouse can qualify if they work an average of at least 20 hours per week, during the part of the year the business operates, in the current year or any five earlier years, doing real duties connected to an excluded business3.
  • 12-Month Common-Law Cohabitation Rule: If common-law partners have lived together continuously for 12 months before getting dividends, they get treated like married spouses for spousal attribution4.

You need proof like timesheets showing hours worked or documents proving dividends reflect fair returns—not just ways to dodge taxes.

If these conditions aren’t met, TOSI applies fully and your spouse pays top taxes on dividends from their shares.

Corporate Tax Considerations When Issuing Shares to a Spouse

Issuing shares to your spouse brings some important Canadian tax points:

  • Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE): This exemption works only when you sell qualified small business corporation shares personally. Shares transferred to a spouse move at your cost under the automatic section 73(1) rollover, and later gains can be attributed back to you under section 74.2.
  • Small Business Limit & CCPC Status: If spouses control the corporation together, it remains a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation eligible for small business deductions—unless associated corporations’ rules bundle incomes past limits.7
  • Associated Corporations Rules: Related owners including spouses may cause different companies within one family to be treated as linked corporations for deduction eligibility.8

Think about how new share classes or voting rights affect control and possible LCGE eligibility later on. Bad structuring can cost you valuable exemptions.

Preparation
  • Review your corporation’s articles for transfer limitations or preemptive rights clauses affecting spouses.

Verdict

A spouse can own shares in almost any private corporation, and in some professional corporations. Whether it saves tax depends on one question: does a TOSI exclusion apply to the dividends? If your spouse works in the business an average of 20 hours a week, or you are 65 or older, the answer may be yes. If not, the shares change ownership and control but not the tax bill.

2026 Update

2026 Update — what is current: This article reflects rules current to 2026. The TOSI rules introduced in 2018, the spousal attribution rules in sections 74.1 to 74.5, the automatic spousal rollover in section 73(1), the 12-month common-law partner test and the Schedule 50 requirement to list shareholders holding 10% or more are unchanged. In Ontario, the top combined rate is 47.74% on non-eligible dividends and 39.34% on eligible dividends, and a dividend caught by TOSI is taxed at that rate from the first dollar. Please note that the age-65 rule looks at the business owner's age and not the spouse's; that the excluded business test counts work in the current year or any five earlier years, so records of past hours matter; that salary paid to a spouse is outside TOSI but must be reasonable for the work done; and that Ontario lets physicians and dentists have family members hold non-voting shares of a professional corporation, while law and accounting professional corporations generally do not.
