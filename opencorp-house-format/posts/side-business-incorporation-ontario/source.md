Tax Implications of Operating a Side Business as Sole Proprietor or Corporation in Ontario
If you run a side business while working in Ontario, taxes can get tricky depending on how you set it up. You can either operate as a sole proprietor or create a corporation. Each choice affects your taxes differently.

As a sole proprietor, you report your side business income along with your regular job salary on your personal tax return (Form T2125). That means the profit is taxed at your personal tax rate. If your side business loses money, those losses can reduce the tax on your other income.

Starting a corporation separates your side business income from your personal earnings. The corporation files its own tax return (T2) and pays corporate taxes on profits. When you take money out as salary or dividends, you pay personal tax on that amount separately. Any losses stay with the company and can’t reduce your employment income.

Incorporation comes with extra paperwork like annual filings and bookkeeping rules. You may also need payroll records if you pay yourself wages. One benefit is you might defer some taxes by keeping earnings inside the corporation, but that doesn’t mean you save money forever.

Personal Tax Rates Compared to Corporate Small Business Tax Rates
Knowing how personal and corporate tax rates compare helps decide if incorporating makes sense for your side hustle in Ontario.

Ontario’s top combined federal and provincial personal tax rate hits about 53.53%. This kicks in when income goes over roughly $235,675 per year.

On the other hand, Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) get a small business deduction (SBD). This lowers tax on active business income up to $500,000 each year.

Here’s how rates stack up for 2026:

Combined federal & Ontario SBD rate: 12.2% on first $500K active CCPC business income
General corporate rate above $500K: about 26.5%
Top marginal personal income rate: 53.53% over high-income levels
Because corporate rates are lower, you might hold profits in the company to pay less tax now. But this depends on things like when and how you take money out.

Benefits and Limitations of the Small Business Deduction for Incorporated Side Businesses
The small business deduction cuts federal and provincial taxes on active business income up to $500,000 for CCPCs in Ontario.

Benefits:
Cuts combined corporate tax from around 26–27% down to about 12%
Lets you keep more after-tax profits inside the corporation
Helps reinvest or delay taking money personally
Limitations:
Only applies if your business is “active” – no passive or investment income
Associated corporations share one $500K limit together

About GST/HST:

If your revenue passes CAD$30,000 over four straight quarters (including related parties), you must register for GST/HST even if incorporated. Below that, registration is optional but brings filing rules.

Understanding Personal Services Business Rules and Their Effect on Taxation
Personal Services Businesses (PSBs) happen when an incorporated company works like an employee under contract with one client — often controlled by that client like a boss would be.

If you start a corporation but basically do employee-like work for one client:

Your company might be called a PSB by tax authorities
PSBs don’t get the small business deduction; they pay full corporate rates (~26%)
They also face limits on what expenses they can deduct
What raises PSB risk? Think:

Working for just one client who controls how/when/where you work
Client provides tools or equipment instead of you having them yourself
Your work looks like traditional employment
If your side client seems close to an employer, check with a CPA before incorporating. Wrong classification means higher taxes later.

Financial Considerations When Transitioning from Sole Proprietorship to Corporation
Losses affect taxes differently in each setup — this matters when switching structures:

It helps to plan carefully if waiting until steady profits show before incorporating works better financially than switching too soon with unused losses.

Comparing Reporting Side Business Income Personally Versus Through a Corporation
When you have a side business while employed in Ontario, you can report income personally or through a corporation. Both ways have different tax and compliance effects.

If you run your side business as a sole proprietor, you report income on Form T2125 with your personal T1 tax return. Your business profit adds to your salary and is taxed at your regular personal rate. So, the money flows straight to you without any corporate layer.

If you start a corporation while employed, the company files its own T2 tax return. The corporation pays taxes on profits at corporate rates. You might keep some earnings inside the company to delay taxes. But any salary or dividends paid to you are taxed personally.

Here are key points about tax implications when incorporating a side business:
Corporate earnings can be kept inside to delay personal taxes.
Corporations file separate returns from your personal taxes.
Incorporation brings more paperwork like bookkeeping and payroll.
Your salary plus dividends increase your personal taxable income.
Choosing how to report depends on profits, cash needs, risk, and how much admin work you want. Both ways have pros and cons around tax timing and complexity.

Handling Start-Up Losses, Home Office, and Vehicle Expenses for Tax Purposes
Start-up losses happen often with new businesses. How you handle them depends on whether you’re incorporated or not.

As a sole proprietor using Form T2125 on your T1 return, losses can reduce other income like your job wages right away.[1] This lowers the taxes due in that year.

Corporations keep losses inside the company. These can only offset future profits within the corporation.[2] You can’t use these losses against your personal employment income outside the company.

For home office expenses when incorporating a side business in Ontario:
Sole proprietors claim parts of utilities, rent (or mortgage interest), and property taxes based on workspace size.
Corporations reimburse employees for home office costs through accountable plans; otherwise claiming those expenses needs good records.[3]
Vehicle costs need careful logs showing how much is for business versus personal use.[4] Deductible amounts depend on kilometres driven strictly for work related to either sole proprietorship or incorporated side hustle.

Do you need a GST/HST number for your side business? If combined revenues go over $30,000 in four straight calendar quarters across all businesses—even incorporated ones—you must register.[5] This affects collecting GST/HST and input credit claims.

Non-disclosure to employers may breach contracts leading to job loss or legal issues. To prevent these: maintain clear records separating personal and corporate transactions; consult experts before incorporating; comply with all CRA rules timely; review employment contracts carefully before proceeding.

What Should You Prepare Before Meeting a CPA?
Bring copies of your employment contract highlighting moonlighting or conflict clauses. Have financial details ready: expected side income, expenses forecasts, existing losses from sole proprietorship if any. Prepare questions about CPP contributions impact and pay structure preferences (salary/dividends). Gather information on clients you serve to assess PSB risk properly.

How Do Side Businesses Differ Across the 10 Industries We Serve?

Glossary
PSB (Personal Services Business): A corporation that resembles employment relationships triggering higher taxes.
NUANS: A report that ensures your chosen corporate name is unique across Canada.
T2125: Form used by sole proprietors to report business income.
T2 Return: Corporate income tax return filed annually.
SBD (Small Business Deduction): Tax reduction benefit available only to active CCPCs under certain conditions.
GST/HST Small Supplier Threshold: Revenue level above which mandatory GST/HST registration applies ($30K).
