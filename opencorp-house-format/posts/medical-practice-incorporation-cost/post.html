What Incorporation Means for Medical Practices in Canada

1

What Incorporation Means

The Basics

In Canada, incorporating a medical practice means creating a legal company separate from yourself. This is called doctor incorporation. When you do this, you run your practice through a physician professional corporation (PPC). The cost to incorporate a medical practice changes depending on the province. You pay fees for registration, permits, and keeping up with rules.

Understanding Physician Professional Corporations (PCs) and Their Legal Structure

A physician professional corporation is made just for licensed healthcare workers. It offers perks like limited liability, which means less personal risk. Also, it can save you money on taxes and lets you keep profits inside the company.

Each province sets its own rules about how PCs work. These rules cover what steps you must take to incorporate and how much it costs.

The medical practice incorporation cost by province usually includes fees to set up at government offices and yearly charges from medical colleges or regulators.

Differences Between Individual Practice and Professional Corporation

Key Stat

$725: first-year government and college fees for an Ontario medical professional corporation: the CPSO certificate at $400, the Ontario Business Registry at $300 and a $25 NUANS report.

Ownership, Shareholder Roles, and Legal Responsibilities in Medical PCs

Physician professional corporations usually have shareholders who are doctors themselves. These shareholders have roles with legal duties under both corporate law and health profession rules:

  • Voting shareholders must be licensed doctors.
  • Directors run daily business activities.
  • Officers handle admin tasks; in Ontario every officer and director must also be a physician shareholder.

Doctors running PCs must also follow laws about healthcare practices and keep good financial records.

Worth Knowing

In Ontario, family members can hold non-voting shares of a medicine professional corporation, but dividends paid to them are usually caught by TOSI unless an exception applies, such as a spouse once the physician is 65 or older.

Why Physicians Consider Incorporation: Overview of Financial and Legal Motivations

Many doctors pick incorporation because it can save money on taxes. Here are some main reasons they think about it:

  • Liability Protection lowers their personal risk from lawsuits.
  • Income Splitting lets them share earnings with family members to reduce taxes.
  • Retirement Planning works better when profits stay inside the corporation.
  • Professional Image looks stronger when they operate as a business entity.

So, understanding what incorporation means helps explain why many Canadian physicians choose this path even though costs differ by province. It gives them more control over money while protecting their personal assets against risks tied to medicine.

Advantages of Medical Practice Incorporation: Tax Deferral, Income Splitting, and Liability Protection

Advantages and Drawbacks

Pros and Cons

Incorporating a medical practice in Canada gives doctors some useful perks. A physician professional corporation lets you keep money inside the company. This means you pay lower corporate tax rates now and delay personal taxes until you take the money out. It’s not a permanent saving but a way to control when you pay tax.

You can also share income with family members who own non-voting shares. This may lower your family’s total tax bill. But watch out: each province’s medical college has its own rules, and TOSI (Tax on Split Income) laws apply too. So, it’s smart to ask an accountant before setting this up.

The other big plus is liability protection. Incorporation won’t stop malpractice claims — those still come at you personally. But it does protect your business from debts or contracts that aren’t related to patient care.

Keep in mind, costs vary by province. Things like college permits, filing fees, NUANS reports, and yearly upkeep add up. Knowing these expenses helps doctors plan for doctor incorporation Canada-wide.

Key advantages include:

  • Tax deferral through lower corporate tax rates
  • Possible income splitting with family members
  • Protection from business debts (not malpractice claims)
  • Clear understanding of incorporation costs per province

Potential Disadvantages: Administrative Burden, Costs, and Restrictions on Funds Retention

Setting up a medical corporation costs money—fees vary by province. You pay once to set it up, then again every year for renewals and filings. These extra steps make running your practice more complex than being unincorporated.

You also have to manage bookkeeping, payroll slips (T4/T5), file corporate taxes (T2), and keep proper minutes. Missing deadlines can lead to penalties or loss of good standing with regulators.

Money kept inside the corporation can’t be used freely without causing personal tax events. That limits how easily you can access your cash compared to getting paid directly as salary or dividends.

If your corporation makes over $50,000 in passive investment income each year, extra taxes kick in (“passive income grind”). That reduces some benefits of holding onto earnings inside the company.

Costs and rules differ across provinces because medical colleges and government offices set their own fees. Doctors should think about these challenges when deciding if incorporating makes sense.

Main downsides include:

  • Setup and annual fees that vary by location
  • Extra paperwork and reporting duties
  • Limits on withdrawing retained funds without tax hits
  • Higher taxes on passive income inside the corporation

Setting Up Step by Step

Setup

Setting up a physician professional corporation (PC) in Canada takes a few key steps. The cost to incorporate medical practice varies by province. Knowing these steps helps doctors handle the process and follow the rules.

Choosing a Name and Business Structure

Picking a good name is the first step in doctor incorporation Canada. The name must be unique and meet your province's medical college rules. You’ll need to do a NUANS Name Search. This search checks if the name is already taken.

The NUANS Report comes from this search and shows if your name is free to use. It also holds the name while you file papers. The cost for this report can range from about $13 to $75, depending on where you are 1.

You also need to choose the right business structure before filing your articles of incorporation.

Filing Articles of Incorporation and Obtaining Provincial Approval

To officially create your PC, you file articles of incorporation with your provincial registry:

  • Ontario: Ontario Business Registry charges $300 for this 2.
  • British Columbia: BC Registries fee is $350 3.
  • Alberta: Alberta Corporate Registry charges $275 per application 4.

These fees cover government processing only. Extra fees like legal help or accounting aren’t included.

After filing, your provincial medical college must approve before you start working under the PC.

Financial Insights and Cost Breakdown by Province

Cost Breakdown by Province

Costs

Transparent Overview of Incorporation Costs Across Canadian Provinces

The cost to incorporate a medical practice in Canada changes a lot from province to province. Different provinces have their own medical college rules, registry fees, and paperwork costs. Doctor incorporation Canada means you must pay for permits or certificates from your provincial medical college. You also need to pay government registry filing fees and get a NUANS name search report. These fees usually happen once at the start but some require yearly payments.

For example, Ontario doctors pay a certificate fee to the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO). They also pay the Ontario Business Registry to incorporate. In British Columbia, the College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC (CPSBC) charges permit fees plus corporate filing fees from BC Registries. Alberta’s Health Professions Act needs CPSA permits with extra registry costs.

Each province sets its own fees based on local rules. It helps to know these differences when you plan how much it costs to set up a physician professional corporation in each province.

Initial Registration Fees, Annual Maintenance Costs, and Other Administrative Expenses

Setting up a physician professional corporation has several key costs:

  • Initial Registration Fees: These include permit or certificate fees from the provincial medical college plus filing articles with the business registry.
  • Annual Renewal Fees: Most provinces want yearly payments to keep your corporation’s authorization active.
  • NUANS Report Fee: This name search report, required in Ontario, Alberta and federally, checks if your chosen name is unique. It usually lasts for 90 days.
  • Corporate Annual Return Filing: Registries like Ontario’s Business Registry ask for this yearly.
  • Other Admin Costs: You might need services like minute book upkeep, registered office handling, bookkeeping help, or payroll if you hire staff.

All these make up the basic yearly expenses for running your corporation legally after setup.

Tax Considerations Unique to Physician PCs: Deferrals, Passive Income Rules, and TOSI Screening

Physician professional corporations offer ways to plan taxes but come with complex rules:

  • The small business deduction applies federally on up to $500,000 of active business income. You can keep income inside the corporation to defer personal taxes—but not avoid them.
  • If passive investment income goes above $50,000, you lose some of this deduction (“passive income grind”). This raises tax rates on passive money inside your PC.
  • Tax on Split Income (TOSI) rules kick in if family members own shares but don’t meet certain exceptions. You must carefully check before paying dividends to shareholders who aren’t the doctor directly.

These tax rules shape how much benefit you get from incorporating instead of taking all income personally. A CPA should review your situation using current CRA rules.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: Please keep personal and corporate money apart. Personal costs paid from the corporation without being recorded as salary, dividends or a shareholder loan can be taxed as a benefit under the Income Tax Act.

Remuneration Strategies for Physicians: Salary, Dividends, and Bonuses Explained

Doctors working through professional corporations use different pay methods:

  • Salary Payments give RRSP contribution room but increase payroll taxes like CPP contributions.
  • Dividends let you distribute money more flexibly and often pay less tax than salary. But they might cause TOSI issues if given beyond allowed shareholders.
  • Bonuses are handy near year-end for managing taxable income or splitting income within legal limits.

No one way fits all cases—talk to CPAs who know tax details for physician professional corporations and provincial shareholding laws.

Eligibility and Support

Eligibility

Physicians who want to incorporate often wonder about eligibility, naming rules, shareholders, and renewals. Each province’s medical college has its own set of rules.

Eligibility: Only licensed doctors in good standing can incorporate. If you're an International Medical Graduate (IMG), you must be licensed first. Incorporation doesn’t replace or grant your license — that’s up to the college.

Naming: Your corporation’s name must follow the college’s rules. Avoid using protected words like “doctor” or “medical” without approval. For example, the CPSO checks the name during their Certificate of Authorization process.

Shareholders: In many provinces, family members can hold non-voting shares if conditions are met (see Ontario Business Corporations Act s. 3.2). But share setups need a legal review to avoid tax problems like TOSI (Tax on Split Income).

Renewal: You have to renew your permits yearly with your regulatory body and file annual returns with corporate registries. Missing renewal deadlines can cause permit suspensions and stop you from billing through your corporation.

Knowing these points helps when budgeting for the cost to incorporate a medical practice across Canada.

At a Glance
  • CPSO certificate of authorization: $400 new, $175 each year
  • CPSBC corporation permit: $350 new, $135 each year
  • CPSA professional corporation: $500 plus GST new, $200 plus GST each year per physician shareholder
  • Ontario Business Registry: $300 to incorporate
  • BC Registries: $350 to incorporate, plus a $30 Name Request
  • Alberta registry: $275 government fee
  • Small business deduction: First $500,000 of active business income
  • Passive income threshold: $50,000 a year
  • Our fee: $35 including HST, plus government and college fees

Who This Is For / Not For

Fit Check
  • For: Licensed physicians in Ontario, British Columbia or Alberta who earn more than they spend and want to budget the setup and yearly cost of a professional corporation.
  • Not For: Medical students and residents without an independent licence, and physicians in Quebec, who follow Collège des médecins du Québec rules not covered in depth here.
Verdict

The fees are modest: $725 in Ontario, $730 in British Columbia and $800 in Alberta in the first year, then $135 to $200 plus GST a year for the college. The real question is income. Incorporation pays when you can leave money in the corporation; if you spend everything you bill, the yearly T2 and bookkeeping cost more than the deferral saves.

2026 Update

2026 Update — what is current: This article reflects fees and rules current to November 2026. The CPSO charges $400 for a new certificate of authorization and $175 to renew. The CPSBC charges $350 for a new permit and $135 a year. The CPSA charges $500 plus GST to register and $200 plus GST a year per physician shareholder. Ontario charges $300 to incorporate, BC $350 and Alberta $275. The small business deduction still covers $500,000 of active business income and is ground down once passive income passes $50,000.
