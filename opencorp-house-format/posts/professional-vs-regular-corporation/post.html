What is a Professional Corporation?

1

What a Professional Corporation Is

The Basics

Definition and Purpose

A professional corporation (PC) in Canada is a type of business set up for regulated professionals like doctors, lawyers, dentists, accountants, and engineers. It lets them practice their trade while limiting their personal liability. In Ontario, you must get a certificate of authorization from the right regulatory body to incorporate as a PC. This step makes sure only qualified people can use this structure.

Who Can Incorporate as a Professional Corporation?

Not every business can form a professional corporation in Ontario. Only certain regulated professionals who meet their regulator’s rules qualify. For example:

  • Regulated professions include medicine (CPSO), dentistry (RCDSO), law (LSO), and accounting (CPA Ontario). Engineers (PEO) practise through a regular corporation that holds a PEO certificate of authorization instead.
  • To form a PC in Canada, you need to:
    • Include “Professional Corporation” in the company name.
    • Have shareholders all from the same profession.
    • Get a certificate of authorization from your profession’s regulator.

Knowing these points helps explain why some jobs must use professional corporations instead of regular business entities.

Worth Knowing

Ontario engineers cannot form a professional corporation. An engineering business uses a regular corporation and gets a certificate of authorization from PEO, which means the shareholder limits and family share rules in this article do not apply to it.

What is a Regular (Standard) Corporation?

2

What a Regular Corporation Is

Standard

General Business Corporation Overview

A regular corporation is any typical business formed under provincial or federal laws that isn’t part of regulated professions. Unlike PCs, these corporations have fewer rules about ownership and management. They offer more freedom to run the company as you see fit.

In Ontario, starting a regular corporation involves paying registration fees to the government. You’ll also file an annual return, which has no government fee, to keep it active. These costs vary but usually cost less than forming special entities like PCs.

Industries and Professions Covered

Regular corporations work in many fields that don’t need tight regulation. Examples include:

  • Retail stores
  • Tech companies
  • Construction firms
  • Hospitality services

Basically, any legal business without strict licensing fits here.

Unlike professional corporations that some occupations must use, regular corporations suit many other sectors well. Still, knowing your industry’s rules helps you pick the best corporate type for your business across Canada’s wide economy.

Key Differences Between Professional Corporations and Regular Corporations

Distinct Legal Structures

A professional corporation in Canada exists for licensed professionals like doctors, lawyers, dentists, engineers, and accountants. The big difference between a professional corporation vs regular corporation is how they're regulated. A professional corporation must follow corporate law and rules set by their professional body. Regular corporations have more freedom. They can have any owner and do a wider range of business activities without strict rules. Choosing between a regular corporation vs professional corporation usually depends on the requirements of the profession’s regulator.

Limited Liability Protections

5

Liability Protection

Liability

Professional corporations and regular corporations are quite different when it comes to liability protections in Canada. A professional corporation Canada must be owned and run by members of a regulated profession. It has to follow strict professional corporation requirements Canada, like those under the Business Corporations Act (Ontario) ss. 3.1–3.4.

Regular corporations usually protect shareholders from business debts and liabilities. But with professional corporation vs regular corporation, the rules differ. Professional negligence isn’t covered by the corporation’s protection. So, while a professional corporation protects personal assets from general business risks, it doesn’t cover claims about malpractice or errors in professional work.

On the other hand, a regular corporation vs professional corporation gives broader limited liability for shareholders. Regular corporations can run any lawful business without limits on who owns them or what they’re called. But you can’t use a regular corporation if the law says you have to form a professional one.

In short, both types protect against most business risks but only regular corporations protect fully except for some specific exceptions.

What Corporations Protect Against

Corporations mainly protect owners from financial risks tied to company debts—not personal wrongdoing or breaking rules.

When comparing professional corporation vs regular corporation:

  • Both stop creditors from going after personal assets for company debts like loans and leases.
  • Neither type shields professionals from malpractice or negligence claims.
  • Professional corporations face more oversight by regulators who enforce extra standards.

Regular corporations protect mainly against creditor claims related to normal business operations. They don’t have special shareholder limits or need licensing certificates.

So, whether you choose either type, your risk for losses drops—but you stay responsible for your own professional actions.

Source: OBCA ss. 3.1–3.4; Regulated Health Professions Act; Income Tax Act

Personal Professional Liability

Even if you run your business as a professional corporation Canada, you stay personally liable for your own actions.

That’s because provincial colleges or licensing bodies like CPA Ontario or CPSO want professionals to keep full accountability. Incorporation helps with tax and admin stuff but does not change that rule.

You can be held personally responsible for:

  • Negligent acts
  • Ethical violations
  • Regulatory penalties against you as an individual

The company might have insurance—for example, LawPRO for lawyers or CMPA for doctors—that covers some risks. But this doesn’t shift blame from the professional when they make mistakes.

So forming a professional corporation Canada meets legal rules but doesn’t remove personal responsibility set by each profession’s regulations.

Malpractice and Negligence Issues

Malpractice claims aren’t covered by incorporation in Canada—whether it’s a professional or regular corporation.

That means:

  • Setting up a professional corporation doesn’t stop lawsuits for medical errors, legal mistakes, accounting faults, or engineering problems.
  • Regulators require insurance that covers these risks alongside yearly certificates of authorization per profession.

For example:

Doctors incorporated under Ontario’s Medicine Act must keep CMPA membership to cover clinical negligence even after forming their professional corporation. Similarly,

Lawyers governed by Law Society rules need LawPRO insurance no matter if they work via regular or professional corporations [Regulated Health Professions Act].

These rules make sure clients get protection no matter how providers organize their business. And professionals get tax benefits through properly formed entities following all professional corporation requirements Canada.

Small Business Deduction and Passive Income

6

Small Business Deduction and Passive Income

Tax

Professional corporations and regular corporations in Canada share many tax rules. Both can qualify for the small business deduction (SBD). But professional corporation requirements Canada add some specific rules that affect how taxes apply.

Eligibility for the Small Business Deduction

Both types of corporations may be Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs). That status lets them claim the federal small business deduction under section 125 of the Income Tax Act. This deduction lowers the tax rate on active business income, up to $500,000 per year federally and in Ontario.[1][2]

To qualify, a corporation must meet:

  • CCPC status: It has to be privately controlled by Canadians.
  • Active business income: Income must come from an active Canadian business.
  • Professional corporation requirements Canada: Regulators decide if a profession must form a professional corporation. This doesn't affect SBD eligibility but sets rules for naming, shares, and allowed activities under OBCA ss. 3.1–3.4.

Both professional and regular corporations get the small business deduction if they meet CCPC rules.[3] The CRA decides eligibility based on facts like who controls the company.

Passive Income Rules and Impacts

Passive income inside a corporation affects the SBD limit. Since 2019,[4] CCPCs lose part or all of their $500,000 federal SBD when their adjusted aggregate investment income goes over $50,000.

Here’s what matters:

  • Both professional corporations vs regular corporations follow the same passive income rules.
  • Adjusted aggregate investment income includes things like interest, dividends (not from connected businesses), rental income unrelated to active work, and some royalties.
  • For each dollar over $50,000 in passive income, the SBD limit shrinks by $5. It disappears entirely at $150,000.[5]

Professionals using professional corporations need to watch passive income carefully. Too much passive earning cuts down on their low tax rates for active profits.

Key Stat

$150,000: the passive investment income at which a corporation loses its entire $500,000 small business limit. Every $1 of passive income above $50,000 removes $5 of the limit.

The main differences between regular versus professional corporations come from regulatory rules — not tax rates themselves.

Practical Implications

In Practice

Incorporating a professional practice in Canada isn’t like setting up a regular business. It follows different rules. You can’t just choose to be a professional corporation because you want to. Incorporating as Physicians, Dentists, Lawyers, CPAs, Engineers

If you’re a physician, dentist, lawyer, CPA, or engineer, you have to follow specific rules when incorporating. For example:

  • Only licensed members can own shares.
  • Ontario’s Business Corporations Act sections 3.1 to 3.4 sets some of these rules.
  • The name must include “Professional Corporation” or “société professionnelle”.

Each profession also has its own set of rules:

  • Physicians: Must follow the Medicine Act and get approval from the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO).
  • Dentists: Governed by the Dentistry Act.
  • Lawyers: Rules come from the Law Society of Ontario.
  • CPAs: Regulated by CPA Ontario.
  • Engineers: Follow Professional Engineers Ontario (PEO) guidelines and practise through a regular corporation holding a PEO certificate of authorization, since engineers cannot form a professional corporation in Ontario.

Sometimes family members can hold non-voting shares if allowed.

Updates, Retirements, Name Changes, and Death of a Shareholder

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

9

Professional Corporation FAQ

FAQ
What is the Certificate of Authorization fee for professional corporations in Ontario?+

The certificate of authorization fee typically ranges from $500 CAD initially, with annual renewal fees around $250 CAD. These fees vary by regulator and profession.

Are there corporation annual renewal fees for professional corporations?+

Yes, professional corporations pay annual renewal fees to their regulatory body; the Ontario annual return itself has no government fee. These ensure ongoing compliance and certification validity.

How does TOSI affect professional corporation shareholders?+

Tax on Split Income (TOSI) may apply to income paid to family members holding non-voting shares. It prevents unfair income splitting among family shareholders.

Which legislation governs professional corporations in Ontario?+

The Ontario Business Corporations Act sections 3.1–3.4 and the Regulated Health Professions Act 1991 primarily regulate professional corporations.

What is the role of CMPA and LawPRO in professional corporations?+

CMPA covers malpractice insurance for physicians, while LawPRO insures lawyers against professional negligence risks. Both are mandatory for compliance.

Can directors and officers be non-members in a professional corporation?+

Directors and officers must be licensed members of the regulated profession or permitted under specific regulator policies.

Do holding companies have restrictions in professional corporations?+

Yes. In Ontario, every share of a professional corporation must be owned by a licensed member, or by a permitted family member for physicians and dentists, so a holding company cannot own shares; other provinces differ.

How do passive investment income thresholds impact small business deductions?+

Passive income above $50,000 reduces the small business deduction limit gradually until it phases out at $150,000.

What are NUANS naming rules for professional corporations?+

Names must include "Professional Corporation" or "société professionnelle", follow the regulator's naming rules, and be checked with a NUANS report before incorporation.

The small business limit remains $500,000, reduced by $5 for every $1 of passive income above $50,000 and eliminated at $150,000. Ontario's combined rates are 12.2% on income eligible for the small business deduction and 26.5% on other active business income. OBCA sections 3.1 to 3.4 still govern Ontario professional corporations: only licensed members, and for physicians and dentists their family members holding non-voting shares, may own shares, and the name must include "Professional Corporation" or "société professionnelle". Regulators set and change their own certificate of authorization fees, so please confirm the current fee with your college or law society before you apply.
