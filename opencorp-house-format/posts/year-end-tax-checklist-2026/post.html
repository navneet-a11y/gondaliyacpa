Understanding Your Business Year-End and Tax Deadlines in Canada

1

Year-End and Deadlines

Deadlines

Defining Your Corporation’s Fiscal Year-End

If you own an incorporated business, knowing your corporation’s fiscal year-end is really important. This date marks when your accounting period ends. It might not be the same as the calendar year. Most Canadian-controlled private corporations pick December 31 as their year-end. This choice matches personal tax deadlines, making things easier.

Your corporate year-end affects how you report income and expenses. Before that day comes, double-check your financial records. Make sure they are correct and ready. That way, switching to the next fiscal period goes smoothly.

Key CRA Deadlines for Filing and Payments by December 31, 2026 and Early 2027

You must know some important CRA deadlines to avoid penalties:

  • T2 Corporate Return Filing Deadline: File this six months after your fiscal year-end.
  • T4 Slip Submission Deadline: Due by February 28 after the tax year ends.
  • T5 Slip Submission Deadline: Also due February 28.
  • Final Payroll Remittances Due Date: Pay by January 15 after your corporate year ends.

Missing these can cost you extra fees or interest. So, mark these dates clearly on your calendar.

Key Stat

March 1, 2027: the due date for 2026 T4 and T5 slips and for 2026 RRSP contributions, because February 28, 2027 falls on a Sunday.

Overview of T2 Filing, T4 Slip Submission, and Annual Return Requirements

Here’s a quick look at what you need to file each year:

T2 Corporate Return

Must be sent in within six months of your fiscal year-end.

Shows all income during that period.

T4/T5 Slips

T4 reports employee income; T5 reports dividends paid out.

Both slips must reach CRA by February 28 yearly.

Ontario Business Registry Annual Return

Corporations in Ontario need to file this yearly with ServiceOntario to stay active.

Corporate Instalments Due Dates

You may have to pay instalments based on last year's taxable income.

These help spread out payments during the tax cycle.

Following these rules carefully keeps your business in good standing with federal laws for CCPCs in Ontario and across Canada.

Preparing and Organizing Financial Records for Year-End Tax Filing

2

Records and Bookkeeping

Records

Getting your financial records in order helps make corporate year-end tax filing easier. You need to gather documents, finish bookkeeping, and use accounting software. Doing these things right helps you follow Canadian tax rules without stress. This part covers important steps from the corporate year-end tax checklist 2026 that owners of incorporated businesses should know for December tax planning Canada.

Gathering Necessary Documentation and Financial Statements

First, collect all the papers you need. This step is key for year-end tax planning for incorporated business owners. Make sure you have:

  • Financial statements: Income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement up to December 31.
  • Bank statements: Every account your corporation used during the year.
  • Invoices and receipts: Both those you sent out and those you received.
  • Payroll records: Salary details, bonuses paid or owed before year-end, including CPP contributions.
  • Shareholder loan balances: Papers showing loans given or repaid as per ITA 15(2).
  • GST/HST filings: Past returns and any supporting schedules if needed.

Having all these ready fits well with a small business year-end tax checklist. It helps report income, expenses, assets, liabilities, payroll deductions, and instalments accurately on T2 returns.

Missing or wrong papers often cause delays or mistakes with CRA deadlines [1]. Business owners should check their files well before December 31 following the corporate year-end tax checklist 2026.

Completing Year-End Bookkeeping and Reconciliation Steps

Good bookkeeping is the base of honest financial reports at year-end. You should:

  • Match bank statements to your records to find mistakes
  • Check accounts receivable amounts to make sure debts are real
  • Count inventory to match what’s actually in stock
  • Review prepaid expenses and accruals for correct yearly cut-offs
  • Reconcile shareholder loans following ITA rules
  • Confirm payroll source deductions like CPP are correct through last payments

Doing this cuts down errors like wrong income or missed deductions that CRA might flag [2]. It also helps finish GST/HST returns that cover periods near December.

Finish these reconciliations early so you can fix problems before final reports go in. These reports are needed for federal T2 filing (due six months after December 31) [3] and Ontario Business Registry annual returns.

Worth Knowing

A dividend counts for the year it is paid, not the year it is declared. Please make sure the money actually leaves the corporate account by December 31 if you want the dividend on your 2026 return.

Using Accounting Software to Streamline Bookkeeping Tasks

Accounting software can make bookkeeping tasks much easier for December tax planning Canada. It can:

  • Bring in bank data automatically to avoid typing errors
  • Show income and expenses live to help predict profits
  • Calculate CPP contributions automatically from payroll info
  • Manage inventory so counts match physical checks
  • Create early financial reports for review

Popular tools like QuickBooks Online or Xero work well with CPA firms that help incorporated businesses [4]. Using software keeps books neat throughout the year instead of rushing at the end.

Still, software won’t replace a CPA’s review before decisions on bonuses or buying assets. Those affect capital cost allowance claims under 2026’s accelerated investment incentive rules [5].

Reviewing Income, Deductions, and Expenses Before Year-End

Income, Expenses and CCA

Deductions

If you run an incorporated business, you need to look closely at your income, deductions, and expenses before year-end. This is part of the corporate year-end tax checklist 2026. Doing this helps you report correctly and plan your taxes smartly. Checking carefully makes sure you don’t miss any eligible deductions and that all income gets counted before December 31.

Here’s why this matters:

  • It keeps your books accurate for your T2 return.
  • It prevents surprises when filing your taxes.
  • It spots missed opportunities for tax savings.

Assessing Corporate Income and Eligible Deductions

Start by checking all income earned during your fiscal year ending December 31, 2026. Make sure every sale or service is recorded properly. That means confirming invoices went out and payments came in or are expected. Getting this right avoids mistakes on your tax return.

Next, review what expenses you can deduct. Common deductible costs include:

  • Employee salaries (including owner-managers)
  • Rent for office space
  • Utilities like electricity and internet
  • Office supplies
  • Professional fees (accountants, lawyers)
  • Advertising directly tied to business

Make sure you keep receipts for everything. The CRA wants proof if they ask.

Also, consider reserves like allowances for doubtful accounts if some money owed might not get paid by year-end. This shows realistic income without overstating losses.

Watch out for non-deductible stuff such as personal expenses charged through the corporation or capital items wrongly claimed as regular expenses. These can cause penalties or reassessments later.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: Please do not buy assets in December only for the deduction. The asset must be available for use by December 31, and a purchase the business does not need still costs more than the tax it saves.

Timing Purchases and Capital Asset Acquisitions for Tax Benefits

December tax planning Canada often means timing buys carefully. When buying equipment or assets before year-end:

  • The item must be mainly for business use.
  • It needs to be ready to use by December 31 to count for 2026 depreciation.
  • Avoid buying just to get a deduction; the purchase should fit real business needs.

Getting necessary equipment before year-end lets you start claiming Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) sooner.

You might also prepay some operating costs like insurance covering periods after December 31. Under ITA section 18(9), the part that covers periods after year-end is generally not deductible right away. The CRA wants you to spread the deduction over the years the coverage relates to.

Applying the Capital Cost Allowance, Half-Year Rule, and Accelerated Investment Incentive in 2026

The Capital Cost Allowance system lets companies write off part of their capital asset costs each year. CRA sets classes with specific rates for depreciation.

Keep these in mind when claiming CCA:

  • The half-year rule limits CCA claims in the first year of an asset by letting you claim only half the usual amount.
  • Assets bought late in the year usually fall under this rule unless exceptions apply.

For assets put into use in 2026 qualifying under accelerated investment incentive rules:

  • You may claim higher first-year CCA rates—up to three times the normal first-year amount—on eligible property acquired after 2024, under the incentive first introduced for property acquired after November 20, 2018 and reinstated by the Budget 2025 measures. A September 2026 proposal would allow immediate expensing of most property acquired on or after September 15, 2026, so please confirm the current status before you buy.
  • This incentive encourages businesses to invest promptly but assets must meet CRA rules exactly.

Make sure you keep clear records of purchase dates and when assets were ready by December 31. Mistakes here could lead to denied claims during an audit.

Salary, Dividends, and Bonus Planning for Incorporated Business Owners

4

Salary, Dividends and Bonus

Pay Yourself

If you own an incorporated business, you need to check your salary, dividends, and bonuses before the year ends. This is part of your corporate year-end tax checklist 2026. These decisions affect both your company's taxes and your personal income taxes. Year-end tax planning for incorporated business owners means knowing the rules about timing and what the law requires. It’s not a sure way to save money but helps avoid surprises.

Evaluating Salary vs Dividends to Optimize Tax Outcomes

Paying yourself a salary costs your corporation money, but it lowers its taxable income. You must report that salary as personal income. Also, a salary adds to your RRSP contribution room because it counts as earned income. Dividends come from profits after tax. They don’t create RRSP room but may give you some dividend tax credits.

What matters most is when you pay these amounts before December 31. Salaries or bonuses must be declared by then and paid in a specific time frame (see next section). Dividends have to be actually paid by December 31 to count for that year’s taxes; otherwise, they count for next year.

You should think about:

  • How much cash you have on hand
  • CPP contributions that come with salaries
  • If family shareholders are involved and TOSI rules apply (explained later)
  • Your company’s profit level

Accrued Bonus Rules and Payment Deadlines for Deductibility

If you want to pay yourself a bonus from your corporation, there are rules you have to follow. The bonus must be declared by December 31, 2026. Then you have 180 days after year-end—until June 29, 2027—to actually pay it. If you miss that payment window, the bonus won’t reduce your company’s taxable income for 2026.

This rule comes from ITA subsection 78(4). It makes sure bonuses are real payments, not just numbers on paper at the end of the year.

Remember: when you pay a bonus through payroll, you must also deduct Canada Pension Plan (CPP) amounts and other required deductions on time.

Don’t forget about filing T4 slips reporting these payments:

  • Deadline is the last day of February of next year (March 1, 2027 for 2026 slips, because February 28 falls on a Sunday).
  • Electronic filing is mandatory if you file more than five slips.
  • Missing deadlines can cause penalties or interest from CRA.

To recap:

  • Declare bonuses before December 31.
  • Pay bonuses by June 29, 2027.
  • Submit payroll deductions quickly.
  • File T4 slips on time.

Wondering if paying yourself a salary or bonus is better? Get advice early—deferring payments doesn’t always mean permanent savings or CRA approval.

The year-end item we fix most often is a bonus booked at December 31 and never paid. If it is not paid within 180 days, the deduction moves to the year it is paid, so we diarize the June 29, 2027 payment date when we prepare the resolution.

Understanding TOSI Screening and Shareholder Loan Implications

TOSI (Tax on Split Income) rules affect family shareholders getting dividends from private companies. If family members receive dividends but don’t meet certain tests—like being actively involved in the business or passing age limits—they might get hit with higher taxes on those dividends.

Before paying dividends near year-end, check if they pass TOSI rules under your small business setup in this corporate year-end tax checklist 2026.

Also watch out for shareholder loans. Under ITA section 15(2), if you borrow money from your company, you generally need to repay it by the end of the corporation's next taxation year—December 31, 2027 for a loan made in a 2026 calendar year. If not repaid, the loan may count as income and get taxed personally.

Keep good records showing loan advances and repayments during the year. This helps avoid surprise tax bills on “benefits” instead of deferring funds inside the corporation.

Managing Payroll, GST/HST, and Instalment Payments

Payroll, GST/HST and Instalments

Payments

Handling payroll, GST/HST, and corporate instalments is a must for business owners as part of the corporate year-end tax checklist 2026. Staying on top of these payments helps avoid penalties and interest. Plus, it makes year-end tax planning in Canada much smoother.

Ensuring Accurate Payroll Source Deductions and CPP Contributions

Payroll source deductions cover income tax, Employment Insurance (EI) premiums, and Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions. If your corporation pays salaries or bonuses before December 31, you must send these deductions by the last payroll remittance deadline.

Final payroll remittance for 2026 is due January 15, 2027.

This includes all pay periods ending in December.

Missing this date means penalties from CRA.

CPP earnings limits changed in 2026. Employees and employers each contribute 5.95% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, plus 4% CPP2 on earnings from $74,600 to $85,000. Owner-managers need to check their payroll reflects these new rates.

Bonuses declared but unpaid by December 31 need special care. The tax law (ITA section 78(4)) says you can’t claim the bonus expense unless it’s paid within 180 days after year-end. It’s smart to get a CPA to review this timing.

Final GST/HST Remittance and Reporting Obligations

If your company is registered for GST/HST, you must file your last return for the period including December 31. The due date depends on your filing schedule:

  • Monthly filers: return due by January 31 after month-end.
  • Quarterly filers: return due by January 31 after the October-to-December quarter.

Make sure you report all sales with GST/HST correctly. Also include input tax credits claimed during this time. Mistakes here can delay refunds or invite audits.

Small businesses using Quick Method Accounting should confirm they still qualify at year-end with proper records.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: If you pay yourself through payroll, please remit December's source deductions by January 15, 2027. Late remittances attract a penalty of 3% to 10% of the amount plus interest, even when the tax is paid in full later.

Reviewing Corporate Instalments Due Dates and Amounts

Corporate instalments help spread out your tax payments based on what you estimate you owe during the year. For companies with a December year-end:

The CRA wants any unpaid balance of tax for the year paid by March 31 next year (three months after year-end for most CCPCs claiming the small business deduction, otherwise two months) to avoid interest charges.

Missing deadlines costs interest that piles up daily until full payment happens. This rate was 7% per year for the first quarter of 2026, and the CRA resets it every quarter.

Owner-managers should compare actual taxable income with instalments paid during their December tax planning in Canada. This way they can adjust future payments if needed.

Strategic Year-End Moves

Strategy

Monitoring Passive Income Levels and Small Business Deduction Impact

Passive income can affect your small business deduction limit. For 2026, if your Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) has over $50,000 in adjusted investment income, the $500,000 small business limit starts to shrink1. This means more passive income means less access to lower tax rates on active business profits.

Keep track of all passive income sources like interest, dividends from other corporations, and rent from inactive properties. Check how these affect your small business deduction before December 31. This step is important in any small business year-end tax checklist or corporate year-end tax checklist 2026.

Missing this can lead to paying higher corporate taxes at general rates instead of lower small business rates. Catching it early lets you talk with a CPA about legal ways to handle your situation without promises of savings.

Clearing Shareholder Loans and Updating Minute Book Resolutions

Shareholder loans still unpaid by year-end need close attention under ITA section 15(2). If you don’t repay them within one year after the fiscal year ends or document them properly, they might be taxed as personal income2.

Before December 31, review all shareholder loans and plan repayments if possible. Also update minute book resolutions to record decisions about bonuses, dividends, or loan dealings properly. This is required under the Ontario Business Corporations Act3. Good records help with annual filings like Ontario corporate annual returns.

Skipping these steps can cause audit problems or fines due to poor documentation or wrongly reported shareholder amounts.

Risk Warning

Risk Warning: A shareholder loan made in 2026 that is not repaid by December 31, 2027 is added to your 2026 income. Please do not repay it with a new loan just before the deadline; the CRA treats a series of loans and repayments as no repayment at all.

Considering RRSP Contribution Room, TFSA, FHSA, and Other Registered Accounts

Getting paid salary before December 31 creates RRSP contribution room based on CRA limits for each year4. In 2026:

  • The max RRSP limit is $33,810.
  • You can contribute until March 1 of next year for the previous year's room5.

Besides RRSPs (Registered Retirement Savings Plans), think about TFSAs (Tax-Free Savings Accounts) and FHSAs (First Home Savings Accounts) during December tax planning Canada. These accounts offer benefits but don’t change your RRSP room.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

7

Frequently Asked Questions

FAQ
What is the exact T2 Corporate Return filing deadline for 2026 year-end corporations?+

The T2 Corporate Return for corporations with a December 31, 2026 year-end is due by June 30, 2027. Late filings may face CRA penalties.

When must T4 and T5 slips be submitted to CRA?+

T4 and T5 slips must be filed with the CRA by March 1, 2027, for the 2026 tax year, because February 28 falls on a Sunday. Timely filing avoids penalties.

What is the payroll remittance deadline for December 2026 source deductions?+

The final payroll remittance for December 2026 is due on January 15, 2027. Missing this date can trigger CRA penalties.

How does ITA section 78(4) affect bonus payments at year-end?+

ITA section 78(4) requires bonuses to be declared by December 31 and paid within 180 days after year-end to be deductible.

What are the repayment rules under ITA section 15(2) for shareholder loans?+

Shareholder loans must be repaid within one year after the end of the corporation's taxation year in which they were made—December 31, 2027 for a loan made in a 2026 calendar year—or they become taxable income.

What is the CPP maximum contribution amount for 2026?+

The maximum CPP contribution for both employee and employer in 2026 is $4,230.45 each, plus up to $416 each of CPP2.

How does the Accelerated Investment Incentive benefit capital asset purchases?+

It allows claiming up to three times the normal first-year Capital Cost Allowance on qualifying assets acquired after 2024 under the reinstated incentive, first introduced for assets acquired after November 20, 2018.

What is the half-year rule regarding Capital Cost Allowance (CCA)?+

The half-year rule limits CCA claims in an asset’s first year to half the normal rate, except when accelerated incentives apply.

When is the Ontario Business Registry annual return due?+

Ontario incorporated businesses must file their annual return through the Ontario Business Registry every year, within six months after fiscal year-end and with no fee, to stay compliant.

What are penalties for late payroll and GST/HST remittances?+

CRA charges penalties of 3% to 10% on late payroll remittances, and 1% plus 0.25% a month for up to 12 months on late GST/HST returns, plus interest on both.

Worth Knowing

A corporation with a non-calendar year-end still runs payroll, T4 slips and RRSP room on the calendar year. Please keep both calendars in mind when you plan bonuses and dividends.
