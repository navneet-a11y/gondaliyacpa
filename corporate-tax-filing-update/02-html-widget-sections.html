1. Corporate Filing Deadlines: Your Annual Calendar

  • Key dates. For a December 31, 2026 year-end, we calendar the tax balance for February 28, 2027 (on time if received March 1) or March 31 under the three-month rule, T4 and T5 slips for March 1, the annual GST/HST return for March 31, personal returns for April 30, and the T2 and Ontario annual return for June 30.
  • Non-December year-ends. A corporation with a year-end other than December 31 runs on two clocks. Corporate deadlines follow the fiscal year, while payroll slips, T5 slips and the owners' personal returns follow the calendar year. We map both clocks, so the February slip deadlines that fall in the middle of a June 30 company's fiscal year are on its calendar.
  • Four CRA accounts. Each filing lands in a program account under one business number: RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll deductions and RZ for information returns such as T5 and T5018. We build the calendar account by account and check each account's statement, so every open account has its due dates and balance checked.
  • First-year set-up. A new corporation needs only the program accounts its activities require: RT once it is no longer a small supplier or chooses to register, and RP when it starts a payroll. We confirm each account, the GST/HST reporting period CRA has on file and the first year-end date, then calendar each first return.
  • Next year's calendar. Each engagement ends with a calendar for the next twelve months: the balance-due date, instalment dates where required, the GST/HST filing dates for your reporting period, the slip deadlines, the T2 date and the Ontario annual return. Every deadline for the filings in your engagement is listed in one place.

2. Year-End Bookkeeping Close Before Anything Is Filed

  • Monthly reconciliations. Every bank and credit card account is reconciled to its statements month by month. An unreconciled month can hide a duplicate entry, a missed deposit or a personal charge, any of which changes revenue, GST/HST or the shareholder loan. When only the books and statements are needed, our corporate year-end accounting service handles the close.
  • Year-end cut-off. A January invoice for December work belongs in December revenue on the T2, but the GST/HST on it follows the Excise Tax Act timing rules, generally the earlier of the invoice date and payment. We review January's sales and bills, move income and expenses into the right year and record each entry's GST/HST effect separately.
  • Accruals and prepaids. Accruals for professional fees, wages and interest, and prepaids such as insurance, are recorded so each expense falls in the year it relates to. A bonus accrued at year-end is deductible that year only if paid within 180 days, under subsection 78(4), so each accrual's payment date goes on the schedule we keep.
  • Inventory. For a corporation that holds inventory, the year-end count or perpetual records are agreed to the general ledger and to cost of sales. We review the valuation, generally the lower of cost and net realizable value under Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises (ASPE), because a change in closing inventory changes taxable income dollar for dollar.
  • Shareholder account. Owner withdrawals, personal costs paid by the company and owner deposits are posted to the shareholder account before T2 work starts. The year-end balance shows how much you must repay, or cover by salary or a dividend, by the subsection 15(2.6) deadline: one year after the end of the tax year in which the loan was made.

3. Year-End Financial Statements

  • Compilation (CSRS 4200). The T2 reports financial data as General Index of Financial Information (GIFI) codes, but lenders and investors may ask for financial statements. Where included in your engagement, we prepare the balance sheet, income statement and notes compiled under CSRS 4200, the standard that replaced the Notice to Reader for periods ending on or after December 14, 2021.
  • One locked trial balance. One locked trial balance produces the financial statements, the GIFI schedules filed with the T2 and the revenue used in the GST/HST reconciliation. We lock it only after every close adjustment is posted, and record any later entry in all three, so anyone comparing the filings finds the same figures.
  • Notes from source documents. The notes disclose the basis of accounting, amounts owed to and by shareholders, related-party transactions and long-term debt terms. Each disclosure has to agree with a source document, so we draft the notes from the loan agreements and the shareholder ledger, not from last year's notes.
  • Lender requirements. A lender or investor may require comparative figures, a set format, or a review or audit instead of a compilation. A review or audit is a different engagement, which we do not perform, so we confirm the requirement with you before year-end. Schedule 141 of the T2 reports the type of engagement actually performed.
  • Prior-year errors. Comparative figures are restated when last year's statements contained an error found during the close. We show the correction in the statements and decide whether the prior year's T2 also needs a reassessment request, so the T2 and the statements show the same figures.

4. Matching the T2 to Your GST/HST, T4 and T5 Filings

  • T2 preparation. Slips and an annual GST/HST return fall due months before the T2, so a difference found in T2 work can mean amending a filed slip or return. We file those amendments first, then e-file the T2, as most corporations must for tax years starting after 2023. Schedule-by-schedule detail is on our Corporate Tax Return Filing (T2) page.
  • Revenue vs GST/HST. Revenue reported on the T2 through GIFI is compared with the sales reported on the year's GST/HST returns. A gap is not always an error, since exempt and zero-rated sales and timing differences exist, but we document every difference before filing so it can be explained in one letter if CRA asks.
  • Wages vs T4s. Salaries deducted on the T2 can differ from the T4 Summary, because T4s report pay in the calendar year paid while the T2 deducts it in the fiscal year earned. We reconcile the two by pay date and list each reconciling item, such as a bonus accrued one year and paid the next.
  • Dividends vs T5s. Dividends on Schedule 3 of the T2 are matched to the T5s and resolutions, allowing for Schedule 3 using the fiscal year and T5s the calendar year. Once the T5 is filed, a dividend recorded without a T5, or a T5 for a dividend never recorded, is fixed by an amended T5 or a correcting entry.
  • Investment slips. Investment income on the T2 is matched to the T5 and T3 slips issued to the corporation, and securities sales on Schedule 6 to its T5008 slips. CRA receives those slips too, so we reconcile them to the general ledger instead of relying on the brokerage's annual summary alone.

5. GST/HST Returns and Year-End Reconciliation

  • Reporting period. The reporting period follows annual taxable supplies: annual filing up to $1.5 million, quarterly over $1.5 million up to $6 million, and monthly above $6 million, with the option to file more often. We confirm the period on file in My Business Account, and corporations that need only their returns filed can use our HST return filing service.
  • Due dates and instalments. Monthly and quarterly GST/HST returns are due one month after the period, and an annual return three months after year-end. An annual filer whose previous year's net tax was $3,000 or more generally owes quarterly instalments, unless this year's net tax falls below $3,000. We test both years from the filed returns and the current ledger.
  • ITC invoices. Input tax credits need proper invoices: the supplier's GST/HST registration number from $100 and, at $500 or more, the buyer's name, the terms of payment and a description of the supply. We review large purchase invoices before claiming, since CRA can deny an input tax credit that lacks the required invoice details.
  • Year-end reconciliation. The GST/HST payable account is reconciled at year-end to the RT statement of account, listing unfiled periods and payments in transit. We decide how each difference is fixed: a missed input tax credit can be claimed on a later return within the time limit, while under-reported tax is corrected by adjusting the original period's return.
  • Out-of-province sales. Under the general place-of-supply rule, services are taxed at the rate of the province of the customer's address, so an Ontario business charges 5% GST alone, not 13% HST, to an Alberta client. We review the customer list by province and confirm the rate on each invoice, checking sales of goods under their own rules.

6. Payroll Year-End: T4 Slips, PD7A Remittances and PIER

  • T4 slips. A T4 that leaves out taxable benefits understates employment income. We prepare the T4 slips and the T4 Summary from the payroll register and add the taxable benefits recorded in the general ledger, such as an automobile standby charge. More than five slips of one type must be filed electronically; filing 6 to 50 on paper costs $125.
  • T4s vs remittances. The T4 Summary totals for CPP, EI and income tax are reconciled to the payroll remittances (PD7A) made during the year. Any difference becomes a balance owing or a credit on the payroll account, so we find the cause, such as a missed pay period or a remittance posted to the wrong month, before filing.
  • PIER review. After T4s are filed, CRA's Pensionable and Insurable Earnings Review (PIER) checks the CPP and EI on each slip. A shortfall comes back as a PIER report with the amount owing and 30 days to reply; without a reply or payment by day 45, CRA assesses. We check deductions slip by slip before filing.
  • Owner EI status. Owner-managers are treated differently from other staff. An owner who controls more than 40% of the voting shares is generally not in insurable employment, so no EI is deducted, while CPP still applies to salary. We confirm each shareholder-employee's status rather than letting payroll software apply EI by default.
  • Remittance dates. Source deductions are due by the 15th of the month after payday for regular remitters, with earlier deadlines for larger employers. Late remittances cost 3% to 10% of the late amount over $500, depending on how late, so we compare remittance dates on the RP account with each pay period.

7. Dividends, T5 Slips and Shareholder Records

  • T5 amounts. Each T5 reports both the actual and the taxable amount of a dividend, in separate boxes for eligible and other than eligible dividends, and the corporation files a T5 Summary with its slips. We prepare both from the dividend register and the bank records, so each owner's return uses the taxable amount and the matching dividend tax credit.
  • Paid vs declared. A dividend is reported in the year it is paid, not the year it was declared. When a dividend is declared in December and paid in January, we report it on the following year's T5 and check that the T2 and the minute book record the same timing.
  • Eligible dividends. An eligible dividend needs written notice to each shareholder at or before payment under subsection 89(14), such as a letter or, where every shareholder is a director, a note in the minutes. A designation above the general rate income pool (GRIP) on Schedule 53 attracts 20% Part III.1 tax on the excess, so we check both first.
  • Share register. A share transfer during the year changes several records at once. We compare the share register and the directors' resolutions with the shareholders paid on the T5 slips and those listed on Schedule 50 of the T2, correcting the minute book first, so the minute book, the T5 slips and Schedule 50 agree.
  • Non-resident shareholders. Residence, not whether a shareholder has a SIN, decides how a dividend is reported. A dividend paid to a non-resident shareholder is subject to Part XIII withholding of 25% unless a treaty reduces it, and goes on an NR4 slip instead of a T5. We confirm residence before payment, so the right amount is withheld and remitted.

8. Contractor Slips: T4A and T5018

  • Contractors (T4A). Fees paid to self-employed contractors go in box 048 of a T4A when payments to one person total more than $500 for the year, and the T4A is due by the last day of February. Collecting each contractor's legal name, address and business number or SIN when the work starts means the slips are ready before the deadline.
  • Construction (T5018). A corporation whose primary business is construction reports payments of more than $500 to each subcontractor on T5018 slips. The return is due six months after the end of the reporting period, which can be the calendar year or the fiscal year. We pull the payments from the vendor ledger and exclude goods-only purchases.
  • Non-resident vendors. Payments to non-residents for services performed in Canada can require 15% withholding under Regulation 105, reported on a T4A-NR slip rather than a T4A. We review foreign vendors in the ledger each year, identify work actually performed in Canada and hand off the withholding and slips to our NR4, NR6 and withholding tax compliance service.
  • Late slip penalties. Late T4, T4A and T5 returns cost the greater of $100 and a daily amount for up to 100 days, $10 a day for 50 or fewer slips, up to $1,000; CRA is not currently assessing box 048 penalties except for certain trucking payments. We prepare those returns together and calendar each T5018 separately.
  • Slips vs expenses. Information returns are matched to the T2: contractor fees reported on T4A or T5018 slips should agree with subcontract and professional fee expenses in the general ledger. We reconcile the totals, so an expense deducted on the T2 is supported by the slips CRA already holds.

9. Ontario Filings: Annual Return and Employer Health Tax

  • Annual return. A missed annual return is an offence under sections 13 and 14 of the Corporations Information Act, and a corporation in default with unpaid fines or penalties cannot maintain an Ontario court action without leave. We check the Ontario Business Registry for each return, due within six months after year-end, and compare its directors with the minute book.
  • Dissolution risk. Ontario can dissolve a corporation that fails to file its annual returns, after a 90-day notice in the Ontario Gazette under subsection 241(3) of the Business Corporations Act. When returns have lapsed, we check the corporation's status on the registry before preparing other filings and tell you which returns are outstanding, or whether it shows as dissolved.
  • EHT exemption. Ontario Employer Health Tax applies to total Ontario remuneration, with the first $1 million exempt for eligible employers in 2026 and no exemption once an associated group's Ontario payroll is over $5 million. Associated employers share one exemption, so we confirm how it is allocated among the owners' companies before the annual EHT return is filed.
  • EHT return. Employers whose Ontario payroll exceeds the exemption file the annual EHT return by March 15, and monthly instalments apply once annual payroll is over $1.2 million. We reconcile EHT remuneration to the T4 totals and document each difference before either return is filed, since the two figures can legitimately differ.
  • WSIB. WSIB premiums in Ontario are calculated on insurable earnings, which are not the same as T4 employment income. We reconcile the payroll used for WSIB reporting to the T4 Summary, explaining each difference, so the year-end WSIB reconciliation and the T4 slips can be compared line by line.

10. Owners' Personal Returns

  • Filing dates. Where your engagement letter includes the owners' personal returns, we confirm each owner's dates. June 15 applies only when the owner, or a spouse or common-law partner, carried on an unincorporated business; an owner paid only salary and dividends files the 2026 return by April 30, 2027, and any balance is due April 30 either way.
  • Salary and dividends paid. Salary creates RRSP contribution room and CPP contributions, while dividends do not. We report the mix of salary and dividends actually paid, from the T4 and T5 slips, on each owner's return and in the corporate filings. Choosing next year's mix is a separate question, handled by our corporate tax planning service.
  • Shareholder loans. A shareholder loan that misses its repayment deadline is included in the owner's income for the year it was received, which can mean amending a filed personal return. A later repayment is deductible under paragraph 20(1)(j) in the year repaid, unless part of a series of loans and repayments. We track both years on the shareholder ledger.
  • Personal instalments. Personal instalments, due March 15, June 15, September 15 and December 15, apply when net tax owing is more than $3,000 in the current year and in either of the two previous years. Dividends have no tax withheld at source, so dividend income can cross that threshold; those dates go on the corporation's calendar.
  • Taxable benefits. A personal expense the corporation pays for an owner has three possible treatments: a debit to the shareholder account that the owner repays, a taxable employment benefit on the T4, or a shareholder benefit under subsection 15(1), taxable to the owner and generally not deductible to the corporation. We decide which applies during the close.

11. Tax Instalments and Payment Planning

  • Corporate instalments. A corporation whose tax payable for both the current and the previous year is more than $3,000 pays instalments, monthly by default or quarterly for a small Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) that qualifies. We combine them with any GST/HST instalments, and the owners' personal instalments where their returns are included, into one payment schedule.
  • Interest. Arrears interest on late taxes and short instalments is charged at CRA's prescribed rate for overdue taxes, which CRA resets every quarter: 7% for October 1 to December 31, 2026, compounded daily. Interest runs from each missed due date, so every instalment and balance-due date goes on your payment schedule.
  • Right account, right period. Each payment must reach the correct program account and period, whether it is paid through online banking, My Business Account or a pre-authorized debit. We give the exact account and period for every payment, because a payment applied to the wrong period can trigger interest even when the money was sent on time.
  • Credits and balances. For October 1 to December 31, 2026, CRA pays corporations 3% interest on overpayments, less than half its rate on overdue tax. A credit left on one program account while another carries a balance costs money, so we review all the corporation's accounts together and ask CRA to transfer the credit to the account that owes.
  • Year-end tax estimate. At year-end we estimate the corporation's tax for the year from the closing trial balance and compare it with the instalments already paid. When your year-end records reach us before the balance-due date, you get that estimate in time to plan the payment, instead of learning the balance when the T2 is filed.

12. My Business Account, Authorization and CRA Letters

  • Authorizing us. A corporation authorizes its accountant online, in My Business Account or by confirming a request submitted through Represent a Client, while Form AUT-01 by mail gives offline access only. We use the online methods wherever a director can sign in to My Business Account, and confirm which program accounts and access level each authorization covers.
  • CRA letters. A review letter's response deadline runs from the date on the letter, not from the day someone reads it. We confirm the corporation's notification settings in My Business Account, so you are alerted to new correspondence, and ask that every CRA letter, online or by post, be forwarded to us when it arrives.
  • CRA document requests. When CRA asks for documents to support a return we prepared, we answer with the specific records requested, organized by the line or schedule involved, and ask in writing for more time before the deadline when needed. A full audit moves to CRA audit support and representation for our tax clients.
  • Notices of assessment. GST/HST and payroll notices of assessment arrive separately from the T2 notice, and each can differ from the filed return. We compare every notice with the return for its program account and explain each difference. The 90-day objection period runs from the notice date, so a disputed item goes to our CRA objections and appeals service.
  • Address records. A corporation's address is held in two places: CRA's business number records and the Ontario Business Registry, which is updated separately. We update both after a move, together with direct deposit for business refunds in My Business Account, so demands to file, notices of assessment and refunds reach the corporation rather than a former office.

13. Switching Accountants, Including Mid-Year

  • Carryforward balances. A new engagement starts with last year's financial statements, the T2 with all schedules and the notice of assessment. We also take over the carryforward balances, namely non-capital losses, undepreciated capital cost by class, GRIP, the capital dividend account and refundable tax balances, and compare each with CRA's records.
  • Opening balances. Closing balances from the prior year must equal opening balances in the new year's books. We reconcile retained earnings, the shareholder account and GST/HST payable across the changeover, because an error in the opening balances otherwise carries into your current year's return.
  • Records and access. With your written authorization, we ask the previous accountant for copies of the prior year's records, the signed T183CORP for each e-filed T2, which the corporation keeps for six years after filing, and any working papers they agree to share. Once our authorization is in place, you can remove their access in My Business Account.
  • Mid-year switch. A mid-year switch starts from CRA's own records. We pull the RT and RP statements of account from My Business Account to see which GST/HST returns and payroll remittances the previous accountant has already filed for the year and what is outstanding, then pick up from the last filed period, so nothing is filed twice or missed.
  • Prior-year corrections. Errors found in prior years are corrected by a reassessment request where they affect tax, and noted in the file where they do not. A CCPC's normal reassessment period runs three years from the date its original notice of assessment was sent. We explain each correction, with its likely tax, interest or refund, before it is requested.

14. Late or Missed Corporate Filings and Penalties

  • Triage by program. For a corporation behind on several programs, we list each overdue T2, GST/HST return, remittance and slip return by account and period. Returns holding back refunds, and slip returns penalized even with no balance owing, go first; a late T2 with no unpaid tax has no late-filing penalty, but its refund is lost after three years.
  • Late GST/HST return. Overdue GST/HST returns are rebuilt from the sales and purchase records for each missed period, claiming each input tax credit backed by a proper invoice. The late-filing penalty is 1% of the amount owing plus 0.25% for each complete month, up to 12 months, and not filing after a CRA demand adds a further $250.
  • Payroll catch-up. Before late T4 slips are filed, we reconcile what was withheld, what was remitted and what was reported, so the corrected filings agree with each other. Late remittance penalties apply to the amount over $500, and a second failure in the same calendar year made knowingly or with gross negligence is charged at 20%.
  • Penalty relief. Penalties and interest on any program account, including GST/HST and payroll, can be covered by a taxpayer relief request on Form RC4288 or in My Business Account, within 10 years from the end of the calendar year or fiscal period concerned. Relief is at CRA's discretion, so we support each request with dated records by account and period.
  • Several years behind. Several years of unfiled returns are handled through our Catch-Up Corporate Tax Filing service. Where amounts were never reported and CRA has not yet contacted the corporation, a Voluntary Disclosures Program application may reduce penalties and interest under the program rules CRA revised on October 1, 2025. We decide which route fits before any return is filed.

What We Need From You

  • Bank and credit card statements for every corporate account for the full year, plus access to your accounting software, such as QuickBooks Online or Xero.
  • Sales invoices or revenue reports, and purchase invoices, especially for equipment, vehicles and any purchase of $500 or more.
  • The GST/HST returns filed during the year and the RT statement of account from My Business Account.
  • Payroll registers, PD7A remittance confirmations and the date any year-end bonus was paid.
  • Contractor names, addresses and business numbers or SINs, and the amounts paid to each.
  • Loan, line of credit and lease statements showing year-end balances.
  • Brokerage, investment and foreign account statements, and any T5, T3 or T5008 slips issued to the corporation.
  • Minute book records for the year: the current share register, the signed resolution for each dividend and bonus, and any share transfer or change of directors.
  • Last year's financial statements, T2, notice of assessment and any CRA letters received during the year.
  • For owners' personal returns, where included in your engagement letter: each owner's slips, RRSP receipts and last year's notice of assessment.
  • If the corporation holds inventory, the year-end count or inventory report; if it owns or leases vehicles, the logbook of business and personal kilometres for the year.
  • WSIB and Employer Health Tax statements for the year, and the confirmation of your last Ontario annual return from the Ontario Business Registry.
