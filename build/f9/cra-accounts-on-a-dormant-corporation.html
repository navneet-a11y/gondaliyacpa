A Business Number Is Not One Account

A corporation has one business number, and that number carries separate program accounts. The corporate income tax account is the one everybody knows about, because it is the one the T2 is filed against. It is rarely the one that causes trouble.

A corporation that ever had staff has a payroll account. One that ever registered for GST/HST has a GST/HST account. Each was opened for a reason, and each stays open until somebody closes it, whatever happened to the reason.

AccountOpened becauseWhat it expectsHow it ends
Corporate income taxThe corporation existsA T2 return for every tax yearIt ends with the corporation
PayrollSomeone was going to be paidRemittances through the year and an annual T4 information returnForm RC145
GST/HSTThe corporation registeredA GST/HST return for every reporting periodForm RC145

The Obligation Follows the Account, Not the Activity

This is the sentence that explains almost every unpleasant surprise a dormant corporation gets. The Canada Revenue Agency expects a return on an open account because the account is open. It does not wait to see whether anything happened first.

So a corporation that stopped trading four years ago, paid nobody and sold nothing, still has returns falling due on every account nobody closed. Each one is a separate filing with its own deadline, and none of them is the T2.

Being dormant is not a status you register. There is no form that tells the Agency a corporation has gone quiet and no notification that pauses an account. Nothing happens until an account is closed, and until then the dates keep arriving.

The Payroll Account: Remittances and an Annual T4

An open payroll account expects two separate things, which is why it is the account most often underestimated. Through the year it expects remittances, including nil ones where nobody was paid. After the calendar year it expects a T4 information return.

The T4 deadline is the one that surprises accountants as well as owners, because it does not derive from the fiscal year-end at all. It reports a calendar year and is due at the end of the following February, whatever year-end the corporation uses.

What the payroll account expectsMeasured fromDue
Remittances, including nil onesEach remittance periodFor as long as the account is open
The T4 information returnThe calendar yearThe end of the following February

So a corporation with a June 30 year-end has a T2 date in December and a T4 date at the end of February, four months apart, neither derived from the other. The T4 nil filing requirement checker gives the date, and the payroll nil remittance checker covers the remittances.

Why These Two Accounts Reach the Directors

The corporate income tax account is the corporation’s own liability. The other two are not, and that distinction is the practical reason an unused payroll or GST/HST account is worth closing rather than ignoring.

Payroll source deductions are money withheld from someone’s pay. Net GST/HST is money collected from customers. Neither was ever the corporation’s to keep, and amounts of that kind can be assessed against a corporation’s directors personally. Corporate income tax, on its own, cannot.

AmountWhose money it wasCan be assessed against a director
Payroll source deductionsWithheld from someone’s payYes
Net GST/HSTCollected from customersYes
Corporate income taxThe corporation’s own liabilityNo, not on its own

A dormant corporation rarely owes corporate income tax, because a nil year assesses at $0.00. What it may have is an open account generating exactly the amounts that do reach a director. The director liability exposure checker sorts which is which.

The Order to Deal With Them In

The sequence is not arbitrary, because each step rests on the one before it. Returns first, because a closure does not file anything. Closures next, because they stop the dates recurring. Anything to do with dissolution last, because it rests on a complete filing record.

OrderStepWhy it sits here
1File every outstanding T2 return, oldest firstEach year opens from the closing balances of the one before it
2File the outstanding GST/HST and payroll returnsA closure does not file what fell due while the account was open
3Close the payroll account with Form RC145Ends the remittances and the annual T4
4Close the GST/HST account with Form RC145Ends the reporting periods
5The final T2, clearance and the registry filingOnly where the corporation is being wound up

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a dormant corporation still have to file GST/HST returns?

While the account is open, yes. A period with no sales is reported as a nil return rather than skipped, because the obligation follows the open account rather than the activity.

Do I have to make payroll remittances if nobody was paid?

While the payroll account is open the Agency expects the remittances, including nil ones, and a T4 information return after each calendar year. Form RC145 closes the account and ends both.

How do I close a GST/HST or payroll account?

With Form RC145, which closes either one. Closing it does not file the returns that fell due while it was open, so the outstanding returns come first.

Which of these can be assessed against me personally?

Payroll source deductions and net GST/HST can be assessed against directors personally, because neither was ever the corporation’s money. Corporate income tax, on its own, cannot.

Is any of this covered by the nil T2 fee?

The nil T2 return is. The GST/HST returns, the payroll remittances, the T4 return and the account closures all sit outside the fee of $300.00 plus HST per tax year.

Can I dissolve the corporation instead of dealing with these?

Not cleanly. A clearance certificate request rests on a complete filing record, so an outstanding period blocks it, and dissolving anyway leaves the tax years open behind the corporation.
