What a Numbered Company Is

A numbered company is a corporation that was incorporated without choosing a name, so the registry assigned it a number instead. In Ontario that reads as a seven-digit number followed by Ontario Inc.

The number is the corporation's legal name. It is not a placeholder and it does not need replacing.

Incorporating with a number is a deliberate choice, often because a name search was not wanted or the corporation was set up to hold something rather than to trade under a brand.

Finding Your Corporation Number and Business Number

Two different numbers are in play, issued by two different bodies, and they are routinely confused.

NumberIssued byUsed for
Corporation numberThe incorporating registryRegistry filings and the corporation's identity
Business numberThe Canada Revenue AgencyTax accounts, including the T2

A corporation can have a corporation number and no business number if it never registered for anything. The business number is obtained before the first return is transmitted.

The Annual Return and the Registry

The annual return keeps the corporation on the register. It confirms the directors, the registered office and that the corporation still exists, and it reports no income.

A numbered company is the most common casualty of a missed annual return, because there is no trading activity reminding anyone it exists.

Missing it does not produce a charge. It produces a striking: the registry dissolves the corporation involuntarily, while its tax years stay open and unsettled.

Keeping the Registered Office Current

The registered office is the address the registry and the Agency write to. A change has to be filed within the period the registry sets.

A numbered holding company whose registered office is a former accountant's address, or a home the shareholder moved out of, receives nothing. The warning that it is about to be struck goes to an address nobody reads.

This is the least demanding piece of maintenance there is, and the one most often left.

Director Records

Directors are recorded with the registry and changes are filed when they happen. A corporation whose sole director has died or resigned without a replacement is not properly constituted.

Directors remain exposed to certain corporate amounts, including payroll source deductions and net GST/HST, which makes an accurate record a matter of personal interest rather than paperwork.

The Minute Book

The minute book holds the articles, the registers of directors and shareholders, the share certificates and the resolutions. It is the corporation's own record of itself.

It is maintained through dormant years. Annual resolutions are still passed, and the shareholdings recorded in it are what Schedule 50 reports on the return.

See the guide to Schedule 50 and shareholder reporting.

Nil Years on a Numbered Company

A numbered company that holds nothing and does nothing files a nil T2 for every tax year, six months after each year-end, until it is dissolved.

The Forgotten Numbered Company

A numbered company set up years ago for a purpose that never materialised is the commonest version of this. Nobody traded through it, nobody filed for it, and it has been quietly accruing open years.

Those years can all be filed. They are prepared oldest first and transmitted together, and a corporation nobody used has the simplest possible set of figures.

Finding the corporation number, the year-end on record and the list of open years is the first step, and it is usually the only part that takes any effort.

See filing for a corporation you forgot about and the guide to catching up on unfiled corporate tax returns.

Adding an Operating Name Later

A numbered company can register a business name to trade under without changing its legal name. The number remains the corporation's name on every filing.

Registering a business name is a registry matter and has no effect on the T2. The return continues to be filed in the corporation's numbered name.

Trading under the new name is, of course, trading. That year is not a nil year.

Closing a Numbered Company

Maintenance ends only at dissolution. Until then the corporation exists and acquires a new filing obligation every year, whether or not anyone is attending to it.

Every outstanding year is filed first, then a clearance certificate, then articles of dissolution, then the final T2 running to the dissolution date.

The sequence is in the guide to winding up an inactive corporation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a Numbered Company Treated Differently by the Agency?

No. Every obligation a named corporation has, a numbered one has identically: the return, the schedules, the deadlines and the annual return.

What Is the Difference Between a Corporation Number and a Business Number?

The corporation number is issued by the incorporating registry and is used for registry filings. The business number is issued by the Canada Revenue Agency and is used for tax accounts, including the T2.

What Happens if a Numbered Company Misses Its Annual Return?

The registry can dissolve it involuntarily. That is a striking rather than a charge, and the corporation's tax years stay open and unsettled afterwards.

Does a Numbered Company That Holds Nothing Still File?

Yes, a nil T2 for every tax year, six months after each year-end, until it is formally dissolved.

Can I File for a Numbered Company I Forgot I Had?

Yes. The open years are prepared oldest first and can be transmitted together. A corporation nobody used has the simplest possible set of figures.

Can a Numbered Company Trade Under a Name?

It can register a business name to trade under while its legal name remains the number. Registering the name has no effect on the T2, though trading under it means that year is not nil.
