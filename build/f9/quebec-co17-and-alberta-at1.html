Most Provinces Collect Through the Federal Return

In most of Canada a corporation files one corporate income tax return. The province levies its own corporate tax, but it is collected through the federal T2, so the federal return settles both and there is nothing separate to send.

Quebec and Alberta are the exceptions. Each administers its own corporate income tax and each has its own return. Quebec’s is CO-17, filed with Revenu Quebec. Alberta’s is AT1.

AlbertaThe federal T2 and AT1
Everywhere elseThe federal T2 on its own

Separate Means Separate

CO-17 is a separate provincial return. AT1 is a separate provincial return. That phrasing is deliberate and it is worth taking literally: neither is a schedule attached to the T2, neither is produced as a by-product of filing federally, and filing the T2 does not file either.

Each has its own form, its own filing, and its own deadline. A corporation that files a perfect T2 and nothing else has met one of its two obligations.

A Quiet Year Is Quiet on Both Returns

A corporation with no activity has nothing to report provincially either, so the provincial return for that year is nil in the same way the federal one is.

Being nil does not make it optional. The obligation follows from the corporation having a permanent establishment in the province, not from there being something to report, so a nil year is still a return to file.

This is the same logic as every other obligation on a dormant corporation, and it is the single most useful thing to understand about nil filing: the question is never whether anything happened, it is whether an obligation exists. The guide to zero-activity corporate tax returns works through that distinction.

What Decides Whether a Province Is Involved

The test is a permanent establishment in the province, not where the corporation was incorporated and not where the director happens to live. A federally incorporated corporation operating only in Quebec files CO-17; an Ontario corporation with no Quebec presence does not.

Where that test lands for a particular corporation is a question of fact rather than something a web page can settle, and it is not a question to answer by guessing. What this page can do is say which returns exist and what each one is.

A dormant corporation is usually the straightforward case: a corporation that has never operated anywhere has no permanent establishment anywhere, and the federal T2 is the whole of its corporate income tax filing.

The Registry Annual Return Is a Different Thing

This is the commonest confusion on the subject, and it is worth separating carefully, because the two obligations share the words "annual" and "provincial" and nothing else.

Alberta: AT1

AT1 is Alberta’s corporate income tax return, and it is a separate provincial return filed with Alberta rather than part of the federal T2.

A corporation with an Alberta permanent establishment files both. A nil year produces a nil AT1 rather than no AT1, on the same reasoning as everything else here: the obligation follows the permanent establishment rather than the activity.

AT1 is not covered by the fee of $300.00 plus HST per tax year.

Quebec: CO-17

CO-17 is Quebec’s corporate income tax return, filed with Revenu Quebec. It is a separate provincial return, not a schedule of the federal T2 and not produced by filing federally.

A corporation with a Quebec permanent establishment files both. A nil year produces a nil CO-17 rather than no CO-17.

CO-17 is not covered by the fee of $300.00 plus HST per tax year.

Quebec is also the province where the two obligations on this page are most often conflated, because the registry filing and the tax return both sit with Quebec institutions. They remain separate filings with separate consequences.

The Federal T2 Is Still Required

Worth saying because the logic occasionally runs backwards: a corporation that files CO-17 or AT1 has not thereby dealt with the federal return. The T2 is required of every resident corporation, in every province, every tax year, until the corporation is dissolved.

So a Quebec corporation files two returns and an Alberta corporation files two returns. Neither replaces the other, and a nil year means two nil returns rather than one.

A true nil return carries no percentage late-filing penalty, because that penalty is a percentage of tax owing and the corporation owes $0.00. The unfiled year still stays open forever, invites a demand to file and an arbitrary assessment, blocks a clearance certificate and dissolution, and costs a non-resident-owned corporation $25.00 per day up to $2,500.00.

Both Matter at a Wind-Up

A corporation being wound up has to be current on everything, and a provincial return is part of everything. An outstanding CO-17 or AT1 is an outstanding return in the same way an unfiled T2 is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Quebec have a separate corporate tax return?

Yes. CO-17 is a separate provincial return, filed with Revenu Quebec. It is not part of the federal T2, and filing the T2 does not file it.

Does Alberta have a separate corporate tax return?

Yes. AT1 is a separate provincial return in the same way, filed with Alberta rather than as part of the federal T2.

What about the other provinces and territories?

Elsewhere the province or territory collects its corporate income tax through the federal return, so the T2 is the only corporate income tax return the corporation files.

Is a nil CO-17 or AT1 still required?

The obligation follows from having a permanent establishment in the province rather than from there being something to report, so a nil year still produces a return on each one that applies.

Are CO-17 and AT1 covered by the fee?

No. The fee of $300.00 plus HST per tax year covers one nil federal T2 return for one corporation for one tax year. Provincial returns are handled by Gondaliya CPA Professional Corporation at gondaliyacpa.ca at $350.00 plus HST per year.

Is this the same as the provincial annual return?

No, and they are easy to confuse. A registry annual return confirms the directors and the registered office and reports no income; a provincial tax return reports income and tax. Missing the registry one gets a corporation struck.
