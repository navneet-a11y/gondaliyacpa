Several Saskatchewan Years at Once, Oldest First

Years behind do not merge. Four missed years are four separate T2 returns, each for its own tax year, each carrying its own preparation and its own CPA review, and each at $300.00 plus applicable sales tax per tax year. We file them in the order they fell due, oldest first, because the Canada Revenue Agency carries figures forward from one year into the next and a year filed out of sequence can be assessed against a balance the Agency has not yet been told about.

Two years is $600.00, three is $900.00 and five is $1,500.00, each before tax. There is no reduced rate for filing several together, and the age of a year does not change its price.

The Canada Revenue Agency assessed returns from 73,110 Saskatchewan corporations for the 2023 tax year, up from 70,550 in 2018. Among the provinces that grew at all over those six years, only Alberta grew more slowly. Saskatchewan also peaked before the series ended: it reached 73,770 in 2022 and gave back 660 of that in 2023. A count that turns down is dissolutions completing, and a dissolution cannot complete while a tax year is still unfiled.

The T2 due date comes from the Income Tax Act and does not vary by province. Corporation counts are the Canada Revenue Agency’s own, rounded to the nearest ten, taken from the jurisdiction code on the T2 return, and they include tax-exempt and inactive corporations. They are refreshed when the Canada Revenue Agency reissues the table.

What the Catch-Up Reaches, and What It Does Not

Every year in which the corporation had no activity can be filed this way, however far back it goes. A year in which something did happen is a different return and is not nil: we tell you which years fall on which side before you pay, not after.

What it does not reach is a second provincial return, because Saskatchewan has none. Provincial corporate income tax is collected through the federal T2, so bringing the T2 series current brings the whole corporate income tax position current with it.

The Position on Penalties, in Full

A true nil return carries no percentage late-filing penalty, because that penalty is a percentage of tax owing and the corporation owes $0.00. The unfiled year still stays open forever, invites a demand to file and an arbitrary assessment, blocks a clearance certificate and dissolution, and costs a non-resident-owned corporation $25.00 per day up to $2,500.00.

That is the whole of it, and it is the reason catching up is worth doing in one pass rather than one year at a time. The exposure on a nil year is not a bill; it is that the year stays open and blocks things that need every year closed.

Who This Is For

This service is for a corporation with no activity in the tax year. A corporation with investment income, rental income, or dividends paid or received is not nil.

Other cases that are not nil include a corporation that is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation, an amalgamation, or a corporation already dissolved or struck.
