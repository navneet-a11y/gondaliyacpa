## The angle — the school that holds other people's money under a regulator

Row 483 (Trade Schools) and this row describe nearly the same regulated business in Ontario, so the
two pages are deliberately split by emphasis rather than left to overlap:

- **Trade Schools (483) is the school as a capital-intensive operation** — the shop, the equipment,
  the consumables, the journeyperson instructors, and the exempt-supply problem that bites hardest
  on a school with a quarter of a million dollars of welding bays in it.
- **This page (484) is the school as a custodian under a regulator.** Registration and programme
  approval, tuition held in trust, the prescribed refund policy as a real balance-sheet liability,
  third-party and student aid funding that is a prepayment rather than revenue, and the tuition
  certificates the college has to issue.

Each names the statute and the exemption once and cross-links to the other.

Four subjects are specific to this college and appear on no page on the site:

1. **The money in the account is mostly not yours yet.** Ontario's career colleges legislation
   requires registration to operate and separate approval of each vocational programme, with
   obligations around how student fees are handled — including holding prepaid fees in trust. That
   produces the same shape of problem as a payment platform's float, with a regulator on top: the
   bank balance is largely a liability, what may be drawn is limited to what has been earned, and the
   trust has to be reconciled rather than assumed. A college that has been treating the trust balance
   as working capital has a problem that is both an accounting one and a compliance one.
2. **The prescribed refund policy is a liability, not a policy document.** A student who withdraws
   in a given week triggers a refund calculated on a prescribed basis. That is a measurable,
   recurring obligation that belongs on the balance sheet and in the cash forecast, and it is the
   reason revenue has to be earned across the programme rather than taken on enrolment.
3. **Third-party funding is a prepayment.** Student aid, band and agency sponsorship, employer
   sponsorship and international student fees frequently arrive before any instruction is delivered
   and sometimes from a payer who is not the student. Money received on a student's behalf is not the
   college's revenue when it lands, and a funder who claws back on withdrawal is a receivable
   running the other way. Reconciling funder statements to student accounts is the record that makes
   the whole file defensible.
4. **The college issues tax slips about its own students.** A certified educational institution
   issues the **Tuition and Enrolment Certificate (Form T2202)** so students can claim the tuition
   credit, and files that information with the CRA. It is a compliance obligation about third parties
   that no other business in the education cluster carries, and getting the eligible fees wrong
   lands on hundreds of students' returns rather than only on the college's.
