## The angle — a different taxpayer from all three siblings

This is the only page in the batch whose subject is **not a US person and not a corporation**. The
reader is a Canadian — usually still resident in Canada — earning **employment income** in or from
the United States. That changes the whole analysis:

- **Row 607** is a Canadian corporation expanding. Corporate, outbound, structural.
- **Row 608** is a US citizen resident in Canada. Taxed by the US on citizenship.
- **Row 609** is a green card holder. Taxed by the US on status, with status itself at stake.
- **Row 610** is a Canadian with no US status at all, taxed by the US only because of **where the
  work is performed**. Nothing about citizenship, status, expatriation or entity choice applies.

The four things this page owns:
1. **Treaty Article XV** allocates employment income, and the question is which country may tax
   which dollars of a salary — driven by where the days were worked.
2. **Which American return** — a non-resident **Form 1040-NR** or a resident **Form 1040** —
   turns on the **substantial presence test**, and a year can be dual-status. Named; no day count.
3. **Two social security systems can reach one paycheque.** **FICA** on the American side, CPP on
   the Canadian, with the **Canada–United States totalization agreement** and **certificates of
   coverage** as the coordinating mechanism. Named; no rate, wage base or outcome.
4. **The day count is the evidence.** Nobody keeps a record of days worked in each country until
   they need it, and that record is what the whole treaty analysis rests on. This is the page's
   single most practical message.

Plus the Canadian side throughout: still resident under **ITA subsection 2(1)** and taxed on
worldwide income, with relief through the **ITA section 126** foreign tax credit, and **Form
T1135** once US accounts and property pass the cost threshold. Where someone actually emigrates,
the **ITA 128.1(4)** departure tax is named and **cross-linked to row 609** rather than developed.
