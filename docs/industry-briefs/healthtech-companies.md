## The angle — "we're in healthcare, so we must be exempt" is the expensive mistake

"Healthtech" returned **zero** industry pages on the site. All the existing health material is about
practices and practitioners — Denturists (56799), the healthcare blog posts (48723, 48331, 48234,
48151) — none of it about a company that *builds* health technology. The technology neighbours are
Cloud Service Providers (54831), Managed Service Providers (54828), Hardware Startups (53866),
Digital Product Businesses (53437) and the older Technology Startups & SaaS Companies page (32317),
and none of them touches the one question that decides a healthtech company's cash position.

Four subjects are specific to this business and appear on no page on the site:

1. **Exempt health care service or taxable software supply — and the input tax credit consequence.**
   Part II of **Schedule V** to the Excise Tax Act exempts certain health care services, and the
   founder instinct is to assume the product falls inside it. If that were right it would be bad
   news, not good: an exempt supplier **cannot claim input tax credits**, so the GST/HST on
   developer contractors, cloud hosting, software subscriptions and office costs becomes a permanent
   sunk cost rather than a recoverable one. In practice software licensed to a clinic is generally a
   taxable supply rather than an exempt health care service, which is the better outcome — but it has
   to be established on the actual supply rather than assumed in either direction, and a company
   making **both** exempt and taxable supplies has to apportion its credits.
2. **SR&ED, where healthtech has two problems other software companies do not.** The first is
   **government assistance**: a healthtech company is unusually likely to have taken provincial
   health innovation funding, a hospital partnership payment or accelerator money, and **ITA
   subsection 127(11.1)** reduces qualified expenditures by assistance received. The second is that
   **clinical validation is usually not the eligible part**. Collecting data and running a trial to
   confirm a product works is routine; what qualifies is the systematic investigation resolving a
   technological uncertainty, and the two get conflated on nearly every first claim we review.
3. **Regulatory spend: capital or current.** Software as a medical device brings Health Canada class
   determination, a device licence and a quality management system. Costs incurred to obtain a
   licence that has enduring value sit awkwardly against the capital bar in **ITA paragraph
   18(1)(b)**, and may belong in **Class 14.1** rather than in the year's expenses. That call is
   worth thousands and is usually made by default rather than deliberately.
4. **Selling into public hospitals and provincial health authorities.** Payment cycles that run
   months against revenue recognised on delivery; purchase orders and pilot agreements that are not
   the same as contracts; and the point founders get backwards — **the customer's status does not
   exempt the supply**. Selling to a public body does not make a taxable supply exempt.

Deliberately **not** this page: the practitioner and clinic side (56799 and the healthcare posts),
generic cloud or managed services (54831, 54828), physical device manufacturing (53866), selling
one's own digital product to consumers (53437).

| 1 | **Part II of Schedule V** to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified health care services. The exemptions are defined by both what the supply is and who makes it — an institutional health care service supplied by a health care facility, and consultative, diagnostic, treatment or other health care services rendered to an individual by specified practitioners. A licence of software to a clinic is generally not one of these. |
| 2 | A supplier making **exempt** supplies is not engaged in a commercial activity in respect of them and therefore **cannot claim input tax credits** on the related inputs. This is the central cash consequence of the characterisation, and it runs the opposite way to what most founders assume. |
| 3 | Where a company makes **both** exempt and taxable supplies, input tax credits must be apportioned to the extent the inputs are consumed in commercial activity, on a fair and reasonable basis applied consistently. |
| 4 | **GST/HST registration** is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed **$30,000** in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Ontario is **13%**. Exempt supplies do not count toward that threshold; zero-rated supplies do. |
| 5 | Supplies made to customers outside Canada are generally **zero-rated** under **Schedule VI Part V** of the Excise Tax Act, so no GST/HST is charged while the supply still counts as taxable for threshold and input tax credit purposes. |
| 6 | **SR&ED**: qualifying expenditures go to the pool under **ITA section 37** and generate investment tax credits under **ITA section 127**, refundable at an enhanced rate for a Canadian-controlled private corporation up to its expenditure limit. The rates and the limit are not stated on this page. |
| 7 | **ITA subsection 127(11.1)** reduces qualified expenditures by government assistance and non-government assistance received or entitled to be received. Provincial health innovation funding, hospital partnership payments and some accelerator money fall to be considered here. |
| 8 | SR&ED **excludes** work that is data collection, routine testing, quality control, market research, style change or routine data processing. Clinical validation of a product that already works is typically outside the claim; resolving a technological uncertainty by systematic investigation is inside it. |
| 9 | **ITA paragraph 18(1)(b)** bars the deduction of a capital outlay. Expenditures that secure an asset of enduring value — including, on the right facts, the cost of obtaining a regulatory licence — are capital rather than current, and an eligible intangible generally falls into **Class 14.1**.
