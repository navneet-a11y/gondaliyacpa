# Shared cross-border and non-resident research lock

Applies to category 18 (Cross-Border and Non-Resident): Canadian companies expanding to the
US, US citizens living in Canada, green card holders in Canada, Canadians working in the US,
and future pages in the same block. `/accountant-for-foreign-shareholders-of-canadian-corporations/`
(57171) already exists in this block and is not governed by this spine.

**This block carries a risk no other category on the site has: half the subject matter is
United States tax law.** Every page here must be rigorous about the line between naming a
form, a provision or a mechanism — which is useful and safe — and stating a threshold, a
rate, a dollar amount or a day count, which is neither, because those move and several are
beyond what can be verified here. **The rule for this block: name the form and the
mechanism; never state a US figure.** The Canadian figures below are the site's own
established numbers and may be used.

| # | Locked fact |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Residency is the threshold question on the Canadian side, and it is a question of fact.** A resident of Canada is taxed on worldwide income under **ITA subsection 2(1)**; a non-resident is taxed only on Canadian-source income under **subsection 2(3)**. Residency turns on residential ties — a dwelling, a spouse or common-law partner, and dependants are the primary ones, with secondary ties considered — and CRA's view is set out in **Income Tax Folio S5-F1-C1**. A determination can be requested on **Form NR73** (leaving) or **Form NR74** (entering). **No page asserts anyone's residency status.** |
| 2 | **Leaving Canada triggers a deemed disposition.** Under **ITA subsection 128.1(4)** an emigrant is deemed to dispose of most property at fair market value on departure. Certain property is excluded, including Canadian real property, registered plans and some pension interests. **Form T1161** lists properties held on emigration where the total exceeds **$25,000**, **Form T1243** reports the deemed disposition, and payment of the resulting tax may be deferred under **ITA subsection 220(4.5)** with acceptable security, elected on **Form T1244**. Whether any particular asset is excluded, and what security is acceptable, is reviewed. |
| 3 | **The treaty is the instrument, and its articles are the map.** The **Canada–United States Tax Convention** governs. **Article IV** decides residence and contains the tie-breaker where both countries would treat a person as resident; **Article V** defines a permanent establishment; **Article VII** allocates business profits; **Article XV** covers employment income; **Article XVIII** pensions and registered plans; **Article XXIV** relief from double taxation. A treaty-based position taken on a US return is disclosed on **Form 8833**. **The articles are named; no outcome is asserted for anyone, and no day count, threshold or tie-breaker result is stated.** |
| 4 | **US citizenship and lawful permanent residence carry a filing obligation regardless of where the person lives.** A US citizen or green card holder files a **Form 1040** reporting worldwide income no matter which country they live in, and that obligation does not end at the Canadian border. Foreign accounts are reported on **FinCEN Form 114** (the FBAR) and specified foreign assets on **Form 8938**. Relief from double tax is claimed on **Form 1116** or, for earned income, **Form 2555**. **Every threshold, exclusion amount and filing floor attached to those forms is an open item.** |
| 5 | **The single most expensive thing a US person in Canada can own is a Canadian corporation.** A US person holding shares of a Canadian corporation is generally within the **Form 5471** information-reporting regime, and the anti-deferral rules that sit behind it can tax undistributed corporate income in the hands of the shareholder. A Canadian-controlled private corporation that is entirely ordinary on the Canadian side can therefore be a severe problem on the US side, which is why incorporation for a US person is modelled before it is done rather than after. **No conclusion, inclusion amount or rate is asserted.** |
| 6 | **Canadian registered plans and funds are the recurring reporting trap.** Whether a **TFSA** or **RESP** is a foreign trust for US purposes — engaging **Form 3520** and **Form 3520-A** — and whether a Canadian mutual fund or ETF is a passive foreign investment company engaging **Form 8621**, are the questions that most often go unanswered until a notice arrives. **RRSPs** are addressed by the treaty. **These are named as live questions and are not answered on any page.** |
| 7 | **Non-resident withholding is the mechanism Canada uses to tax outbound payments.** Part XIII tax applies under **ITA section 212** at the statutory rate, reduced by treaty where it applies, with **NR4** reporting. **Regulation 105** withholding applies to fees paid to a non-resident for services rendered in Canada, and **Regulation 102** to employment. A non-resident disposing of taxable Canadian property engages the **ITA section 116** clearance certificate process. **The statutory rate may be named as a statutory rate; no treaty-reduced rate is asserted for anyone.** |
| 8 | **Canadian foreign reporting is unforgiving and the forms are the obligation.** **Form T1135** where specified foreign property costs more than **$100,000**; **Form T1134** for foreign affiliates; **Form T106** for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents; **Forms T1141 and T1142** for foreign trusts. Foreign accrual property income under **ITA sections 91 and 95** can attribute a foreign affiliate's passive income back to the Canadian shareholder. Transfer pricing under **ITA section 247**. The thin capitalisation rule in **ITA subsection 18(4)** and the excessive interest and financing expenses limit in **ITA section 18.2** are named without ratios or thresholds. |
| 9 | **Credit for foreign tax paid is claimed, not assumed.** A Canadian resident claims a foreign tax credit under **ITA section 126** for tax paid to another country on foreign-source income, subject to limits by country and by income type. The interaction with the US side is where double tax actually gets resolved or missed, and it is computed rather than estimated. |
| 10 | Canadian corporate and personal baseline: roughly **12.2%** Ontario combined on the first **$500,000** of active income under **ITA section 125**, against a top personal rate of **53.53%**. **$1.25M** Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption under **ITA 110.6**. Section **85** rollover on **Form T2057**. HST registration once taxable supplies pass **$30,000** over four consecutive calendar quarters. Records six years under **ITA section 230**. |
| 11 | Compliance baseline: late filing costs **5%** of the balance owing plus **1%** per month up to twelve months. A Voluntary Disclosures Program application on **Form RC199** accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full and gives roughly **50%** interest relief. Notice of Objection within **90 days**; taxpayer relief on **Form RC4288**. On the US side, the **Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures** and the **Relief Procedures for Certain Former Citizens** exist as routes back into compliance — **named only, with no eligibility criterion, penalty figure or outcome asserted.** |
| 12 | Lender, investor and buyer view: a **CSRS 4200** Notice to Reader compilation across two fiscal years, with intercompany balances, foreign-currency translation and any cross-border management or royalty charges shown transparently, is what supports financing or a sale where more than one country is involved. |

## Open items &mdash; NOT asserted on any category 18 page until confirmed

1. **Anyone's residency status**, in either country, and the result of the treaty tie-breaker in Article IV. The factors and the folio are named; the conclusion is a question of fact.
2. **Every United States dollar amount, rate, threshold, filing floor, exclusion, day count and penalty.** This includes the FBAR and Form 8938 thresholds, the Form 2555 exclusion, the substantial presence test's day counts, the expatriation tests and any covered-expatriate figure. **The forms and mechanisms are named; no number is stated.**
3. **Whether a TFSA, RESP or any registered plan is a foreign trust** for US purposes, and whether a Canadian mutual fund or ETF is a passive foreign investment company. Named as live questions; no conclusion.
4. **Whether a US person's Canadian corporation gives rise to an income inclusion**, and in what amount. Form 5471 and the anti-deferral regime are named; no conclusion, inclusion or rate.
5. **Whether a permanent establishment exists** in either country for a given business, and what follows from it. Article V and Article VII are named; no threshold, day count or conclusion.
6. **United States state and local tax** — nexus, registration, apportionment, sales tax and franchise taxes. Named as real and as a question for US advisers. No state, threshold, rate or conclusion.
7. **The treaty-reduced withholding rate** for any payment, and whether treaty relief is available at all in a given case. The statutory Part XIII mechanism is named; no treaty rate is asserted.
8. **Whether Regulation 105 or Regulation 102 reaches a given payment**, and any waiver. Named; no rate or outcome asserted.
9. **Transfer pricing method, margin or outcome**, and any T106, T1134 or T1135 penalty amount. The obligations are named; nothing else.
10. **Immigration consequences of anything.** Whether residence abroad affects a visa, a green card or a status, and what any immigration authority requires, is for immigration counsel. **No page in this block gives immigration advice or states an immigration requirement.**
11. **Social security and payroll across the border**, including the totalization agreement, certificates of coverage, FICA and CPP interaction. The agreement is named as existing; no rate, threshold or outcome asserted.
12. **Eligibility for any voluntary disclosure or relief procedure**, in either country.
13. **Thin capitalisation and EIFEL ratios and thresholds.** The provisions are named; no ratio or figure.
14. **Whether US tax advice is being given at all.** Each page states plainly which side of the border the firm is advising on and refers the US positions to the client's US adviser or a cross-border specialist, rather than implying a US opinion.
