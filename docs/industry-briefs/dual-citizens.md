## The overlap problem, stated honestly

A dual Canadian-American citizen living in Canada **is** a US citizen living in Canada, which is
row 608 (page 57215, live). The underlying tax machinery is the same: citizenship-based taxation,
the annual Form 1040, FBAR and Form 8938, the TFSA and Form 8621 problem, Form 5471 for a CCPC.
Repeating that at length would produce a near-duplicate of a live page and both would rank worse.

So this page **names** that shared machinery once each and **cross-links to 608 for the detail**,
then spends its depth on four subjects 608 and 609 deliberately leave open. Keyword separation is
proved by term counts at build time, not asserted.

## The angle — four subjects no other page in this category develops

1. **Renunciation of citizenship.** Row 609 covers abandoning *permanent-resident status* on
   **Form I-407**. Citizenship is a different act entirely: it is renounced before a consular
   officer abroad, and the document produced is a **Certificate of Loss of Nationality (CLN)**.
   The tax side is the expatriation statement on **Form 8854**. The two halves are run by two
   different departments and people routinely assume one does the other.
2. **Transmission to children born in Canada — the accidental American.** US citizenship can pass
   to a child born in Canada to an American parent, documented by a **Consular Report of Birth
   Abroad (CRBA)**. That child may carry an American filing obligation from birth without anyone
   in the family realising it. Practical corollary nothing else on the site says: **a US citizen
   cannot get an ITIN** — a citizen needs a **Social Security Number**, so the **Form W-7** route
   used on rows 610 and 611 is not available here.
3. **FATCA from the Canadian side.** The reason most dual citizens discover any of this is a bank
   asking whether they are a US person. That question comes from the Canada-US intergovernmental
   agreement, implemented in Canadian law as **Part XVIII of the Income Tax Act** (CRS is Part
   XIX). The obligation being discharged is the Canadian financial institution's, not the client's
   — a distinction nobody explains to them.
4. **The saving clause.** Named as a provision of the Canada-United States Tax Convention
   (**Article XXIX**) that limits how far the treaty relieves a country's own citizens. Named
   only; its effect on any person is **not** asserted.

Deliberately **not** this page: the full citizenship-based-taxation walkthrough (608),
permanent-resident status and the Form I-407 route (609), employment income and Article XV (610),
days of presence and US real property (611), corporate expansion and transfer pricing (607).

## Framing line used throughout

**Canada taxes on residence; the United States taxes on citizenship.** Canadian residents are
taxed on worldwide income under **ITA subsection 2(1)** and non-residents on Canadian-source
income under **ITA subsection 2(3)**. Canadian citizenship is irrelevant to both, and American
citizenship changes nothing on the Canadian return. Every exposure on this page comes from the
American half of that sentence.
