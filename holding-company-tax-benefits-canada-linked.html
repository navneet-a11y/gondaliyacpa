Holding Company Tax Benefits in Canada: How Business Owners Can Defer Personal Taxes

A holding company lets profits sit behind the corporate rate instead of the personal one, which is a timing benefit worth real money while it lasts. It is not a permanent saving, and once the money inside starts earning investment income the structure can cost the group more than it defers.

Holding Company Tax Benefits in Canada: Deferral, Dividend Flows and What the Rules Take Back

Holding company tax benefits Canada offers are real, and they are narrower than they are usually described. A holding company mainly owns shares in other businesses; it does not run daily operations or sell products. Instead it holds assets or stock to control those businesses, which can help with holding company tax planning and with managing money more deliberately. The operating business pays corporate tax on its profits first, and if the after-tax money then stays inside a holding company rather than being paid out to the owner, the personal tax on it is postponed. Gondaliya CPA explains how income splitting, deferral and asset separation actually work in a holdco structure, where the small business deduction gets ground down by passive income, and which Income Tax Act rules decide the answer in each case.

Quick Summary

Leaving profits in a holding company postpones personal tax rather than removing it, and the gap between the 12.2% combined small business rate and the 53.53% top Ontario personal rate is what makes the postponement worth having. Please note that the same money, once it is invested inside the group, produces adjusted aggregate investment income, and every dollar of that above $50,000 takes $5 off the group's small business limit. Whether a holdco helps or hurts therefore depends on how long the money stays in and what it earns while it is there.

Holding Company and Operating Company: What Actually Differs

The Structure

What a Holding Company Is and How It Differs from an Operating Company in Canada

A holding company owns shares in other businesses. It does not run daily operations or sell products; it holds assets or stock in order to control those businesses. An operating company runs the actual business, selling goods or services and employing the people who do it. Everything that follows in this article comes from that one structural difference, because it changes where income arises, when personal tax falls due, and which assets are exposed to which risks.

Key Differences Between Holding Companies and Operating Companies

Five differences matter in practice, and each one has a tax or a risk consequence attached.

Operating companies pay tax on income right away, and the owner then pays personal tax when profits are shared out as dividends or salary. Holding companies change the second half of that sentence rather than the first: shareholders pay personal tax later, when they take money out. Knowing which company is doing which job is the starting point for sensible structuring, and owners should think about what fits best for separating assets, managing investments and handling taxes efficiently. If you want advice on using these structures well, including the changes taking effect through 2026, we can set out your options against the CRA rules that apply to them. The compliance side of the same structure, including what each corporation has to file once it exists, is covered in our companion article on holding company tax filing in Canada.

Asset Separation Through Separate Legal Entities

A holding company keeps assets in a different corporation from the one that trades. That separation is why owners move intellectual property, real estate or accumulated cash into a holdco: a claim against the operating business is a claim against the operating company's assets, and assets held elsewhere are not on that company's balance sheet. By holding earnings inside a separate company, owners also keep retained profits away from the risks that attach to daily operations, which reduces the amount at stake if something goes wrong.

How much protection that actually gives is a question of corporate and insolvency law, not of tax law. It is not absolute. Personal guarantees, directors' liabilities, fraudulent or undervalue transfers, security granted to lenders and statutory claims can all reach assets an owner believed were out of range, and a transfer made when the operating business was already in difficulty is exactly the kind of transaction an insolvency process examines. The Income Tax Act does not grant or recognise creditor protection, and nothing in it authorises a holding company structure for that purpose. We are not giving legal advice on this point, and a structure put in place for creditor reasons should be reviewed by a lawyer as well as by us.

  • Hold assets in a different corporation from the one that carries the trading risk.
  • Keep retained earnings away from operational exposure.
  • Get legal advice on how far the separation actually reaches, and when it was made.
Our Actual Experience

Two owners of a contracting business put roughly $900,000 of accumulated cash into a new holding company on the same advice, in the same month. One did it in a year when the operating company had a full order book and no disputes, and the transfer was never questioned. The other did it eleven weeks after a subcontractor had served a claim the company had not told anybody about, and the transfer became the first thing the claimant's lawyers asked about. The tax analysis was identical in both files. The outcome was not, and nothing in the Income Tax Act had anything to do with the difference. Figures changed for privacy.

Tax Deferral: A Timing Benefit, Not a Saving

Timing

Tax Deferral With a Holdco Structure: What the Deferral Is Worth and When It Ends

A holding company lets you delay paying personal tax on profits that stay inside the corporate group. The operating business pays corporate tax first. If the after-tax money then stays in the holdco instead of being paid out immediately, the personal tax is deferred. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth money because of the size of the gap between the two rates rather than because of anything clever in the structure.

Put a number on it. Take $100,000 of active business income inside the business limit in an Ontario Canadian-controlled private corporation. The combined small business rate is 12.2%, being the federal 9% and the Ontario 3.2%, so $12,200 of corporate tax leaves $87,800 available to invest. Paid out to the owner as salary instead, at Ontario's top combined marginal rate of 53.53%, the same $100,000 would have left $46,470. The deferral is therefore worth 41.33 points of cash kept working for as long as the money stays in.

Now close the loop, because this is the part that is usually left out. When the $87,800 is eventually paid out as a non-eligible dividend to a shareholder at the top Ontario rate of 47.74%, the personal tax is $41,916. Add the corporate tax and the total is $54,116 on the original $100,000 — against $53,530 if it had simply been paid as salary. Deferral did not make the tax go away. It moved it, and it cost about six-tenths of a point for the privilege. Above the business limit the arithmetic is slightly worse: the combined general rate of 26.5% leaves $73,500, and paying that out later as an eligible dividend taxed at 39.34% costs a further $28,914.90, bringing the total to $55,414.90, or 55.41%.

So the honest version is this. Deferring tax means you pay later when money leaves the company, and the benefit is timing rather than avoidance. What the timing buys you is compounding on money that would otherwise have gone to the CRA this year, control over which tax year the personal income lands in, and the ability to smooth a spike in one year against a lean one. Those are genuine advantages. They are not the same thing as paying less tax overall, and a structure sold on "tax savings" without the second half of the calculation has not been explained properly.

  • Defer personal tax on earnings kept inside the holding company.
  • Keep the deferred amount invested, which is where the real value sits.
  • Expect the personal tax in full on distribution, give or take integration.
Where the Deferral Shrinks or Reverses

Three things eat into the benefit. The first is a distribution: taking dividends or a shareholder loan out of the holdco triggers the personal tax the structure was deferring, so the deferral simply ends. The second is investment income earned inside the group, which is taxed at high corporate rates while it is being earned and which grinds down the small business limit available to the operating company — covered in the next section, and the single most expensive thing on this page. The third is imperfect integration: as the numbers above show, the combined corporate-and-personal result can land slightly above the salary result, so a long deferral is usually worth it and a very short one may not be.

There is one more risk worth naming. If profits accumulate in a corporation for years and are then extracted badly — through an unrepaid loan, a benefit conferred on a shareholder, or a distribution that does not use the right dividend designation — the owner can end up taxed on amounts in a way that leaves the overall burden higher than if nothing had been deferred at all. The deferral is only as good as the plan for ending it.

Knowing these rules is what sets realistic expectations about deferral against permanent savings, and shows where the gaps would cost you more tax later rather than less. If you are weighing whether the structure is worth it at all, our article on when to set up a holding company works through the triggers in order.

Our Actual Experience

An owner-manager left about $340,000 of after-tax profit in a holdco over four years, invested it in a conservative portfolio, and then asked us to model what the deferral had actually been worth. The honest answer was two numbers, not one. The investment return on the deferred tax was roughly $47,000 better than if the money had been drawn as salary and invested personally. Against that, the investment income the portfolio produced had ground the operating company's business limit down for three of those four years, costing about $28,000 of extra corporate tax. Net benefit around $19,000 on $340,000 — real, worth having, and about a fifth of what the structure had been described to him as being worth. Figures changed for privacy.

The Passive Income Grind: $5 of Business Limit for Every $1

The Grind

Small Business Deduction Impacts and Passive Income Considerations

The small business deduction lowers the tax rate on active business income for Canadian-controlled private corporations, taking the combined Ontario rate down from 26.5% to 12.2% on the first $500,000 of qualifying income. A holding company sitting beside an operating company has to watch what its investment income does to that deduction, because the two corporations are almost always associated, and the business limit is shared and ground down at group level.

Here is the rule that matters, in paragraph 125(5.1)(b) of the Income Tax Act. Where the adjusted aggregate investment income of the corporation and its associated corporations exceeds $50,000 in the preceding year, the $500,000 business limit is reduced by $5 for every $1 of that excess. It reaches nil at $150,000 of adjusted aggregate investment income, because $100,000 of excess multiplied by five is the whole limit.

That multiplier is the point. At $100,000 of adjusted aggregate investment income the reduction is five times the $50,000 of excess, which is $250,000, so the surviving business limit is $250,000 — half of it gone on investment income that many owners expect to cost them $50,000 of limit. Reading the threshold as a one-for-one clawback under-estimates the cost by a factor of five, and the discovery usually arrives on an assessment rather than in a plan.

Price the middle row. Losing $250,000 of business limit moves $250,000 of active business income from 12.2% to 26.5%, a difference of 14.3 points, which is about $35,750 of extra corporate tax in that year. A portfolio inside the holdco earning $100,000 a year will do that every year it earns it. Set that against the deferral benefit from the previous section and you can see why the question "should I leave the money in the holdco" has no general answer: for some groups the grind costs more than the deferral is worth, and for others it does not come close.

So money left in a holding company might delay personal tax but can shrink the tax break on active business income. Good holding company tax planning means forecasting the group's investment income before the year starts rather than measuring it afterwards, and our small business deduction grind calculator is a quick way to see where a group sits.

The Second Grind: Taxable Capital Employed in Canada

There is a separate reduction in paragraph 125(5.1)(a) that catches larger groups regardless of their investment income. Where the combined taxable capital employed in Canada of the corporation and its associated corporations exceeds $10,000,000, the business limit is reduced on a straight line, reaching nil at $50,000,000. The $50,000,000 upper end applies to tax years beginning on or after 7 April 2022; before that it was $15,000,000, so a comparison against an older calculation will be wrong.

Where both grinds apply, the greater of the two reductions is the one that takes effect — they are not added together. A holding company matters here too, because assets parked in a holdco still count towards the group's taxable capital.

How Investment Income Is Taxed While It Sits There

Investment income inside a corporation is taxed differently from active business income and at a materially higher combined rate, and part of that charge is the additional refundable tax on a CCPC's investment income of 10 2/3% under section 123.3 of the Income Tax Act. That additional tax is refundable, which means it comes back — but only when the corporation pays taxable dividends, and only through the mechanism described in the next section. Until then it is the CRA holding the corporation's cash rather than the corporation holding it.

Holding investment profits in a holdco therefore means more exposure to refundable taxes, in exchange for choosing when the funds come out as dividends. That is a cash-flow trade rather than a tax saving, and real estate and portfolio holdings are where it bites hardest because their income is passive by nature.

Our Actual Experience

A group with three associated corporations came to us after an assessment they could not explain. The operating company had claimed the small business deduction on its full $500,000 of active income. The holdco had earned about $112,000 of interest and dividends in the prior year, which the previous adviser had treated as reducing the limit by $62,000. The actual reduction was five times that, $310,000, leaving a limit of $190,000. The resulting reassessment on $310,000 of income at the 14.3-point difference was close to $44,000 plus arrears interest. Nothing had been hidden and nothing had been aggressive; one multiplier had been read as a one. Figures changed for privacy.

    Dividends Between Corporations, Part IV Tax and Section 55(2)

    Intercorporate Flows

    Moving Dividends Up to the Holdco: What Flows Freely and What Does Not

    Dividends can usually flow from an operating company up to its holding company without an immediate tax cost, and that is what makes the structure work at all. The authority is subsection 112(1) of the Income Tax Act, which gives a corporation a deduction in computing taxable income for dividends received from a taxable Canadian corporation. Subsection 112(2) and the subsections that follow are restrictions on that deduction, not the grant of it — a distinction worth getting right, because the restrictions are where real files come unstuck.

    This makes moving money within a group straightforward in the ordinary case, and it is why operating profits can be swept up into a holdco year by year and invested there. The CRA also refunds part of the tax paid on investment income in defined circumstances, which is the dividend refund described below.

    Section 55(2): The Rule That Defeats the Easy Answer

    The sentence "dividends flow between related companies without extra tax at that moment" is true far more often than it is false, and the rule that makes it false is subsection 55(2). Where a dividend is received by a corporation and one of the purposes of the payment was to reduce a capital gain that would otherwise have been realised on a disposition of a share at fair market value, subsection 55(2) can recharacterise the dividend as a capital gain or as proceeds of disposition. The deduction under subsection 112(1) then does not shelter it.

    Two concepts decide the outcome. The first is safe income, broadly the after-tax income the payer has earned and retained that is attributable to the share: a dividend paid out of safe income on hand is generally protected. The second is the purpose test, which asks what the dividend was actually for. A routine annual sweep of trading profits to a holdco is usually unproblematic; a large dividend paid shortly before a share sale, or paid to move value ahead of a reorganisation, is exactly the fact pattern subsection 55(2) was written for.

    For an owner-managed group this is the gap that matters most in the whole subject, because the planning that triggers it is the planning people do when something important is happening — a sale, a buy-out, a freeze, a split between family branches. Safe income needs to be computed before the dividend is declared, not reconstructed afterwards.

    Connected Corporations and Part IV Tax

    Part IV tax is charged under subsection 186(1) of the Income Tax Act at 38 1/3% on assessable dividends received by a private corporation, and it applies to corporations generally rather than only to CCPCs. What it applies to depends on whether the payer is connected with the recipient, a term defined in subsection 186(4). A payer is connected with a particular corporation if either the payer is controlled by that corporation, otherwise than by virtue of a right referred to in paragraph 251(5)(b); or that corporation owned both more than 10% of the issued share capital of the payer having full voting rights under all circumstances and shares of the payer having a fair market value of more than 10% of the fair market value of all its issued shares. The second limb is two tests, not one — votes and value, and both have to be met:

    • Dividends from a non-connected corporation — a portfolio dividend, in other words — attract Part IV tax on the full amount received.
    • Dividends from a connected corporation attract Part IV tax only to the extent that the payer received a dividend refund for paying them.

    That second limb is what keeps an ordinary opco-to-holdco dividend out of Part IV tax: if the operating company has no refundable pool and gets no dividend refund, the holdco pays no Part IV tax on the dividend it receives. A holdco holding a portfolio of public company shares is in the opposite position, and every dollar of those dividends carries the 38 1/3% charge.

    Part IV tax is refundable, and the refund mechanism is subsection 129(1) — a separate provision from subsection 186(1), and not the charging rule. The dividend refund is the lesser of 38 1/3% of the taxable dividends the corporation pays in the year and the relevant balance of refundable dividend tax on hand. So the tax comes back when taxable dividends go out, which means a corporation that accumulates investment income without distributing anything has money sitting with the CRA rather than working.

    ERDTOH, NERDTOH and Why the Pool Matters

    Since 2019 refundable dividend tax on hand has been split into two pools: eligible RDTOH, which broadly holds Part IV tax on eligible dividends received, and non-eligible RDTOH, which holds the refundable portion of tax on the corporation's own investment income and the rest of its Part IV tax. Ordering rules govern which pool a distribution draws on, and paying the wrong type of dividend can leave a refund stranded in a pool the corporation is not accessing.

    The general rate income pool is a different thing again. It governs whether a dividend can be designated eligible in the first place; it is not a refundable tax pool and should not be treated as one. Bad timing or badly ordered intercompany dividends create refundable taxes that tie up cash rather than save it, which is the opposite of what the structure was built for. Our corporate investment income calculator shows how the charge and the refund interact on a given amount.

    Our Actual Experience

    A holdco paid itself a $1,200,000 dividend from its operating subsidiary eight weeks before the owner signed a letter of intent to sell the opco shares. The intention, as described to us afterwards, had been "to get the cash somewhere safe". The purpose test in subsection 55(2) does not care much what a transaction was called, and safe income on hand attributable to those shares was about $410,000. The exposure on the remaining $790,000 was a capital gain nobody had budgeted for, arriving in the same year as the sale. Had the dividend been computed against safe income first, most of it could still have been paid. Figures changed for privacy.

    Getting Money Out: Salary, Dividends and Shareholder Loans

    Withdrawals

    Dividend Strategies, Income Splitting and Taking Money Out of a Holdco

    Every deferral ends with a withdrawal, and the route chosen decides what it costs. Dividends paid by a holdco can be eligible or non-eligible, each with its own gross-up and dividend tax credit under rules including subsections 82(1) and 89(1) of the Income Tax Act. An eligible dividend carries a 38% gross-up with a federal dividend tax credit of 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount; a non-eligible dividend carries a 15% gross-up with a federal credit of 9/13 of that gross-up. The practical consequence is a different top rate for each.

    For 2026, Ontario's combined top personal marginal rates are 53.53% on regular income including salary, 47.74% on non-eligible dividends, 39.34% on eligible dividends and 26.76% on capital gains at the one-half inclusion rate. Those four numbers do most of the work in a remuneration decision. Salary creates RRSP contribution room and CPP entitlement and is deductible to the corporation; dividends create neither but carry the dividend tax credit and avoid payroll remittances; leaving the money in defers everything and feeds the passive income grind. A mix usually suits an owner-manager better than any pure strategy, and the right mix changes with the year.

    You also need to balance how often you pay dividends against reinvesting earnings, because the dividend that releases a refundable tax pool is the same dividend that ends the deferral. That keeps cash flowing while avoiding the small business deduction problems that arise at associated-group level.

    Income Splitting and Tax on Split Income (TOSI)

    Holding companies let families share investment income among members who hold shares, and controlling the timing and the recipient of dividends can balance tax across a family. The rule that limits this is the tax on split income in section 120.4 of the Income Tax Act, which applies the top marginal rate to split income received by a specified individual from a related business, wiping out the benefit of the split. The question is never whether TOSI exists; it is whether an exclusion applies to this shareholder for this amount.

    The main exclusions are worth naming, because this is where the planning actually happens:

    • Excluded shares — broadly, a shareholder aged 25 or over holding at least 10% of the votes and value of a corporation that is not a professional corporation and that earns less than 90% of its income from services. Shares of a holding company commonly fail this test, which is the trap.
    • Excluded business — the individual is actively engaged on a regular, continuous and substantial basis in the business in the year, or was in any five previous years.
    • Reasonable return — an amount reasonable in the circumstances having regard to the individual's work, property contributed, risks assumed and previous amounts received.
    • Age 65 and over — amounts that would have been excluded for an older spouse are excluded for the other spouse where that spouse is 65 or older in the year, which is why retirement reorganisations look different from mid-career ones.

    Ignoring TOSI can produce personal tax that wipes out the corporate benefit entirely, and testing it shareholder by shareholder and amount by amount is the only way to know where a family actually stands.

    Shareholder Loans: Three Separate Rules

    Taking money out of a holdco as a loan rather than as a dividend is common, and it is governed by rules that are frequently run together. They are distinct.

    Subsection 15(2) includes the amount of a loan to a shareholder in the shareholder's income for the year the loan was made. Subsection 15(2.6) provides the repayment exception, and its terms matter: the loan must be repaid within one year after the end of the lender's taxation year in which the loan was made, and the repayment must not be part of a series of loans or other transactions and repayments. That is not the same thing as "within one year of borrowing" — for a lender with a 31 December year end, a loan taken in January 2026 has until 31 December 2027, while a loan taken in December 2026 has the same deadline. Repaying and re-borrowing to reset the clock is precisely what the series wording is there to catch.

    Section 80.4 is a separate charge. Where a shareholder loan carries no interest or interest below the prescribed rate, an imputed interest benefit is included in the shareholder's income for each year the loan is outstanding, whether or not subsection 15(2) applies to the principal.

    Section 80 has nothing to do with any of this — it deals with the forgiveness of commercial debt. If you use shareholder loans to take money from your holding company, handle them deliberately: a loan documented, bearing interest at the prescribed rate and repaid within the statutory window is a legitimate route, and the same loan left on the books and forgotten turns the deferral into an income inclusion plus an interest benefit.

    Corporate Attribution Is a Different Rule Again

    Corporate attribution lives in section 74.4 of the Income Tax Act and has nothing to do with shareholder loans. It applies where an individual transfers or lends property to a corporation and one of the main purposes is to reduce the individual's income and benefit a designated person — a spouse, or a related minor. Where it applies, the individual is deemed to have received interest at the prescribed rate on the outstanding amount of the transfer, which can be an expensive annual inclusion on a structure that looked complete.

    It is a real constraint on family holdco planning, particularly on freezes involving minor children, and it is usually managed through the design of the share structure rather than fixed afterwards. Treating it as part of the shareholder loan rules, as it often is, means it gets tested against the wrong facts.

    Our Actual Experience

    An owner drew about $180,000 from his holdco across three years and recorded every draw as a shareholder loan, repaying each one in full with a transfer from his personal account a few weeks before the corporation's year end and re-borrowing a similar amount shortly afterwards. He had read the one-year rule and believed he was inside it. The series wording in subsection 15(2.6) is what the CRA relied on, and the whole $180,000 came back into income across the three years, with imputed interest under section 80.4 on top because the loans carried no interest. The money had been available as dividends the entire time at a far lower cost. Figures changed for privacy.

    Setting Up, Estate Freezes, the LCGE and Staying Within the Rules

    Structuring

    Incorporation, Share Transfers and Section 85 Rollovers

    Setting up a holding company follows a clear sequence. First, choose the corporate statute, federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the relevant business corporations Act. Then prepare and file the articles of incorporation, and obtain a business number for tax purposes. Our article on federal versus provincial incorporation in Ontario sets out what turns on that first choice, and the incorporation itself is the smallest part of the work.

    The substantive step is usually transferring shares of the operating company to the holdco using a rollover under section 85 of the Income Tax Act. A section 85 election lets property move at an elected amount between the adjusted cost base and fair market value, deferring the capital gain that an outright transfer at fair market value would realise. Used correctly it moves shares into the holdco without a tax charge at the time; used carelessly, or filed late, it is one of the more expensive mistakes available in this area. We cover the mechanics in detail in our article on section 85 planning to defer capital gains.

    Things to settle during incorporation:

    • Shareholder agreements, and how they interact with the new share classes.
    • How dividends will move between the related companies, and in what order.
    • Which corporation will hold which assets, and why that one.
    Governance and Ongoing Management

    Clear records are what make the structure defensible, and they show that decisions were made where and when the documents say they were. A holdco's corporate records should carry meeting minutes, resolutions of the directors or the shareholders, a share register showing ownership changes, and documentation for every dividend declaration. That paperwork meets the statutory requirements and it is the first thing requested if the CRA asks questions.

    Appointing directors and officers properly matters as well. Directors carry duties under the governing statute — in Ontario, the Business Corporations Act — and they are the people who authorise the distributions that move retained earnings around the group. Steady governance lowers the risk of an unauthorised payment or an undocumented transaction, either of which can undo the tax position on deferred earnings. The standing accounting work that goes with it, including the compilation engagement for a holding company, is where most of that record gets kept current.

    What Setting Up a Holdco Actually Involves

    Costs vary by jurisdiction and by how much restructuring is needed, and they fall into three groups: the government filing fees charged by the registry, the legal work on the share reorganisation and the agreements behind it, and the accounting and tax work on the valuation, the elections and the annual filings that follow. A holdco files a T2 every year whether or not it did anything, and it reports intercorporate dividends and investment income on schedules the operating company does not need.

The work grows with the number of subsidiaries and the number of investments, and that is the honest driver of the cost rather than any hourly rate. Whether the structure is worth that cost is a question about your facts, which is what the consultation is for; it is not a question that can be answered in general terms, and nobody should promise that an upfront spend will produce savings later.

    Estate Freezes, Succession and the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption

    Holding companies help pass wealth between generations, and the usual tool is an estate freeze: the existing shareholder exchanges common shares for fixed-value preferred shares, and new common shares, which will carry the future growth, are issued to the next generation or to a family trust. The current value is locked in for the freezor, and growth after the freeze accrues to the new shareholders. An estate freeze is ordinarily a commercially motivated transaction — it fixes a value for estate planning, it brings successors into the ownership, and it makes the eventual transition possible.

    The lifetime capital gains exemption is where this kind of planning is most often misunderstood, so it is worth setting out carefully. For 2026 the exemption on qualified small business corporation shares is $1,275,000, with a corresponding capital gains deduction of $637,500 at the one-half inclusion rate. To use it, the shares disposed of must meet the qualified small business corporation share tests in subsection 110.6(1), which include asset tests measured both at the time of disposition and throughout the preceding 24 months.

    Shares of a holding company are not automatically qualified small business corporation shares. A holdco full of portfolio investments and real estate will typically fail the asset tests, because the tests look through to whether the assets are used in an active business carried on primarily in Canada. In a holdco-and-opco structure the exemption is therefore usually accessed on the opco shares, and where several family members are to use their own exemptions that is commonly arranged by having a family trust hold the opco shares and allocate the gain among its beneficiaries. Inserting a holdco does not multiply access to the exemption by itself, and in some structures it removes access that existed before. This needs to be designed deliberately, in advance, with the asset tests checked against the actual balance sheets.

    The Anti-Avoidance Rules You Have to Respect

    The general anti-avoidance rule is subsection 245(2) of the Income Tax Act, and it applies where a transaction or series results in a tax benefit, is an avoidance transaction, and is abusive of the provisions relied on. It was amended by Bill C-59, which added an economic substance rule to the misuse-and-abuse analysis and a penalty of 25% of the tax benefit for tax years beginning on or after 1 January 2024, subject to a reporting-based exception.

    Those rules have to be respected, and that is a different statement from saying freezes and holdco structures are suspect. They are not. A freeze done for succession reasons, with real share terms, a defensible valuation and documented commercial purpose, is ordinary planning. What draws attention is a step with no purpose other than the tax result, a valuation nobody can support, or a series that only makes sense read backwards from the tax outcome. Expert advice is necessary here because the facts, the documents and the order of the steps are what decide it.

    Estate freezes also shift timing rather than eliminating tax: the gain is still there, it simply accrues to different shareholders and is realised on a later disposition or on death. Owners should work with advisers familiar with trust law and with the group-level passive income caps that reduce the small business deduction, because a freeze that solves the succession problem can create the grind problem.

    Professional Corporations, Real Estate and Personal Portfolio Transfers

    Professional corporations face share ownership restrictions, and those come from the provincial regulator for the profession together with the governing business corporations statute — not from the CRA, which administers the tax rules rather than the ownership rules. In Ontario, for example, the Business Corporations Act sets out who may hold shares of a professional corporation and the relevant college or regulator imposes its own conditions through its by-laws and certificate of authorisation. Those rules frequently rule out a holding company as a shareholder, so the tax plan has to be tested against the professional rules before it is designed, not afterwards.

    Real estate holdings add their own complication, because rental income is generally passive. It faces the higher combined corporate rates on investment income together with the additional refundable tax of 10 2/3% under section 123.3, and it counts towards the adjusted aggregate investment income that grinds the group's business limit. Keeping real estate inside a holdco can therefore create refundable taxes recoverable only when dividends are paid, which changes timing without cutting total tax.

    Moving a personal investment portfolio into a holdco may help with management and with consolidating control, and it does not reliably create a permanent saving. Portfolio income — interest, foreign dividends, Canadian dividends and realised gains — is taxed differently from active business earnings, and some of those streams integrate poorly through a corporate layer. Interest and foreign income in particular are among the worst candidates. Owners should check whether a corporate holding actually suits the income they earn before transferring anything, and should remember that a transfer of appreciated securities to a corporation is itself a disposition unless an election is filed.

    Ontario Factors and When a Holdco Is the Wrong Answer

    Provincial rates shape how much deferral is available, because each province sets its own rates for active and investment income. In Ontario the combined small business rate is 12.2% and the combined general rate is 26.5%, and those numbers against the personal rates in the previous section are what size the deferral. The 2026 Ontario rates and brackets are the starting point for any modelling. Federal incorporation suits a business operating across Canada; provincial incorporation can be simpler and cheaper where the business operates in one province. In either case the corporation is taxed where it carries on business, so the incorporating jurisdiction does not set the tax rate.

    When choosing, consider how retained earnings will actually be used in the holdco, the planned exit route for shareholder payouts, and how the choice affects integration between the corporation and its shareholders.

    And it is worth saying plainly when the answer is no. Owners who need the cash out every year, or whose active income already sits comfortably below the small business limit, often find a holdco adds filing obligations, professional fees and complexity without a deferral worth having — because there is nothing being deferred. Owners with uneven income and no intention of reinvesting profits are in the same position. Where a planned exit involves a share sale, the holdco may actively interfere with access to the lifetime capital gains exemption unless it was designed around that from the start. The structure is a tool with a cost, and it earns its keep in some files and not in others; the final call comes down to detailed facts rather than to general claims about deferral or savings.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    FAQs

    FAQs on Holding Company Tax Benefits in Canada

    What is the current federal general corporate tax rate for holding companies?+

    The federal general corporate rate is 15%, and it applies to income that does not qualify for the small business deduction. With Ontario's general rate of 11.5% the combined rate is 26.5%, against 12.2% combined on income within the business limit.

    How does the Ontario small business deduction limit affect holding companies?+

    The business limit is $500,000 of active business income federally and $500,000 in Ontario, shared across associated corporations. Investment income inside a holdco reduces that limit for the whole group under paragraph 125(5.1)(b), which increases the tax paid by the operating company.

    What is Part IV tax and when does it apply?+

    Part IV tax is charged under subsection 186(1) at 38 1/3% on assessable dividends received by a private corporation. It applies to the full amount of dividends from a non-connected corporation, and to dividends from a connected corporation only to the extent the payer received a dividend refund for paying them. "Connected" is defined in subsection 186(4). The tax is refundable through the dividend refund in subsection 129(1), which is a separate provision.

    What is the top personal marginal tax rate in Ontario for 2026?+

    53.53% on regular income, including salary. The rate on dividends and gains is lower: 47.74% on non-eligible dividends, 39.34% on eligible dividends and 26.76% on capital gains. Those four figures decide most dividend-versus-salary questions.

    How does the capital gains inclusion rate affect holding company sales?+

    The inclusion rate is one half, so half of a capital gain is taxable; the proposed two-thirds rate was cancelled. That rate drives exit planning, the value of the lifetime capital gains exemption and the cost of any gain triggered on a freeze or a reorganisation.

    What is the dividend gross-up on eligible dividends?+

    Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38%, with a federal dividend tax credit of 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount plus a provincial credit. Non-eligible dividends are grossed up by 15% with a federal credit of 9/13 of the gross-up. The gross-up raises taxable income and the credit then reduces the tax, which is what produces the different top rates.

    What does Income Tax Act subsection 15(1) regulate concerning holding companies?+

    Subsection 15(1) is the shareholder benefit rule. Where a corporation confers a benefit on a shareholder — personal use of corporate property, an expense paid on their behalf, an asset sold to them below value — the value of that benefit is included in the shareholder's income. It does not authorise or recognise any structure; it is the rule that catches value moving out of a corporation without being properly reported.

    What is the timing value in tax deferral using a holding company?+

    It is the return earned on the tax you have not yet paid. Income taxed at 12.2% in the corporation rather than 53.53% personally leaves 41.33 points more cash working, and the value of the deferral is whatever that cash earns before the personal tax eventually falls due on distribution.

    When does the deferral shrink or reverse?+

    When money comes out, because the personal tax arrives in full. It also shrinks while the money stays in, if the investment income it earns grinds the group's business limit, and integration is imperfect enough that the combined corporate-and-personal result on small business income is marginally above the salary result. A short deferral can genuinely be worse than paying salary.

    What happens to active business income limits when funds are retained in a holding company?+

    Retained funds that generate investment income reduce the small business limit for the associated group. Every dollar of adjusted aggregate investment income above $50,000 removes $5 of limit, so $100,000 of investment income leaves a limit of $250,000 and $150,000 removes it entirely, pushing active income to the 26.5% general rate.

    These answers cover the rates, the provisions and the thresholds that decide whether a holding company helps a particular owner. They are general information about how the rules work rather than advice on any specific structure.

    Industry Expertise

    The rules are the same in every sector. What differs is how much profit is retained, how much of the group's income is passive, and whether the regulator has already decided who may own the shares. Here are eleven, and what tips the holdco decision in each.

    • Technology startups & SaaS: A holdco is usually about holding the shares and the cash rather than the operating risk, and the exemption on a future exit lives on the operating company's shares, not the holdco's.
    • E-commerce & online retailers: Inventory-heavy trading throws off cash in bursts, and the question is whether the cash swept up to the holdco gets invested into a business limit grind or held back for working capital.
    • Consulting firms: Almost all income is services income, which means holdco shares rarely qualify as excluded shares for TOSI and the planning has to run through the excluded-business or reasonable-return tests instead.
    • Construction, contractors & skilled trades: A holdco holding the yard, the plant and the equipment keeps those assets away from job-site claims, and the opco keeps the active business assets it needs to pass the asset tests.
    • Property developers & builders: Development land and work in progress sit awkwardly in a holdco, because the asset tests look at whether property is used in an active business carried on primarily in Canada.
    • Real estate investors & holding companies: Rental income is passive, so a property-holding corporation feeds the adjusted aggregate investment income grind every year and attracts the 10 2/3% additional refundable tax.
    • Transportation, logistics & trucking: Equipment and real property in a holdco with the trucks and the contracts in the opco is a common split, and the group's taxable capital is what to watch as the fleet grows.
    • Restaurants & food and beverage: Single-location operators with modest retained profit frequently find a holdco costs more in filings than the deferral is worth, because little is being retained to defer.
    • Daycare, childcare & CWELCC services: Funding conditions and programme reporting are set by the regulator rather than by the tax rules, and they often constrain what the corporate structure can look like.
    • Dentists & dental practices: Share ownership of a professional corporation is set by the provincial regulator and the business corporations statute, which commonly prevents a holdco from holding the shares.
    • Medical doctors & physician corporations: Physician corporations face the same ownership restrictions, so the planning usually runs through dividends, remuneration mix and a separate corporation for non-practice investments.

    Industry Spotlights: Holding Company Planning Across Eleven Sectors

    Two questions separate the sectors above. How much of the group's income is passive, which decides whether the small business deduction survives; and who is allowed to hold the shares, which in several professions is decided before tax is considered at all.

    Medical Doctors & Physician Professional Corporations

    Share ownership of a physician professional corporation is restricted by the provincial regulator and by the governing business corporations statute, which commonly rules out a holding company as a shareholder. Planning therefore runs through the remuneration mix and, where investments are to be held corporately, through a separate corporation rather than a holdco above the practice.

    • The ownership rules come from the college and the statute, not from the CRA.
    • Investment income held corporately still grinds the business limit.
    • Annual corporate filings are required for every corporation in the group.
    Dentists & Dental Practices

    Dental corporations face the same category of restriction, set by the provincial regulator with the business corporations statute behind it. Where retained profit is being invested, the passive income threshold is reached faster than most practice owners expect, because equipment financing frees up cash that then earns investment income.

    • Confirm who may hold shares before designing anything.
    • Forecast investment income against the $50,000 threshold each year.
    • Keep shareholder loan accounts documented and repaid within the window.
    Daycare / CWELCC Services

    Funding and programme conditions shape what the corporate structure can look like, and incorporation is often a condition of access. Where a holdco is added above an operating entity, the reporting obligations and the ownership disclosures that come with funding need checking first.

    • Programme conditions can constrain ownership and distributions.
    • Registered office and director details must stay current for reporting.
    • Retained surpluses invested corporately count towards the group grind.
    Real Estate Investors & Holding Companies

    This is the sector where the grind does the most damage, because rental income is passive by nature. A property-holding corporation that nets $120,000 of rent has removed $350,000 of business limit from the associated group before anything else happens.

    Separating the active business from the property-holding corporation is usually the fix, and it works far better before the property is acquired than afterwards.

    Construction Companies & Skilled Trades

    Holding the yard, the plant and the equipment in a separate corporation keeps those assets away from job-site claims, and leaves the operating company holding the assets it needs to pass the active business asset tests. The equipment corporation's lease income, though, is investment income at group level.

    • Decide which corporation holds which assets before the next acquisition.
    • Price intercompany rent realistically, because it is income to the lessor.
    • Get legal advice on how far the asset separation actually protects.
    Technology Startups / SaaS Companies

    The exit is what matters, and the exemption on a sale lives on the operating company's shares. A holdco inserted above the opco without planning can put the shares that would have qualified into the wrong hands.

    What that means in practice:

    1. Decide who holds the opco shares long before a sale is contemplated.
    2. Check the asset composition of the opco against the 24-month tests.
    3. Sweep surplus cash up to a holdco so the opco stays asset-clean.

    That last point is the one genuine exit benefit of a holdco, and it only works if the sweeps are done annually rather than the month before closing.

    E-commerce / Retailers

    Cash in these businesses arrives in bursts around seasonal peaks, and the temptation is to move surplus up to a holdco and invest it. Whether that is sensible depends on whether the money is working capital in disguise.

    Key points:

    • Money swept up and invested starts generating adjusted aggregate investment income.
    • Money swept up and held in cash does not, which is often the better answer.
    • Bringing it back down is a loan or a dividend, each with its own consequences.

    Netting the deferral benefit against the grind is the calculation that decides it.

    Restaurants / Food & Beverage Businesses

    Most single-location operators retain little, and a holdco adds a second T2, a second set of records and a second professional fee to defer tax on a sum that was never going to stay in.

    What to work out first:

    • How much profit will actually be retained rather than drawn
    • Whether an exit or a family transfer is realistically in view
    • Whether asset separation is the real objective rather than deferral

    Where the answer to all three is no, the simpler structure is the right one.

    Transportation / Logistics Companies

    Equipment-heavy groups accumulate taxable capital quickly, and the second grind in paragraph 125(5.1)(a) starts at $10,000,000 of combined taxable capital employed in Canada regardless of investment income.

    Things to watch:

    • Measure the group's taxable capital, not just its investment income
    • Remember the greater of the two reductions applies, not the sum
    • Assets held in a holdco still count towards the group total

    A group can lose its business limit on taxable capital alone while earning almost no passive income.

    People Also Ask

    Related Questions
    Is a shareholder loan an effective way to access funds from a holding company?+

    It can be, if it is done to the terms of the exception rather than to a rough memory of it. Subsection 15(2) includes the loan in your income; subsection 15(2.6) excepts it where the loan is repaid within one year after the end of the lender's taxation year in which it was made, and where the repayment is not part of a series of loans and repayments. A low-interest or interest-free loan also attracts an imputed interest benefit under section 80.4 for every year it is outstanding. Used deliberately, a documented interest-bearing loan repaid inside the window is a legitimate route; used as a substitute for a dividend policy, it converts a deferral into an income inclusion.

    Which arrangements sit outside typical holding company planning?+

    Family trusts and other trust structures, professional corporations where the regulator restricts ownership, and any cross-border element — a non-resident shareholder, a foreign subsidiary, assets outside Canada — all need specialist input beyond a standard holdco setup, and the trust and legal documents in particular need a lawyer. We also refer out SR&ED claim preparation, in the same way we refer out audit and review engagements. What we do handle directly is the corporate tax work, the structure planning, the annual filings and representation through a CRA audit.

    Should you hold funds in a holdco or pay them out as dividends or salary?+

    It depends on two things: how long the money will stay in, and what it will earn while it is there. Holding funds defers personal tax and buys a return on the deferred amount, at the cost of increasing the group's investment income and grinding the small business limit. Paying out triggers personal tax now — 53.53% on salary, 47.74% on a non-eligible dividend, 39.34% on an eligible one — and ends the exposure. A long deferral on money that stays in cash or in low-yield holdings is usually worth it; a short deferral on money that will be invested aggressively often is not.

    What changes affect holding company strategies year to year?+

    Four things move. Legislation, including amendments like the Bill C-59 changes to the general anti-avoidance rule. Indexed amounts, such as the lifetime capital gains exemption, which is $1,275,000 for 2026. Rates, both corporate and personal, federally and provincially. And your own facts — the group's investment income, its taxable capital, who holds which shares, and whether a sale or a transfer has come into view. The structure is set once; the arithmetic that justifies it has to be redone every year.

    Salary, dividend or leaving it in: which suits most owners best?+

    A mix, and the proportions change with the year. Salary creates RRSP room and CPP entitlement and is deductible to the corporation. Dividends carry the dividend tax credit, avoid payroll remittances, and are the mechanism that releases a refundable tax pool. Leaving money in defers personal tax and feeds the passive income grind. In practice most owner-managers take enough salary to fund RRSP room and personal cash needs, dividends to manage the rest and to access refunds, and leave in whatever genuinely will not be needed for several years.

    Glossary of Key Terms

    Definitions
    • Holding company: a corporation that owns shares or assets in order to control other businesses rather than to trade itself.
    • Operating company: the corporation that carries on the active business, selling goods or services.
    • Tax deferral: postponing personal tax by leaving after-tax corporate profits inside the group rather than distributing them.
    • Small business deduction: the reduction that brings the combined Ontario rate on the first $500,000 of active business income to 12.2%.
    • Business limit: the $500,000 of active business income eligible for the small business deduction, shared across associated corporations.
    • Adjusted aggregate investment income: the measure of group investment income that grinds the business limit above $50,000 under paragraph 125(5.1)(b).
    • Taxable capital employed in Canada: the second grind measure in paragraph 125(5.1)(a), running from $10,000,000 to $50,000,000.
    • Additional refundable tax: the 10 2/3% charge on a CCPC's investment income under section 123.3, recovered through the dividend refund.
    • Part IV tax: the 38 1/3% tax under subsection 186(1) on assessable dividends received by a private corporation.
    • Connected corporation: a payer connected with the recipient under subsection 186(4), either because the payer is controlled by that corporation or because the corporation owns both more than 10% of the full-voting issued share capital and shares worth more than 10% of the fair market value of all issued shares.
    • Dividend refund: the refund under subsection 129(1), being the lesser of 38 1/3% of taxable dividends paid and the relevant refundable pool.
    • RDTOH: refundable dividend tax on hand, split since 2019 into eligible and non-eligible pools with their own ordering rules.
    • Intercorporate dividend deduction: the deduction in subsection 112(1) for dividends received from a taxable Canadian corporation.
    • Safe income: broadly the retained after-tax income attributable to a share, which shelters a dividend from subsection 55(2).
    • Shareholder loan: an amount lent by a corporation to a shareholder, included in income under subsection 15(2) unless the subsection 15(2.6) exception applies.
    • Corporate attribution: the deemed interest inclusion under section 74.4 where property is transferred to a corporation to benefit a spouse or minor.
    • TOSI: tax on split income under section 120.4, applying the top rate unless an exclusion is met.
    • QSBC share: a qualified small business corporation share meeting the asset tests in subsection 110.6(1), which is what the exemption applies to.
    • Lifetime capital gains exemption: $1,275,000 for 2026 on QSBC shares, giving a capital gains deduction of $637,500.
    • Section 85 rollover: the election that transfers property to a corporation at an elected amount, deferring the gain.
    • Estate freeze: a reorganisation that fixes the current shareholder's value in preferred shares and directs future growth to new common shares.
    • GAAR: the general anti-avoidance rule in subsection 245(2), amended by Bill C-59 with an economic substance rule and a 25% penalty.

    Verdict

    Four things decide whether a holding company is worth having. How much profit genuinely stays in — the deferral is the gap between 12.2% in the corporation and 53.53% personally, and it is worth nothing on money that gets drawn anyway. What that money earns while it is in — every dollar of group investment income above $50,000 removes $5 of business limit, so $100,000 of investment income leaves a limit of $250,000 and costs roughly $35,750 of extra corporate tax in the year. What the structure is actually for — separating assets from operating risk, bringing successors into the ownership and controlling which year personal income lands in are real benefits that survive the grind, and they are usually the honest reason to build one. And whether the exit still works — holding company shares are not qualified small business corporation shares by default, the exemption is normally accessed on the operating company's shares, and a structure assembled without that in mind can remove access that existed before.

    Before You Start

    Four things to bring before anyone designs a structure. First, the financial statements of every related corporation, because the asset composition of each one decides both the grind and the exemption, and neither can be assessed from a description. Second, the current share structures and any shareholder agreements — who holds what class, with what rights, and what the agreement says happens on a death or a departure. Third, a list of the investments held inside the corporations and what they earn, because that total against the $50,000 threshold is the single most important number in the analysis. Fourth, your short-term cash needs set against your long-term plans: how much you need to draw each year, whether a sale or a family transfer is in view, and over how long. Tell us which provinces you operate in or expect to expand into as well, because the rates and the registry obligations follow that.
