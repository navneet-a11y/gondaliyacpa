Business and Corporate Tax Filing

  • We prepare your corporation's T2 with the small business deduction under ITA section 125 applied against the $500,000 limit, computed on income earned rather than on the fees that happen to have been collected.
  • That distinction is the whole file. A career college's bank balance and its taxable income are different numbers by design, because much of the balance is money held for students who have not yet been taught.
  • We set instalments against the tax actually owing rather than against cash on hand, which otherwise produces the familiar pattern of a college overpaying after an intake and underpaying after a refund season.
  • We choose a fiscal year end that falls between intakes rather than inside one, which gives a cleaner cut on partly delivered programmes and puts the compliance work outside your busiest administrative weeks.
  • And we keep the six years of records ITA section 230 requires, which for a career college means the student ledger and the trust reconciliations alongside the invoices and the bank statements.
2

Tuition Held in Trust

  • Start with the uncomfortable fact: a large part of the money in your bank account is not yours. Ontario's career colleges legislation requires fees collected in advance of delivery to be held in trust for the student.
  • Which makes the balance a liability rather than working capital, and means only fees that have actually been earned may be drawn out of the account for the college's own use.
  • So the trust account has to be reconciled to the student ledger, student by student, with what each has paid, what each has been taught, and what therefore remains held on their behalf.
  • That reconciliation is the control that distinguishes a solvent college from one that is quietly spending next term's tuition, and it is the first thing we build when a college comes to us.
  • We state no statutory figure, percentage or deadline from the career colleges legislation on this page. The obligations are real and specific, and they are checked against the legislation rather than quoted from a website.
3

The Prescribed Refund Policy as a Liability

  • A career college cannot write its own refund terms. The legislation prescribes what a student is entitled to on withdrawal or on the college ceasing to deliver, and your contract cannot reduce it.
  • Which has a direct accounting consequence most colleges miss: the refund entitlement is a liability sitting against the fees you hold, and its size changes as each student progresses through the programme.
  • So revenue is earned across the programme as instruction is delivered rather than recognised on enrolment, because on the day a student signs you are holding their money and owing them a course.
  • A college that books the full fee at enrolment reports a profit it has not made, pays tax on income it may have to refund, and discovers the problem in the quarter withdrawals cluster.
  • We also track your historical withdrawal pattern by programme, because a cohort with a thirty per cent attrition rate and one with five per cent are not the same business however similar the fee schedule looks.
4

Revenue Earned Across the Programme

  • The mechanics matter as much as the principle. Revenue is earned as instruction is delivered, so a forty-week programme starting in September is earned across that period rather than banked in September.
  • Which means a deferred revenue balance that moves every month and has to be computable from the student ledger rather than estimated, because it is the figure your whole balance sheet rests on.
  • Registration and administrative fees need separate thought. Whether a non-refundable fee is earned on enrolment or across the programme depends on what the student actually receives for it and when.
  • A student who withdraws mid-programme, one who defers to the next intake and one who transfers between programmes each need handling differently, and the student ledger is what makes that possible at all.
  • And we show revenue and instructional cost by programme rather than in total, because a college running six programmes almost always has two carrying the other four and usually does not know which.
5

Third-Party Funding as a Prepayment

  • A large share of your fees never comes from the student. Government student aid, band or agency sponsorship, employer-funded retraining and international student fees all arrive from somebody other than the person being taught.
  • Which does not change the accounting but does change the risk. The money is still a prepayment earned as instruction is delivered, and it is still held for the student rather than owned by the college.
  • The difference is who you owe it back to. Where a funder is entitled to recover its payment if the student withdraws, that clawback is a liability to the funder rather than to the student.
  • So we track funded enrolments by funder as well as by student, which is also what makes the reporting those funders require possible without reconstructing a year's records in a fortnight.
  • Receivables from a funder behave nothing like receivables from a student, with longer settlement, partial payments and conditions attached, and a college that nets them together cannot see which of them is actually collectable.
6

Form T2202: An Obligation About Other People's Taxes

  • Here is an obligation no other education business on our books carries. A certified educational institution issues Form T2202, the Tuition and Enrolment Certificate, to each student for their own tax return.
  • Which makes it a compliance obligation about third parties' taxes rather than your own, and one you discharge on their behalf with amounts and months that have to be right student by student.
  • The amount certified is the eligible tuition actually paid for the year, which is not the amount invoiced, not the amount held in trust, and not the amount the college recognised as revenue.
  • Four different numbers for the same student, which is why the certificates are filed late or wrong by colleges whose student ledger cannot produce any of them on demand.
  • We prepare and file the certificates and the information return on time from the ledger, and we handle the amendments when a student withdraws or a funder's payment changes what was eligible.
7

Exempt Tuition and the Credits You Lose

  • Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, and the vocational programmes a career college delivers are the kind of supply that provision was written to cover.
  • Exemption is not relief. You charge no tax on exempt tuition and claim no input tax credits on the inputs used to make that supply, so the HST on your campus costs stays with you.
  • Which on a campus business means rent, utilities, furniture, computers, software, marketing and the professional fees are all more expensive than their invoices suggest, permanently and by a double-digit percentage.
  • Marketing is the one that stings, because a college spending heavily on student acquisition is paying tax on all of it with nothing to claim back against exempt tuition revenue.
  • We settle the characterisation on your actual programmes rather than asserting it, because whether a given programme is an exempt supply and whether you meet the statutory definition are questions of fact.
8

Mixed Supplies and Apportionment

  • Few career colleges are purely exempt, and finding the taxable side is worth real money. Textbook and materials sales, uniform and equipment sales and room rental are generally taxable supplies carrying credits.
  • So is instruction that is not vocational in the statutory sense: a short interest course, a corporate training day sold to an employer, or an exam preparation session open to the public.
  • Which makes most colleges mixed suppliers, with credits on shared inputs apportioned on a basis that is fair and reasonable on your facts, documented when adopted and applied the same way every period.
  • The shared inputs are the obvious ones: the premises, the utilities, the administrative staff, the software and the professional fees. A round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing an auditor examines.
  • And a programme fee that bundles exempt instruction with taxable materials into one price needs looking at before somebody else looks at it, because a single amount covering both is a question with no good answer.
9

Instructors and Admissions Staff

  • A career college's largest cost is people, and the classification question arrives twice over: once for the instructors who deliver the programmes and again for the admissions staff who fill them.
  • It is decided on the whole relationship rather than the agreement's label: control, who provides the premises and materials, chance of profit, risk of loss, and integration into the college's business.
  • An instructor teaching your approved curriculum on your timetable in your classroom to your students, term after term, looks a great deal like an employee however the engagement is papered.
  • Commission-paid admissions staff are the second exposure, and a college paying recruiters on invoices with no slips filed collects penalties per slip rather than once when the question is finally asked.
  • We assess each arrangement individually, run payroll properly where it is employment with source deductions remitted on schedule, and prepare the T4 or T4A slips on time either way.
10

Campus Costs and Capital

  • A campus is a capital commitment. Classroom fit-out, practical laboratories, computer suites, furniture, servers and the leasehold improvements that turn an office floor into a college are capital property rather than expenses.
  • So the cost is recovered through capital cost allowance over time rather than deducted when the contractor is paid, with the unrecoverable HST forming part of the cost that enters the register.
  • We state no capital cost allowance rate on this page, because the class an asset sits in turns on what the asset actually is and the leasehold recovery period is tied to the lease term.
  • Which makes the lease a planning question as much as a property one, because a short lease on a heavily fitted campus recovers that fit-out faster but leaves you exposed on renewal.
  • And we separate the genuine improvements from the repair work and the moveable furniture in a single contractor invoice, because that invoice routinely contains all three and nobody splits it afterwards.
11

Incorporation and Structure

  • Most career colleges are incorporated already, often because a regulator or a funder expects it, so the real work is making the structure serve the business rather than merely exist.
  • Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit on active business income, the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates, and limited liability around a student-facing operation.
  • We model salary against dividends for the owners, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions, and on whether the income record will satisfy a lender or a regulator asking about financial capacity.
  • One caution specific to you: retained earnings are not spare cash when a large share of the bank balance is held in trust, and we will say so plainly when a planned distribution would reach into it.
  • We discuss no capital gains inclusion rate and no lifetime capital gains exemption amount on this page, because those figures have moved and we will confirm the current ones with you rather than publish one.
12

CRA Audit Resolution

  • When your revenue recognition is examined, we produce the student ledger, the deferred revenue computation and the trust reconciliations, which is why we build those as a matter of routine rather than at audit.
  • When an instructor or an admissions recruiter is reassessed as your employee, we handle the source deduction exposure and argue the classification on the real relationship rather than conceding to close the file.
  • When your exempt and taxable apportionment is questioned, we produce the documented basis and the invoices behind it, which is a short conversation when the basis was set deliberately and recorded.
  • When a Form T2202 amount is challenged by a student's own assessment, we reconcile what was certified to what was actually paid and amend the certificate where the ledger shows we should.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment and pursue taxpayer relief on Form RC4288, cancelling penalties and interest where a prior preparer's error was the cause.

Case Studies: Career Colleges

Toronto Business College — Tax Paid on Money It Had to Refund

The problem: A Toronto career college recognised each student's full programme fee as revenue on enrolment, which produced a strong September and January and a corporation tax bill to match. Then a cohort with heavy attrition withdrew through the middle of the programme, the prescribed refunds went out, and the college discovered it had paid tax on income it was now returning to students out of a trust account that was already short.

What we did: We rebuilt the student ledger so each student's payments, delivered instruction and refund entitlement were visible, restated revenue as earned across programme delivery, and computed the deferred revenue balance from the ledger rather than estimating it. We reconciled the trust account to the ledger student by student, corrected the affected returns, and set up a monthly reconciliation the owner now reviews.

The result:

  • Revenue restated as earned across delivery, returns corrected
  • Trust account reconciled student by student for the first time
  • Attrition tracked by programme so the fee model could be repriced

Mississauga Healthcare College — Certificates Nobody Could Produce

The problem: A Mississauga college delivering personal support worker and healthcare administration programmes had issued Form T2202 certificates using the amounts it had invoiced, because that was the only figure its system could produce. Students began receiving CRA queries on their own returns when the certified amounts did not match what they had paid, and a cohort funded partly by an agency created a second mismatch nobody could explain.

What we did: We rebuilt the ledger so that invoiced, paid, held in trust and recognised were four separate figures per student, identified which payments came from the student and which from a funder, re-derived the eligible tuition actually paid for each affected year, amended the certificates and refiled the information return. We then put the certificate preparation on the ledger permanently.

The result:

  • Certificates amended from actual payments, not invoiced amounts
  • Funder payments separated from student payments per enrolment
  • Students' CRA queries resolved from the college's own records

Ottawa Language College — Marketing Tax Nobody Had Budgeted

The problem: An Ottawa college running language and pathway programmes for international students was spending heavily on agent commissions, digital advertising and recruitment events, and had been claiming input tax credits on all of it. Its tuition was exempt. The credits were not available, the position had run for three reporting years, and the college had also never identified the genuinely taxable parts of what it sold.

What we did: We settled the exempt and taxable characterisation on the actual programme mix, found the taxable side — textbook and materials sales, room rental, exam preparation sessions sold to the public and corporate training days — and set a documented apportionment basis for the shared inputs. We corrected the prior periods proactively and pursued relief on the resulting interest.

The result:

  • Credit position corrected before a sales tax audit found it
  • Taxable activities identified so real credits could be claimed
  • Apportionment basis documented and applied consistently since

Career College Accounting & Tax FAQs

Is the money in our bank account ours?
Much of it is not, and that is the single most important fact about a career college's accounts. Ontario's career colleges legislation requires fees collected in advance of delivering a programme to be held in trust for the student, which makes that balance a liability rather than working capital. Only fees that have actually been earned — that is, matched by instruction you have delivered — may be drawn out for the college's own use. The practical consequence is that a healthy-looking bank balance tells you almost nothing about whether the college is solvent, because the balance belongs partly to students who have not yet been taught. We state no statutory figure, percentage or deadline from that legislation on this page: the obligations are specific and they are checked against the legislation as it applies to your registration, not quoted from a web page.
How does the trust account work in our books?
As a liability reconciled to a ledger, every month. The account balance on its own is not a control — it only becomes one when it can be tied back to the student ledger, student by student: what each has paid, who paid it, what instruction has been delivered to them, and what therefore remains held on their behalf. That reconciliation is what separates a college that is genuinely solvent from one that is quietly funding this term's payroll out of next term's tuition, and it is the first thing we build when a college comes to us. It is also what every other number on your file depends on. Your revenue figure, your refund liability, your funder reporting and your Form T2202 amounts are all derived from the same ledger, and none of them can be produced reliably without it.
When do we recognise tuition as revenue?
As instruction is delivered, not when the student enrols or pays. A forty-week programme starting in September is earned across that period, so the fee arriving in August is a liability in August and revenue over the following months. The balance in between is deferred revenue, and it has to be computable from the student ledger rather than estimated, because it is the figure your whole balance sheet rests on. Registration and administrative fees need separate thought: whether a non-refundable fee is earned on enrolment or across the programme depends on what the student actually receives for it and when. Withdrawals, deferrals to a later intake and transfers between programmes each need handling differently. And we show revenue and instructional cost by programme rather than in total, because a college with six programmes usually has two carrying the other four and rarely knows which.
How does the prescribed refund policy affect our accounts?
It turns the refund entitlement into a liability you carry, and it is the reason enrolment-based revenue recognition is dangerous for a college rather than merely imprecise. You cannot write your own refund terms: the legislation prescribes what a student is entitled to on withdrawal or if the college ceases to deliver, and your contract cannot reduce it. So the amount you would owe a given student if they left tomorrow sits against the fees you hold, and it changes as they progress through the programme. A college that books the full fee at enrolment reports a profit it has not made and pays tax on income it may have to return — and it finds out in the quarter withdrawals cluster, which is also the quarter the trust account is under most pressure. We also track your historical attrition by programme, because a cohort losing thirty per cent and one losing five per cent are different businesses.
What about student aid, band funding and employer sponsorship?
Same accounting, different risk. Government student aid, band or agency sponsorship, employer-funded retraining and international student fees are all still prepayments earned as instruction is delivered, and still held for the student rather than owned by the college. What changes is who you owe the money back to. Where a funder is entitled to recover its payment if the student withdraws, that clawback is a liability to the funder, not to the student, and a college that cannot tell the two apart cannot tell what it actually owes. So we track funded enrolments by funder as well as by student, which is also what makes the periodic reporting those funders require possible without reconstructing a year's records in a fortnight. Receivables from a funder behave nothing like receivables from a student either — longer settlement, partial payments, conditions attached — so we never net them together.
What is Form T2202 and do we have to issue it?
If you are a certified educational institution, yes — and it is an obligation about your students' taxes rather than your own, which makes it unlike anything else on your compliance calendar. Form T2202, the Tuition and Enrolment Certificate, reports the eligible tuition a student actually paid for the year and the months of enrolment, so they can claim it on their own return. The amount you certify is the eligible tuition paid, which is not the amount you invoiced, not the amount you hold in trust, and not the amount you recognised as revenue. Four different figures for the same student in the same year. That is exactly why these certificates go out late or wrong: the college's system can produce one of the four and somebody uses it. We prepare and file the certificates and the information return from the ledger, and amend them when a withdrawal or a funder payment changes what was eligible.
Do we charge HST on our tuition?
Often not. Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, and the vocational programmes a career college delivers — instruction in courses leading to a certificate or diploma attesting to competence to practise a trade or vocation, supplied by a vocational school as the legislation defines it — are the kind of supply that provision was written to cover. But whether a particular programme qualifies, and whether your college meets the statutory definition, are questions of fact. We settle them on your actual programmes and document the reasoning rather than asserting an answer on a web page. Getting it wrong in either direction lands on you: charging tax you should not have charged creates one problem, and failing to charge tax you should have creates a larger one, because the liability does not go away because your students have moved on.
If tuition is exempt, can we claim the HST on our campus costs?
No, and on a campus business that is expensive in a way most colleges underestimate. Input tax credits are available to the extent an input is acquired for use in a commercial activity, and making exempt supplies is not a commercial activity for this purpose. So the HST on rent, utilities, classroom furniture, computers, software, professional fees and marketing attributable to exempt tuition is not recoverable — it is simply cost, permanently, at a double-digit percentage on top of every invoice. Marketing is the line that stings most. A college spending heavily on agent commissions, digital advertising and recruitment events is paying tax on all of it with nothing to claim back against exempt tuition revenue, and a student acquisition cost modelled without that is understated. The partial relief is that few colleges are purely exempt, which is what the next answer is about.
What about textbooks, uniforms and corporate training days?
Those are usually where your recoverable credits live, so finding them is worth real money. A sale of goods is a supply of goods rather than educational instruction, so textbook and materials sales, uniform and equipment sales, and room or facility rental are generally taxable supplies carrying credits with them. So is instruction that is not vocational in the statutory sense — a short interest course, an exam preparation session open to the public, a corporate training day sold to an employer. That makes most colleges mixed suppliers, with credits on shared inputs apportioned rather than claimed or forgone wholesale. The shared inputs are the obvious ones: premises, utilities, administrative staff, software, professional fees. The method matters more than the percentage: pick a basis that is genuinely reasonable on your facts, document why, and apply it the same way every period. A round number nobody can account for is the first thing a sales tax auditor takes apart.
Are our instructors and admissions staff employees?
Often yes, and the question arrives twice over. Classification is decided on the whole relationship rather than the agreement's label: control over how the work is done, who provides the premises and materials, chance of profit, risk of loss, and integration into the business. An instructor teaching your approved curriculum, on your timetable, in your classroom, to your students, term after term, looks a great deal like an employee however the engagement is papered — and the reassessment lands on the college as the payer, with the source deductions that should have been withheld. Commission-paid admissions staff and recruiters are the second exposure and the one more often overlooked: a college paying a dozen recruiters on invoices with no slips filed collects penalties per slip rather than once. We assess each arrangement individually rather than labelling everyone a contractor, run payroll properly where it is employment, and file the T4 or T4A slips on time either way.
How do we account for the campus fit-out?
As capital property, over time. Classroom fit-out, practical laboratories, computer suites, furniture, servers and the leasehold improvements that turn an office floor into a college are capital rather than expenses, so their cost is recovered through capital cost allowance rather than deducted when the contractor is paid. The unrecoverable HST forms part of the cost entering the register, which on an exempt college is a real addition to every line. We state no capital cost allowance rate here, because the class an asset sits in turns on what the asset actually is, and the recovery period for a leasehold improvement is tied to the lease term rather than to the asset's physical life. That makes the lease a planning question as much as a property one: a short lease recovers a heavy fit-out faster but leaves you exposed at renewal. And we split the improvements, the repair work and the moveable furniture that routinely share one contractor invoice.
Can we take a dividend out of the college?
Carefully, and the answer depends on something no ordinary business has to check. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit on active business income, the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates, and limited liability around a student-facing operation. We model salary against dividends for the owners, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions, and on whether the income record will satisfy a lender or a regulator asking about financial capacity. But the caution specific to you is this: retained earnings are not spare cash when a large share of the bank balance is held in trust for students who have not been taught. A distribution that looks sustainable against a profit figure can still reach into money that is not the college's to distribute, and we will say so plainly rather than process it. We also publish no capital gains inclusion rate or lifetime capital gains exemption amount here, and will confirm the current figures with you directly.
