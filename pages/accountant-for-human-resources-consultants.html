Business and Corporate Tax Filing

  • We prepare your corporation's T2 or your Form T2125 as a sole proprietor, claim the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, and file on time so the late filing penalty never starts.
  • That penalty is 5% of the balance owing plus 1% for each complete month the return is late, to a maximum of twelve months, and it is charged on a balance you may already have the cash to pay.
  • We set instalments once the tax owing justifies them, because nothing is withheld at source on consulting fees and a practice that has never paid instalments meets the requirement in its second profitable year.
  • We claim business-use-of-home under ITA subsection 18(12), limited to the income of the business with the excess carried forward, and we keep the calculation on the same basis from one year to the next.
  • We keep the six years of records ITA section 230 requires, including the client address evidence behind each rate, because a sales tax question arriving three years later is answered from the file or not at all.
2

The Place of Supply, Client by Client

  • This is the first thing we build and the one that decides most of what follows. The place of supply rules for a service generally look to the recipient's address obtained in the ordinary course of business, and that address sets the rate.
  • So we map every client to a province: 13% where the supply is made in Ontario, 15% in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador and Prince Edward Island, 14% in Nova Scotia, and 5% GST everywhere else in Canada.
  • The address we use is the business address most closely connected with the supply, which is not always the one on the signature block, and we record which address was used and when it was obtained.
  • We then check the template, because the rate is set once in your invoicing software and repeats until somebody looks at it. A practice that added its first Atlantic client in year two usually never changed the default.
  • And we keep the map current as the client list moves, since a head office relocation or a new regional contact can change the rate on the next invoice without anybody treating it as a tax event.
3

Nova Scotia, and Rates Already Charged

  • Nova Scotia's HST rate came down to 14% effective 1 April 2025, the provincial part falling by one percentage point. Supplies made before that date carried the former rate, and both numbers are correct for their own period.
  • It is the single most common error we see on a consulting invoice, because the old figure is the one in the template, in the memory and in the spreadsheet somebody built when the practice took its first Halifax client.
  • Over-collecting and under-collecting are not symmetrical problems. Tax collected is tax to be remitted whether or not it should have been charged, while tax not charged is usually money you now have to find yourself.
  • So we review what has actually been invoiced rather than what the engagement letters say, correct the issued invoices properly, and adjust the returns for the affected periods instead of quietly fixing the rate going forward.
  • Where a client has already paid at the wrong rate we deal with the credit note and the refund of the excess tax in the right order, so the correction does not create a second problem in the next return.
4

Retainers, Deposits and Deferred Revenue

  • A retainer has two dates and they are rarely in the same quarter. Section 168 makes the tax payable at the earlier of the day the consideration is paid and the day it becomes due, which is usually when the money arrives.
  • The revenue, though, is earned only as the service is performed. An unearned retainer is a liability of the practice until the work is delivered, not sales, and that is true however long the engagement runs.
  • So we report the tax in the period you received the deposit and carry the fee as deferred revenue until the hours or the deliverables land, which keeps the HST return and the income statement both right.
  • On a fixed-fee investigation or a six-week compensation study we track what has been delivered against what was quoted, so the year-end does not depend on somebody remembering how far a file had got in December.
  • And we handle the cancelled engagement properly: what is refunded, what is retained for work already done, and what that does to the tax already reported, rather than leaving an old deposit sitting in the ledger for years.
5

Input Tax Credits Under Section 169

  • Registration is not purely a cost, and this is why. Because advisory fees are a taxable supply rather than an exempt one, section 169 lets you recover the tax on what you buy to make them.
  • For this practice that means the salary benchmarking data licence, the practice management and survey software, the laptop and second monitor, the professional subscriptions, and the mileage and travel to a client workplace.
  • The test is the extent to which the property or service is acquired for consumption, use or supply in your commercial activity, so a laptop used partly at home for other things is claimed on that basis.
  • We keep the supporting documentation the credit depends on, because a credit is only as good as the invoice behind it and a card statement showing an amount and a vendor name is not an invoice.
  • Where a non-resident supplier charges you tax on a digital subscription, we check that it was charged correctly and that your registration number has been given where it should have been, rather than claiming whatever appears.
6

GST/HST Registration and the Quick Method

  • Registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the four previous consecutive calendar quarters, and consulting fees count in full toward that test.
  • For most of these practices the threshold goes by on a single fractional retainer, so the practical question is not whether to register but when the obligation actually arose and what was invoiced in the meantime.
  • The Quick Method is available only where annual taxable supplies stay within $400,000, and the remittance rate depends on the province and the type of business rather than on a single national figure.
  • It bars credits on your operating inputs while leaving credits on capital property available, which matters for a practice whose largest recoverable costs are data licences and software rather than equipment.
  • So we run both methods on your own figures before choosing, and you can try the arithmetic yourself with our Quick Method calculator before you speak to us.
7

The Personal Services Business Question

  • A fractional engagement is the fact pattern worth checking. ITA subsection 125(7) defines a personal services business where an incorporated employee performs the services and would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the recipient but for the corporation.
  • The exclusions matter as much as the test: the rules do not apply where the corporation employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year, or where the services are supplied to an associated corporation.
  • If the rules do reach an engagement the cost is severe. The small business deduction is denied on that income and ITA paragraph 18(1)(p) restricts deductions to the incorporated employee's salary and benefits and a short list of specified amounts.
  • So we work on the evidence that points the other way: concurrent clients, your own tools and premises, a real chance of profit and risk of loss, your own insurance, and engagement letters that match how the work is actually done.
  • We will not tell you on a web page whether the rules reach your retainer, because it turns on your actual arrangement. We look at the engagement, we tell you plainly, and we document the position while the work is live.
8

Associates, Investigators and Payroll

  • When two complainants need separate interviewers in the same week you bring in an associate, and at that moment you are the payer and the classification question becomes yours rather than your client's.
  • It is decided on the whole relationship: control over how the work is done, ownership of tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration into your practice. The label in the agreement does not settle it.
  • Where the associate is genuinely independent you report the fees for services on a T4A, with no CPP or EI withheld, and we set that up so the slips are not being reconstructed in February.
  • Where the arrangement is employment in substance we say so and set payroll up properly from the first payment, with source deductions, remittances and T4s, because a correction after the fact costs more than doing it once.
  • If you take work from law firms as a retained expert, we keep the billing and the subcontract trail separate per engagement, since that is the file where a question about who paid whom is most likely to arrive.
9

Non-Resident Clients and Cross-Border Work

  • A consulting supply made to a client outside Canada can be zero-rated, which means tax at 0% while your input tax credits on the inputs to that work remain fully available to you.
  • Whether a particular engagement qualifies is a question of fact on the terms of that engagement, so we look at what is actually supplied and to whom before anything is invoiced at 0%.
  • That matters more than it sounds, because the difference between a zero-rated supply and an exempt one is the difference between keeping your credits and losing them on the related costs.
  • Where you travel to a client outside the province or the country, we keep the expense record on the same basis as the domestic files so the credits and deductions do not fall apart at year end.
  • We advise on Canadian tax and coordinate with a specialist where another country's rules are engaged, rather than guessing at a foreign filing obligation that is not ours to answer.
10

Vehicle, Home Office and Equipment

  • Most of this practice runs from a home office and a laptop, so these are the deductions that actually move the number, and they are the ones most often claimed on an estimate rather than a record.
  • The home office is claimed under ITA subsection 18(12), deductible only against the income from that business with any excess carried forward, on a reasonable area or usage basis applied consistently each year.
  • Vehicle costs are deductible on the business portion, which means a log. The drive to a client workplace for an investigation interview is business; the drive to your own office at home generally is not.
  • Equipment, software and the data licences a compensation practice depends on are tracked as current costs or capital depending on what they are, with the input tax credits claimed on the same evidence.
  • We keep all of it in one set of books rather than two, so the T2 or the T2125, the HST returns and the credits all come off the same records instead of three versions of the year.
11

Incorporation and the Client Mix

  • Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the first $500,000 of active business income, which is the whole reason most consultants ask about it in the first place.
  • But for this practice the question is inseparable from the client mix, because a corporation whose revenue comes mostly from one long fractional retainer is the arrangement the personal services business rules are aimed at.
  • So we model it on your actual engagements and your actual draw rather than on a revenue rule of thumb, and we say plainly when the answer is that incorporating would not help you yet.
  • We compare salary against dividends on real numbers, including the RRSP room salary creates, the CPP cost it carries, and what you actually need to take out of the practice each month.
  • And we choose a fiscal year end deliberately, which for a practice whose work arrives in project waves is worth a conversation rather than defaulting to December because that is what the forms assume.
12

CRA Audit Resolution

  • When a rate charged on an out-of-province invoice is questioned, we answer with the client address record and the engagement letter behind it rather than with an explanation assembled after the query arrives.
  • When your input tax credits are reviewed, we produce the invoices behind them and the basis on which anything used partly privately was apportioned, which is where most credit reviews are actually won or lost.
  • When an associate is reassessed as your employee, we deal with it on the classification factors and the real working arrangement, and we deal with the payroll consequences that follow if the reassessment stands.
  • When the personal services business question is raised on a fractional retainer, we put forward the independence evidence built while the engagement was live, which is worth considerably more than evidence assembled afterwards.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment, request relief from penalties and interest on Form RC4288 where the facts support it, and use Form RC199 before the CRA contacts you where that is still open.

Toronto HR Consultancy — Three Years of Atlantic Invoices at the Wrong Rate

The problem: A Toronto practice with a fractional retainer in Moncton, a policy project in Charlottetown and two Halifax clients had charged 13% on every invoice since the day it registered. The invoicing template had been set up in the first year, when every client was in Ontario, and nobody had touched the tax field since. Two of the Atlantic engagements had been running for three years.

What we did: We built the client address record first, mapped each client to the province of the address obtained in the ordinary course of business, and worked out what should have been charged in each period, including the Nova Scotia change on 1 April 2025. The result:

  • Under-collected tax quantified and settled on a known number, not a guess
  • Client address evidence held with every engagement letter from then on
  • Template rebuilt so a new province no longer inherits Ontario's rate

Mississauga Compensation Practice — A Year Booked on Deposit

The problem: A Mississauga compensation and job evaluation specialist billed 50% of each project on signature. Deposits were being recorded as revenue when received, so a December that happened to contain four signed engagements looked like the best month in the practice's history while most of the work was delivered the following spring. The HST on those deposits had also been reported late, in the quarter the work finished.

What we did: We separated the two dates. Tax on each deposit was reported in the period it was received under section 168, and the fee itself was carried as deferred revenue until the study was delivered. We then rebuilt the prior year on the same basis and tracked delivery against what was quoted for every open file.

The result:

  • HST on deposits reported in the right period, ending the recurring lateness
  • Deposits carried as a liability until delivery, not booked as sales
  • Project margin visible per engagement rather than per calendar month

Ottawa Workplace Investigator — An Associate Reassessed

The problem: An Ottawa investigator taking files from employers and law firms used the same associate on roughly forty weeks of work a year, set her schedule, supplied the templates and the case management system, and paid her monthly on an invoice. No slips had ever been issued. The CRA reassessed the arrangement as employment and raised source deductions with penalties and interest.

What we did: We looked at the factors honestly rather than arguing the label in the agreement, and told the client that on control, tools and integration the reassessment was largely right. We dealt with the payroll consequences, set up proper payroll from that point, and separated the genuinely independent associates, who now receive a T4A for fees for services each February.

The result:

  • Payroll set up correctly rather than argued about for another year
  • Genuinely independent associates identified and reported on T4A slips
  • Engagement terms rewritten to match how the work is actually done

What HST rate do I charge a client in another province?
The rate follows the client, not you. The place of supply rules for a service generally look to the address of the recipient that you obtained in the ordinary course of business — the business address most closely connected with the supply — and the rate for that province applies. So the same compensation study is 13% for an Ontario client, 15% for a client in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador or Prince Edward Island, 14% for a Nova Scotia client, and 5% GST for a client in Alberta, British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Quebec or the territories. Which address you used, and when you obtained it, is worth recording with the engagement letter, because an invoice issued today may have to be defended three years from now.
Is Nova Scotia 14% or 15%?
It is 14%, and it has been since 1 April 2025, when the provincial part of the HST came down by one percentage point. Supplies made before that date carried the former rate, so both numbers are correct for their own period and your old invoices are not wrong. The problem is that 15% is the figure in most invoicing templates, in most spreadsheets and in most memories, and a template does not update itself. If you took your first Nova Scotia client before April 2025 and have not looked at the tax field since, the rate on your invoices is almost certainly still the old one. It is the single most common error we see on a consulting invoice.
What if I have been charging the wrong rate for years?
It is fixable, and the two directions are not symmetrical. Tax you collected is tax to be remitted whether or not it should have been charged, so over-collecting from an Alberta client at 13% does not leave you better off — it leaves you holding money that is not yours. Under-collecting is worse in practice, because the tax was still payable and it is usually you rather than the client who ends up finding it. The right order is to establish what should have been charged in each period, correct the issued invoices, deal with any credit note and refund of excess tax properly, and adjust the affected returns rather than quietly changing the rate going forward and hoping nobody looks back. We would rather quantify it on a known number than leave it as an open exposure.
Do I have to register for GST/HST?
Once your worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter, or over the four previous consecutive calendar quarters, yes. Consulting and advisory fees are a taxable supply rather than an exempt one, so every dollar of fee income counts toward the test — there is no part of this practice's revenue that sits outside it. For a fractional chief human resources officer on a monthly retainer, the threshold is usually crossed inside the first year and sometimes on a single engagement. The practical question is therefore rarely whether to register but when the obligation actually arose, what was invoiced in the meantime without tax, and whether registering voluntarily earlier would have been worth it for the credits.
When does a retainer go into my HST return?
In the period you received it, in most cases. Section 168 of the Excise Tax Act makes the tax payable at the earlier of the day the consideration is paid and the day it becomes due, which for a deposit taken on signature is the day the money arrives. That is true even though you have not done any of the work yet, and even if the engagement is later cancelled. It is one of the two most common timing errors we correct on these files: a practice that reports the tax when the project finishes is reporting it one or two quarters late, every time, and the interest on that quietly accumulates.
Is an unearned retainer revenue?
No. A fee received before the service is performed is a liability of the practice until you deliver, and it is earned as the work is done rather than when the money arrives. This is the other half of the same question, and the two halves have different answers on purpose: the tax on a deposit is reported when it is received, while the revenue is recognised as the engagement is delivered. A practice that drives both from the same number gets one of them wrong. In practical terms it means a December with four signed engagements is not your best month if the work lands in the spring, and tracking what has been delivered against what was quoted is what keeps the year-end honest.
Can I claim the HST on a benchmarking data licence or software subscription?
Yes, and this is the reason registration is not purely a cost. Because your advisory fees are a taxable supply, section 169 allows an input tax credit to the extent that property or a service is acquired for consumption, use or supply in the course of your commercial activity. For this practice that reaches the salary benchmarking and survey data licences, the practice management software, the laptop and monitor, professional subscriptions, and the travel and mileage to a client workplace. Two practical points: a credit is only as good as the invoice behind it, so a card statement showing an amount and a vendor name is not enough, and anything used partly privately is claimed on the business extent rather than in full.
Is the Quick Method worth it for a consulting practice?
Sometimes, and it is arithmetic rather than a rule. The Quick Method is available only where annual taxable supplies stay within $400,000, and the remittance rate depends on the province and the type of business rather than being one national figure, so we will not quote you a rate on a web page. The trade-off is what matters: the method bars credits on your operating inputs while leaving credits on capital property available. For a practice whose largest recoverable costs are data licences and software subscriptions rather than equipment, giving up those credits can cost more than the method saves. We run both on your own figures before choosing, and you can try the arithmetic yourself with our Quick Method calculator.
Am I a personal services business if I work on a fractional retainer?
It is the right question to ask, and we will not answer it on a web page because it turns on your actual engagement. ITA subsection 125(7) defines a personal services business as one where an individual — the incorporated employee — performs the services on behalf of the corporation, that individual or a related person is a specified shareholder, and the individual would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the person receiving the services but for the existence of the corporation. The rules do not apply where the corporation employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year, or where the services are supplied to an associated corporation. A fractional engagement with one client, running full time for a long period, on the client's systems and under the client's direction, is the fact pattern worth looking at carefully. Several concurrent clients, your own tools and premises, a real chance of profit and risk of loss, and your own insurance all point the other way. We look at the engagement and tell you plainly.
What happens if the CRA says I am one?
Two things, and the second is worse than the first. The small business deduction is denied on that income, so the corporate rate advantage you incorporated for is gone. Then ITA paragraph 18(1)(p) restricts what the corporation may deduct against that income to the salary and benefits of the incorporated employee and a short list of specified amounts. Ordinary business expenses — rent, professional fees, software, most vehicle costs — are denied. The effect is that the corporation is taxed at a high rate on something close to its gross revenue, which is usually a worse outcome than never having incorporated. That is why we look at this before anything else on a fractional file, and why the independence evidence is worth building while the engagement is live rather than assembling it after a question arrives.
Should I incorporate as an HR consultant?
Only once we have looked at your client mix, because for this practice the two questions are inseparable. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the first $500,000 of active business income, and it gives you a separate legal entity. But a corporation whose revenue comes mostly from one long fractional retainer is exactly the arrangement the personal services business rules are aimed at, and if they apply the incorporation can leave you worse off than before. The honest answer also depends on how much you actually need to draw: the advantage lies in deferral on income you leave in the company, and a consultant who takes out everything they earn gets much less from it than the general advice suggests. We model it on your engagements and your draw, and we say plainly when the answer is not yet.
How do I pay an associate investigator, and do I issue a T4A?
If the associate is genuinely independent, you pay the invoice and report the fees for services on a T4A, with no CPP or EI withheld. Whether they are genuinely independent is decided on the whole relationship — control over how the work is done, ownership of tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration into your practice — and it is a question of fact rather than a matter of what the agreement calls them. An associate who works forty weeks a year on your files, to your schedule, on your templates and in your case management system is on the wrong side of most of those factors, whatever the paperwork says. Where the arrangement is employment in substance, we say so and set up payroll with source deductions and T4s from the first payment, because correcting it after a reassessment costs considerably more.
