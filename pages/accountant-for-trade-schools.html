Business and Corporate Tax Filing

  • We prepare your corporation's T2 or your Form T2125, with the small business deduction under ITA section 125 applied against the $500,000 limit on the income from your active business.
  • A school's taxable income rarely matches its bank balance, because tuition taken in August for a programme running to April is not August's income and the equipment bought in between is not an expense.
  • We set instalments against the tax actually owing rather than against a cash position that swings with the intake calendar, which is how schools end up paying instalment interest in a profitable year.
  • We choose the fiscal year end deliberately. A school whose intakes cluster in September and January is better served by a year end that falls between them than by December 31 out of habit.
  • And we keep the six years of records ITA section 230 requires, which for a trade school means the equipment register and the consumables counts as much as the invoices and the bank statements.
2

Exempt Supplies and the Credit You Cannot Claim

  • This is the provision that shapes your whole balance sheet. Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, and vocational instruction leading to a trade certificate sits inside it.
  • Exemption sounds like relief and is nothing of the sort. You charge no tax on exempt tuition, which also means you claim no input tax credits on the inputs used to make that supply.
  • Which bites hardest on a school like yours. A welding school fitting out eight bays, buying hoists, lathes, extraction and a plasma table pays the HST on all of it and keeps none of it back.
  • So the tax is part of the capital cost rather than a recoverable amount, and a capital budget built without it is understated by a double-digit percentage before anyone has negotiated a price.
  • We settle the characterisation on your actual programmes rather than asserting it, because whether a particular course is an exempt supply and whether you are a vocational school as defined are questions of fact.
3

Mixed Supplies: Tools, Kits and Non-Vocational Courses

  • Almost no trade school is purely exempt, which is the saving grace. Tool and kit sales to students are generally taxable supplies of goods, and so is instruction that is not vocational in the statutory sense.
  • A weekend hobby course, a corporate upskilling day, equipment rental to a local contractor and shop time sold to the public all sit on the taxable side and all carry credits with them.
  • Which means your credits get apportioned rather than claimed or forgone wholesale, on a basis that is fair and reasonable on your facts, documented when adopted and then applied the same way every period.
  • The practical consequence is that your chart of accounts has to separate exempt tuition from taxable sales at the point of entry, because nobody can reconstruct that split reliably from a year's bank feed.
  • And a tuition fee that bundles a tool kit into one price needs looking at, because a single amount covering an exempt supply and a taxable one is not a question you want answered on audit.
4

The Shop: Capital Cost Allowance on Equipment

  • A trade school is a capital-intensive business wearing an education label. Welders, lathes, mills, hoists, HVAC trainers, electrical panels and diagnostic rigs are capital property, not supplies, however the invoice reads.
  • So the cost is recovered through capital cost allowance over the asset's life rather than deducted in the year of purchase, which is the single largest difference between your statements and your return.
  • We maintain a proper asset register showing each item, when it was acquired, its cost including the unrecoverable tax, the class it sits in and the undepreciated balance carried forward.
  • We name no capital cost allowance rate on this page because the classes and rates turn on what the asset actually is, and a rate quoted from a website is how schools end up amending returns.
  • Repairs and maintenance on a hard-used shop are current costs and we keep them clearly apart from improvements that add to an asset, which is a line that moves with the facts rather than the invoice heading.
5

Leasehold Improvements and Fitting Out the Bays

  • Fitting out a shop in leased premises is its own accounting problem. Ventilation and extraction, three-phase power, concrete pads, welding curtains, overhead cranes and compressed air lines are leasehold improvements rather than repairs.
  • They are capital property claimed over time rather than expensed in the year the contractor is paid, and the HST on all of it stays with you to the extent the premises serve exempt instruction.
  • Which makes the lease term matter in a way most schools do not expect, because the recovery period for the improvement is tied to the lease rather than to how long the ventilation will last.
  • We separate the genuine improvements from the repair work and the moveable equipment in the same build, because a single contractor invoice routinely contains all three and nobody splits it afterwards.
  • And we account for a landlord inducement or a rent-free fitting-out period properly rather than treating it as a windfall, because it affects both the improvement's cost and the rent you deduct.
6

Consumables as Inventory

  • A trade school burns through materials in a way no classroom does: welding rod and wire, shielding gas, sheet steel, lumber, copper pipe, refrigerant, abrasives, filters, fasteners and the contents of every student kit.
  • Most schools expense all of it on purchase, which overstates cost in a month of bulk buying and understates it in the month the shop actually consumes what was bought.
  • ITA subsection 10(1) requires inventory to be valued for the purpose of computing income from a business, so the materials on hand at year end are an asset rather than a cost already taken.
  • Which means a count. Not a perfect one, but a real one, done consistently, so the cost of materials consumed per student is a figure you can price a programme from rather than guess at.
  • We also separate the kits you sell to students from the consumables the shop burns, because one is inventory held for resale on the taxable side and the other is a cost of exempt instruction.
7

Scrap, Disposals and Obsolete Trainers

  • The shop generates scrap, and scrap has value. Steel offcuts, copper, aluminium and the remains of practice pieces sold to a metal dealer are business income rather than a tidy-up nobody needs to record.
  • Those receipts are often paid in cash and often go unrecorded, which is a small amount with a disproportionate consequence if an auditor finds the dealer's records and not yours.
  • The disposal side matters more. Training equipment goes obsolete when an industry standard moves, and a trainer rig that no longer teaches anything current still sits in your capital accounts at a written-down value.
  • Disposing of it has a tax consequence that depends on what you get for it against that balance, and we work it out properly rather than letting a line simply vanish from the register.
  • We also deal with equipment donated to the school or bought at auction without a clean invoice, because an asset has to enter the register at a defensible cost before it can be claimed at all.
8

Programme Approval and Accreditation Costs

  • Getting a programme approved costs real money: consultants, curriculum development, submission work, inspections and the staff time of whoever shepherds it through. The question is whether any of that is deductible now.
  • ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) prohibits the deduction of an outlay of capital or a payment on account of capital except as the Act expressly permits, which is where a one-off approval cost usually lands.
  • Class 14.1 is where intangible property of this kind generally sits, and we state no rate for it here because the characterisation has to be settled before any rate is worth discussing.
  • Ordinary annual renewal fees and the routine updating of an existing curriculum are a different matter and are generally current, so the distinction we care about is one-off establishment against recurring maintenance.
  • We make that call deliberately on each programme and record why, because a school that expenses every approval cost and a school that capitalises every one are both wrong in different directions.
9

Journeyperson Instructors: Contractor or Employee

  • The hardest classification call in this sector. A journeyperson who teaches two nights a week and runs a trade business the rest of the time genuinely looks like a contractor from one angle and an employee from another.
  • It is decided on the whole relationship rather than the agreement's label: control over how the teaching is done, who provides the tools and the shop, chance of profit, risk of loss, and integration.
  • Most of those factors point one way for an instructor teaching your approved curriculum, on your timetable, in your shop, with your equipment, to students who are your customers rather than theirs.
  • Which does not settle it, because the instructor's own trade business, their own insurance and their genuine ability to refuse a term all point the other way and carry weight.
  • We work it through on the actual arrangement rather than labelling everyone a contractor for convenience, and we tell you where the exposure sits, because the reassessment lands on the school as the payer.
10

Payroll for Instructors and Shop Staff

  • Where the answer is employment, we set payroll up properly from the first pay: source deductions calculated and remitted on the right schedule, and T4 slips filed on time at the end of February.
  • Where it is genuinely contract, we prepare the T4A slips on time, because a school that pays a dozen instructors on invoices and files nothing collects penalties per slip rather than once.
  • Instructor pay in a trade school is rarely a flat salary, so we handle the per-course rates, the hourly shop supervision, the overtime through a compressed intake and the term-to-term variation in hours.
  • We deal with the WSIB position for shop staff and instructors rather than leaving it, because a school running welding and machining bays is not a workplace where coverage is a paperwork detail.
  • And we show instructional cost per programme in your statements, because a school that cannot see what a cohort costs to teach cannot tell which of its programmes is subsidising the others.
11

Incorporation and Structure

  • Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit on active business income, the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates, and limited liability around a shop full of hazards.
  • For a capital-intensive school the retained-earnings point is the substantive one, because the next intake's equipment is funded out of after-tax profit and the rate that profit bears decides how fast you can re-equip.
  • We model salary against dividends for the owners, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions, and on whether the income record will support borrowing against the next fit-out.
  • Where the premises or the heavy equipment are owned rather than leased, we look at whether holding them in the operating company is the right answer or whether a separate structure makes sense.
  • We discuss no capital gains inclusion rate and no lifetime capital gains exemption amount on this page, because those figures have moved and we will not publish one we have not confirmed with you.
12

CRA Audit Resolution

  • When your exempt and taxable split is examined, we produce the documented apportionment basis and the invoices behind it, which is why we settle that basis when we adopt it rather than at audit.
  • When an instructor is reassessed as your employee, we handle the source deduction exposure and argue the classification on the real relationship rather than conceding the point to close the file quickly.
  • When your capital cost allowance claims or an asset's classification are questioned, we produce the register, the invoices and the reasoning that put each item in the class it sits in.
  • When the consumables or the scrap receipts are looked at, we produce the counts and the dealer records, which is a short conversation if you kept them and a long one if you did not.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment and pursue taxpayer relief on Form RC4288, cancelling penalties and interest where a prior preparer's error was the cause.

Case Studies: Trade Schools

Brampton Welding School — A Capital Budget Short by the Tax

The problem: A Brampton welding school was expanding from four bays to ten and had built its capital budget from supplier quotes, assuming the HST would come back as input tax credits the way it did at the owner's previous construction business. Nothing in the plan accounted for the fact that tuition on its vocational programmes was exempt, so the tax on the hoists, extraction system, plasma table and the whole fit-out was permanent cost.

What we did: We settled the exempt and taxable characterisation on the actual programme mix, identified the genuinely taxable activities — hobby weekend courses, corporate upskilling days and shop rental to local contractors — and set a documented apportionment basis for the shared inputs. Then we rebuilt the capital budget on real after-tax cost and separated the leasehold improvements from the moveable equipment in the contractor's single invoice.

The result:

  • Capital budget corrected before the equipment was ordered
  • Apportionment basis documented, credits claimed on the taxable side
  • Improvements and moveable equipment split on the one invoice

Hamilton Automotive School — Instructors on Invoices

The problem: A Hamilton automotive training school paid eleven journeyperson instructors on invoices, with no T4A slips filed for any of them over three years. All eleven taught the school's approved curriculum, on the school's timetable, in the school's shop, using the school's equipment. The owner had been told by a previous bookkeeper that anyone with their own trade business is automatically a contractor.

What we did: We assessed each arrangement on its real facts rather than as a group, and found the eleven were genuinely different: some ran substantial independent trade businesses and declined terms freely, others taught nothing but this school's courses year round. We filed the outstanding T4A slips, moved the clearest cases onto payroll from the next term, and pursued relief on the slip penalties.

The result:

  • Eleven arrangements assessed individually, not as one group
  • Outstanding T4A slips filed and relief pursued on penalties
  • Clearest cases moved to payroll before the next intake

London HVAC School — Trainers That Vanished

The problem: A London HVAC and refrigeration school had replaced a generation of refrigerant trainer rigs when the industry standard changed, scrapping the old ones. The old rigs had simply been deleted from the fixed asset listing by a bookkeeper tidying up, with no disposal entry at all, while the school's substantial undepreciated balances continued being claimed. Scrap receipts from the copper had been taken in cash and never recorded.

What we did: We reconstructed the asset register from purchase invoices, recorded the disposals properly against their undepreciated balances, corrected the capital cost allowance claimed in the affected years, and brought the scrap receipts into income. We then set up a register that tracks acquisition, class, balance and disposal so the next replacement cycle is handled as it happens.

The result:

  • Asset register reconstructed and disposals properly recorded
  • Scrap receipts brought into income before anyone asked
  • A register in place for the next replacement cycle

Trade School Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do we charge HST on trade school tuition?
Often not, and that is the starting point for everything else on your file. Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, and the exemption reaches instruction in a course leading to a certificate or diploma attesting to competence to practise a trade or vocation, where the supplier is a vocational school as the legislation defines that term. Vocational instruction at a trade school is the kind of supply the provision was written for. But whether a particular programme qualifies, and whether your school meets the statutory definition, are questions of fact that we settle on your actual facts rather than assert on a web page. Getting it wrong in either direction is expensive: charging tax you should not have charged, or failing to charge tax you should have, both land on you rather than on your students.
If our tuition is exempt, can we claim the HST on our equipment?
No, and this is the single most consequential fact about a trade school's economics. Input tax credits are available to a registrant to the extent an input is acquired for use in a commercial activity, and making exempt supplies is not a commercial activity for this purpose. So the HST on the hoists, the lathes, the extraction system, the plasma table, the trainer rigs, the shop rent and the consumables used in exempt instruction is not recoverable. It is part of the cost. For a school fitting out a shop, that means a capital budget built from supplier quotes alone is understated by a double-digit percentage before anyone has negotiated a price — and we have seen schools order equipment on exactly that assumption. The partial relief is that almost no trade school is purely exempt; see the next answer.
What about the tool kits we sell to students?
Generally taxable, and that matters more than the dollars suggest, because taxable supplies carry credits with them. A sale of goods to a student is a supply of goods rather than educational instruction, so tool kits, consumable packs, safety gear and textbooks sold separately typically sit on the taxable side. So does instruction that is not vocational in the statutory sense — a weekend hobby welding course, a corporate upskilling day, equipment rental to a local contractor, shop time sold to the public. Which makes almost every trade school a mixed supplier, with credits apportioned rather than claimed or forgone wholesale. Two practical consequences: your chart of accounts has to separate exempt tuition from taxable sales at the point of entry, and a tuition fee that bundles a kit into one price needs looking at before an auditor looks at it for you.
How do we account for shop equipment — expense or capital?
Capital, almost always, and this is the largest difference between your statements and your tax return. Welders, lathes, mills, hoists, HVAC and refrigeration trainers, electrical panels, diagnostic equipment and CNC machines are capital property whatever the invoice calls them, so their cost is recovered through capital cost allowance over the asset's life rather than deducted in the year you bought it. The cost going into the register includes the HST you could not recover, which on an exempt school is a real addition. We maintain a register showing each item, its acquisition date, its cost, the class it sits in and the undepreciated balance carried forward. We deliberately state no capital cost allowance rate on this page, because the class an asset belongs in turns on what the asset actually is, and a rate taken from a website is how schools end up amending returns.
What about fitting out the shop in a leased building?
Those are leasehold improvements, which are their own category and routinely misposted. Ventilation and fume extraction, three-phase power, concrete pads, welding curtains, overhead cranes, compressed air lines and reinforced flooring are capital property rather than repairs, claimed over time rather than expensed when the contractor is paid. The recovery period is tied to the lease term rather than to how long the ventilation will physically last, which surprises most schools and is worth knowing before you sign a short lease on a shop you intend to fit out heavily. One practical point: a single contractor invoice for a shop build routinely contains genuine improvements, ordinary repair work and moveable equipment all at once, and nobody splits it afterwards. We split it at the time. We also account for a landlord inducement or rent-free fitting-out period properly, because it affects both the improvement's cost and the rent you deduct.
Do we really need to count welding rod and consumables?
Yes, and not only for the tax. ITA subsection 10(1) requires inventory to be valued for the purpose of computing income from a business, so the rod, wire, shielding gas, sheet steel, lumber, copper pipe, refrigerant, abrasives and kit stock on hand at your year end is an asset rather than a cost you already took. A school that expenses everything on purchase overstates cost in a month of bulk buying and understates it in the month the shop actually consumes what was bought — which makes the monthly statements useless for the one decision that matters, namely what a cohort costs to teach. The count need not be perfect. It needs to be real and done the same way each year. We also separate the kits held for resale on the taxable side from the consumables the shop burns in exempt instruction, because they behave differently for both income and tax purposes.
What do we do about scrap sales?
Record them. Steel offcuts, copper, aluminium and the remains of students' practice pieces sold to a metal dealer are business income, not a tidy-up nobody needs to account for. The amounts are usually modest and often paid in cash, which is exactly why they go unrecorded — and exactly why they are a disproportionate problem if an auditor has the dealer's records and you do not have yours. An unrecorded cash receipt invites a question about what else was not recorded, and answering that question costs far more than the scrap was worth. So we bring scrap into income as a line of its own, which also tells you something useful: scrap volume is a reasonable proxy for material waste per cohort, and a school that tracks it can see which programme is burning through steel faster than its fee assumed.
What happens when a trainer rig becomes obsolete?
It has to be disposed of properly in your records, not deleted from them. Training equipment goes obsolete when an industry standard moves — a refrigerant changes, a code is updated, a vehicle platform is retired — and a rig that no longer teaches anything current is still sitting in your capital accounts at an undepreciated value. Scrapping it, selling it, or giving it to another school each has a tax consequence that depends on what you receive against that balance. What we see instead is a bookkeeper tidying the fixed asset listing, the old rigs simply vanishing, and capital cost allowance continuing to be claimed on balances for equipment that left the building two years ago. That is a correction waiting to happen. We also handle the opposite case: equipment donated to the school or bought at an auction without a clean invoice still has to enter the register at a defensible cost before it can be claimed.
Can we deduct the cost of getting a programme approved?
Some of it, probably not all of it, and the distinction is worth getting right. Getting a programme approved costs real money — consultants, curriculum development, submission work, inspections, and the time of whoever shepherds it through. ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) prohibits the deduction of an outlay of capital or a payment on account of capital except as the Act expressly permits, and a one-off cost of establishing a new approved programme usually lands on the capital side. Class 14.1 is where intangible property of this kind generally sits; we state no rate for it here, because the characterisation has to be settled before a rate is worth discussing. Ordinary annual renewal fees and routine updating of an existing curriculum are a different matter and are generally current. So the line we care about is one-off establishment against recurring maintenance, and we make that call programme by programme and record why.
Are our journeyperson instructors contractors or employees?
It is the hardest classification question in this sector and it genuinely does not have a standard answer. A journeyperson who teaches two nights a week and runs a trade business the rest of the time looks like a contractor from one angle and an employee from another. The question is decided on the whole relationship rather than the agreement's label: control over how the teaching is done, who provides the tools and premises, chance of profit, risk of loss, and integration into the business. Most of those point one way for someone teaching your approved curriculum, on your timetable, in your shop, with your equipment, to students who are your customers. Their own trade business, their own insurance and a genuine ability to refuse a term point the other way and carry real weight. We assess each arrangement individually rather than labelling everyone a contractor, because the reassessment lands on the school as the payer.
Should the school be incorporated?
Usually yes, and for a reason specific to a capital-intensive school. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit on active business income, limited liability around a shop full of genuine hazards, and the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates. That last point is the substantive one for you: the next intake's equipment is funded out of after-tax profit, so the rate that profit bears directly determines how fast you can re-equip. We model salary against dividends for the owners, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions, and on whether the income record will support borrowing against the next fit-out. Where premises or heavy equipment are owned rather than leased we look at whether they belong in the operating company. We publish no capital gains inclusion rate and no lifetime capital gains exemption amount here, and will confirm the current figures with you directly.
How should we choose our fiscal year end?
Around your intakes, not out of habit. A school whose cohorts start in September and January has a cash position that swings hard across the year, with tuition arriving before programmes run and equipment bought in between, and a December 31 year end can land in the middle of that for no good reason. A year end that falls between intakes gives you a cleaner cut: fewer part-finished programmes to apportion, a consumables count you can actually do with the shop quiet, and a figure for the year that reflects a complete teaching cycle rather than half of two. It also spreads the compliance work away from the busiest administrative weeks. For an incorporated school the choice is genuinely yours within the rules, so it is worth making deliberately once rather than inheriting it. We also use it as a planning lever on equipment timing, since when an asset becomes available for use affects the year it starts being claimed.
