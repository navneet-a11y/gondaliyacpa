Income or Capital Characterisation

  • Profit from an adventure or concern in the nature of trade is business income under ITA section 9 and fully taxable, while a genuine capital gain is not, so the characterisation decides your tax bill.
  • The answer turns on the badges of trade rather than on what you call yourself: your intention when you acquired the item, how often you transact, how long you held it, and what you did to it.
  • Buying ten pairs of the same sneaker on release day and listing them the same week is not the same transaction as selling one pair you wore for two years, and the law treats them differently.
  • We will not tell you on a web page which side of that line your sales fall on, because it is a question of fact, and a conclusion asserted without the facts is the kind that collapses on review.
  • What we do is document the position at the time rather than reconstructing an argument afterwards, because the evidence of your intention is far more persuasive when it was written before CRA asked.
3

Personal-Use and Listed Personal Property

  • Property used primarily for your own enjoyment is personal-use property under ITA section 54, and ITA subsection 46(1) deems both its cost and its proceeds to be not less than $1,000.
  • That rule is why clearing out your own closet usually produces no taxable gain at all: an item that cost under a thousand dollars and sold for under a thousand dollars yields nothing to report.
  • It cuts the other way too. A loss on personal-use property is denied outright by ITA subparagraph 40(2)(g)(iii), so selling your own things for less than you paid gives you nothing deductible.
  • Listed personal property is the subset that catches collectors: art, jewellery, rare books, stamps and coins. ITA section 41 confines an LPP loss to LPP gains, carried back three years and forward seven.
  • Most of our collectible clients are inside this regime for part of their activity and running a business with the rest, in the same year, and we separate the two rather than averaging them.
4

Inventory Valuation and Dead Stock

  • Where the activity is a business, your goods are inventory, and ITA subsection 10(1) values them at the lower of cost and fair market value at each year end rather than at what you hope to get.
  • That provision is the most valuable one on this page, because it is what lets you write stock down. A pallet of phone cases nobody wants is not worth what you paid, and the write-down is real.
  • We run a real year-end count rather than a plug figure, because opening and closing inventory sit directly in your cost of goods sold and an estimate there moves your taxable income by the same amount.
  • We age the stock so you can see what has sat unsold for a year or more, which is both the write-down conversation and the single most useful management number a reseller can have.
  • Damaged, counterfeit-flagged or returned goods are dealt with on the facts and documented when it happens, rather than quietly vanishing from the count and leaving an unexplained gap a reviewer will find.
5

Cost of Goods Sold Without Invoices

  • This is the problem no other e-commerce page has to solve. Your stock came from a thrift store, a garage sale, an estate auction or a stranger on Marketplace, and most of it was cash with no receipt.
  • ITA section 230 requires records, and the consequence of not having them is not a scolding. With no supportable cost base there is nothing to deduct, and an assessment can be raised on your gross proceeds.
  • So we set you up with a contemporaneous sourcing log: date, place, what you bought, what you paid, and a photograph. Thirty seconds at the till, and it is the record that saves the deduction.
  • For unique secondhand items we cost by specific identification, because each piece has its own price and averaging them would make every individual margin meaningless and every write-down impossible to support.
  • For a liquidation pallet bought at one price we allocate that cost across the units on a reasonable basis and apply the same basis every time, since consistency is most of what makes an allocation defensible.
6

Marketplace Payout Reconciliation

  • A marketplace payout is not your revenue. It is gross sales less selling fees, less shipping labels, less refunds, less chargebacks, and sometimes less tax the platform collected and remitted for you.
  • A reseller who books the deposit as sales has understated revenue and expenses in the same entry, repeated monthly for years, and has probably also measured the GST/HST threshold against the wrong number.
  • We build a monthly bridge from each payout back to gross sales with every deduction identified, and we do it across all the marketplaces you sell on rather than only the biggest one.
  • Selling on four platforms in two currencies is the ordinary case for a working reseller, not the complicated one, so the reconciliation handles currency on a consistent and documented basis from the start.
  • The bridge also gives you margin by platform, which usually surprises people: the marketplace that moves the most volume is frequently not the one that pays you best after fees and shipping.
7

GST/HST Registration and Place of Supply

  • Registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, and we pin down the exact quarter you crossed from the platform exports.
  • Used goods are not exempt. A registrant selling secondhand items in the course of a commercial activity is making a taxable supply at the ordinary rate, and there is no general secondhand exemption.
  • Canadian sales carry the rate for the buyer's province under the place of supply rules, with Ontario at 13%, so one Ontario reseller can be charging several different rates in the same week.
  • Sales shipped outside Canada are generally zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, but they still count toward the threshold, which catches sellers whose buyers are mostly American.
  • We also run the Quick Method against the regular method on your figures, capped at $400,000 of annual taxable supplies, which on a reseller buying cash stock from unregistered sellers often wins.
8

Importing, Division III GST and Duty

  • Buy a pallet from Ohio or a case of sneakers from a United States seller and Division III GST becomes payable at the border under Excise Tax Act section 212, by you as the importer.
  • Where the goods are for use in your commercial activity that import GST is generally recoverable as an input tax credit, so the cash cost is a timing problem rather than a permanent one.
  • That is a genuinely different regime from the one a stockless seller faces, where there are no goods crossing a border and the question is self-assessment on imported services and intangibles instead.
  • Customs duty and the value for duty are separate determinations from the GST, and they are not ours. We state no tariff classification or duty rate and we tell you to use a customs broker.
  • What we do insist on is that the landed cost of imported stock includes the duty, the freight and the brokerage, because a margin calculated on the invoice price alone is simply wrong.
9

Platform Reporting and What CRA Already Has

  • Part XX of the Income Tax Act requires a reportable platform operator to collect each reportable seller's identifying information and report the total consideration paid to them to the CRA every year.
  • The return is due by 31 January following the calendar year reported, which means the data on your sales generally reaches the CRA before you have filed the return that reports them.
  • That single fact has retired the oldest assumption in reselling. “They will never know” was never good advice and it is now simply false, which changes the arithmetic on coming forward voluntarily.
  • There is a de minimis exclusion for the smallest sellers of goods. We check its thresholds against the rules on your actual numbers rather than publishing a figure here that we have not verified.
  • Separately, the distribution platform operator rules can make the marketplace rather than you responsible for collecting GST/HST on a sale, and we will not assert which named platform falls where without reading your agreement.
10

Incorporation When It Is Time

  • We model incorporation on your actual numbers rather than on a rule of thumb, because the answer for someone clearing a few thousand a year is genuinely different from one clearing six figures.
  • Where it makes sense we apply the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit and track that limit across any associated corporations that happen to be in the picture.
  • We model salary against dividends for the owner, including the effect on your RRSP room and on whether you are building the income record a mortgage lender will actually want to see.
  • We pick a fiscal year end deliberately, which for a reseller whose stock peaks before the holidays is a real planning lever rather than an administrative detail nobody thinks about twice.
  • And we say plainly when the answer is to stay a sole proprietor for now, because the cost and compliance of a corporation is a poor trade for a business still finding its footing.
11

Catch-Up Filing and Registration

  • We reconstruct unfiled years from marketplace exports, payment processor statements and whatever sourcing evidence exists, rebuilding the six years ITA section 230 requires from source data rather than from estimates.
  • Late filing costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month to twelve months, so we file the oldest unfiled year first to stop the penalty compounding and limit the arrears interest.
  • We fix a GST/HST registration that should have happened earlier, quantify the unregistered period honestly and deal with it, rather than starting the clock from today and hoping nobody looks backwards.
  • Here is the part that surprises people: the corrected years often produce far less tax than feared, because the cost of goods, the shipping, the fees and the home storage were never claimed.
  • Where it is appropriate we bring the position forward on Form RC199 before the CRA contacts you, which cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief, with no lecture from us.

Case Studies: Online Resellers

Toronto Sneaker Reseller — Assessed on Gross Proceeds

The problem: A Toronto reseller had moved a substantial volume of sneakers and streetwear over three years, almost all of it sourced in cash at consignment shops and from private sellers. He had kept nothing: no receipts, no log, no photographs. When a review opened, the CRA had his gross sales from the platform and he had no supportable cost base to set against it, so the proposed assessment was effectively on his revenue.

What we did: We rebuilt what could honestly be rebuilt — bank and card withdrawals matched to buying trips, consignment shop records, message threads with private sellers, listing photographs showing condition and acquisition dates — and presented a documented, conservative cost of goods rather than an estimate. We then put the sourcing log in place so the next three years would never be in this position.

The result:

  • A supportable cost of goods accepted in place of a gross assessment
  • Three years restated on documented figures rather than estimates
  • Contemporaneous sourcing log in place from that month on

Mississauga Thrift Seller — Taxed on Her Own Wardrobe

The problem: A Mississauga seller listed both her own clothing and furniture and items she bought specifically to resell, all through the same two accounts. A previous preparer had treated every dollar of proceeds as business income, which meant she was paying tax on the sale of her own used belongings. Nobody had mentioned the personal-use property rules to her, and nobody had separated the two activities.

What we did: We separated the activity on the facts: items she had owned and used, against items acquired for resale. For the first group we applied the personal-use property rules, where ITA subsection 46(1) deems cost and proceeds to be at least $1,000 and ordinary household items therefore produce no gain. The second group was properly a business, with cost of goods, fees and shipping all claimed.

The result:

  • Proceeds from her own belongings removed from business income
  • The genuine resale activity reported with its real costs claimed
  • Two open years adjusted, producing a refund

Ottawa Pallet Buyer — A Garage Full of Unsold Stock

The problem: An incorporated Ottawa reseller bought liquidation pallets from the United States, allocated no cost to individual units and had never counted his stock. He had expensed every pallet in the year he bought it, so his profit swung wildly between years for no commercial reason. Roughly a third of his garage was goods that had not sold in two years, and he had never claimed the GST paid at the border on any import.

What we did: We built an inventory system with a reasonable, consistently applied allocation of each pallet price across its units, ran a real count, aged it, and wrote the dead stock down to fair market value as ITA subsection 10(1) permits. We then recovered the Division III import GST as input tax credits for the open periods and brought duty, freight and brokerage into landed cost.

The result:

  • Dead stock written down to fair market value under ITA subsection 10(1)
  • Import GST recovered as input tax credits for the open periods
  • Landed cost including duty, freight and brokerage, so margin is real

Online Reseller Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I pay tax on reselling in Canada?
If you buy things in order to sell them at a profit, yes. That is an adventure or concern in the nature of trade, the profit is business income under ITA section 9, and it is fully taxable. The nuance is that a reseller usually has two different activities running through the same account: items bought to resell, and items they owned and used themselves. Those are taxed under completely different rules, and a preparer who lumps them together will overstate your income in one direction or your risk in the other. What matters most practically is not the sales figure — the platform has already reported that to the CRA — but the cost figure. Everything you paid for the stock, the shipping, the selling fees, the packaging, the mileage to go and get it and the share of your home used to store it is deductible, and the single reason resellers overpay is that none of it was recorded.
Is reselling a hobby or a business?
The useful question is not hobby against business but whether what you are doing is an adventure in the nature of trade, and that is decided on the badges of trade rather than on how seriously you take it. The factors are your intention when you acquired the item, how often you transact, how long you held it, the nature of the thing itself, what work you did to it, and how the activity relates to the rest of your working life. Buying ten of something on release day and listing them that week points one way; selling a jacket you wore for two years points the other. It is a question of fact, so we will not give you a conclusion on a web page. What we will do is look at your actual pattern, tell you plainly which side it sits on — including when that is unwelcome — and document the reasoning at the time rather than building an argument after a letter arrives.
Do I pay tax when I sell my own clothes and furniture?
Usually not, and this is the most useful thing on this page for most people. Property used primarily for your own enjoyment is personal-use property under ITA section 54, and ITA subsection 46(1) deems both its adjusted cost base and its proceeds of disposition to be not less than $1,000. The effect is that an ordinary item — a sofa, a coat, a bicycle — that cost under a thousand dollars and sold for under a thousand dollars produces no taxable gain at all. The rule cuts both ways, though, and people forget the second half: a loss on personal-use property is denied outright by ITA subparagraph 40(2)(g)(iii). Selling your own things for less than you paid gives you nothing deductible, no matter how much you lost. Where it gets interesting is a genuinely valuable item, or a collection, which can exceed that threshold and be taxable — so the answer is not simply “used stuff is never taxed”.
Is a sneaker flip a capital gain or business income?
On most reseller patterns it is business income, fully taxable, rather than a capital gain. Entering a release-day draw with the intention of listing the pair immediately is close to the textbook description of an adventure in the nature of trade: the intention at acquisition was resale, the holding period is short, and the frequency is high. A single pair bought to wear, kept for two years and then sold because it no longer fits is a genuinely different transaction, and may well sit in the personal-use property rules instead. Most of our sneaker clients are doing both, which is why we separate the activity rather than averaging it. We will not characterise your sales on a web page, because it is a question of fact and an asserted conclusion with no facts behind it is exactly the kind that collapses on review. What we do is apply the badges of trade to your real pattern and write the reasoning down at the time.
How do I prove what my stock cost if I paid cash?
This is the central problem of a reseller file and it has a boring answer: a log, kept as you buy. ITA section 230 requires records for six years, and the consequence of not having them is not a scolding — with no supportable cost base there is nothing to deduct from the proceeds the platform reported, and an assessment can be raised on your gross. We have had to defend exactly that position and it is not a good place to argue from. So the record we ask for is five fields: date, place, what you bought, what you paid, and a photograph. Thirty seconds at the till. Where the records were never kept, not all is lost: cash withdrawals matched to buying trips, consignment and auction receipts, message threads with private sellers and your own listing photographs showing condition and timing can support a documented, conservative figure. It is slower, more expensive and less persuasive than the log would have been, but it beats being assessed on revenue.
How do I value inventory that will not sell?
At the lower of cost and fair market value, which is the single most valuable provision on this page. Where your activity is a business, the goods you hold are inventory, and ITA subsection 10(1) values them at the lower of what they cost you and what they are actually worth at your year end. That is what lets you write down stock that has stopped moving: a pallet of phone cases nobody wants is not worth what you paid for it, and the write-down is a real deduction rather than a concession. The two things that make it stand up are a genuine count and evidence of value. We run the count rather than using a plug figure, because opening and closing inventory sit directly in your cost of goods sold and an estimate there shifts your taxable income by the same amount. Then we age the stock so you can see what has sat unsold for a year or more, which is both the write-down conversation and the most useful management number a reseller can have.
Does eBay report my sales to the CRA?
Platforms report, and you should assume yours does. Part XX of the Income Tax Act requires a reportable platform operator to collect each reportable seller's identifying information and report the total consideration paid to them to the CRA every year, with the return due by 31 January following the calendar year reported. In practice that means the CRA generally has your gross sales figure before you have filed the return that reports it. One assumption in reselling is therefore finished: “they will never know” was never good advice and it is now simply false, which changes the arithmetic on coming forward voluntarily rather than waiting. There is a de minimis exclusion for the smallest sellers of goods, and we check its thresholds against the rules on your numbers rather than publishing a figure here we have not verified. What we will not do is tell you whether a particular named marketplace is or is not a reportable platform operator — that turns on the facts of the platform, and we check it rather than guess.
Do I need to register for GST/HST as a reseller?
Once your worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, yes. Two traps catch resellers specifically. The first is measuring the threshold against the money that landed in your bank. A marketplace payout is net of selling fees, shipping labels, refunds and chargebacks, so a seller watching deposits often crossed the real gross threshold a year before they noticed. The second is assuming export sales do not count: sales shipped outside Canada are generally zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, but they still count toward the threshold, so a reseller whose buyers are mostly American can be required to register while charging tax on very little. We pin down the exact quarter you crossed from the platform exports, register from the correct date, and deal with any unregistered period honestly rather than quietly starting the clock from today. We also check which of your marketplaces is collecting the tax on your behalf.
Do I charge HST on used items?
Yes. There is no general exemption for secondhand goods, and this surprises people more than almost anything else on the file. A registrant selling used items in the course of a commercial activity is making a taxable supply at the ordinary rate, exactly as if the goods were new. So once you are registered, the vintage jacket and the refurbished camera carry tax the same way a brand-new item would. The rate follows the place of supply rules, which set it by your buyer's province rather than yours, with Ontario at 13% — meaning one Ontario reseller can legitimately be charging several different rates in the same week, and a seller who applies 13% to every Canadian order has both overcharged some buyers and misreported the remittance. The real planning point for resellers is the other side: because so much of your stock comes from unregistered private sellers and carries no tax, you often have very little input tax credit, which is precisely the profile where the Quick Method tends to win.
Do I pay GST on goods I import, and can I get it back?
You pay it at the border and you generally get it back. Division III GST on goods imported into Canada is payable by the importer under Excise Tax Act section 212 at the time of importation, so a pallet from Ohio or a case from a United States seller carries tax before it reaches your garage. Where the goods are for use in your commercial activity, that import GST is generally recoverable as an input tax credit, which makes it a cash timing problem rather than a permanent cost — but only if you claim it, and we routinely find resellers who have paid it for years and never did. Note that this is a different regime from the one a stockless seller faces, where nothing crosses a border and the question is self-assessment on imported services and intangibles instead. Customs duty and the value for duty are separate determinations and they are not ours: we state no tariff classification or duty rate, and we will tell you to engage a customs broker. We do insist that duty, freight and brokerage go into landed cost.
Can I claim the room where I store my stock?
Usually yes, and most resellers qualify because the stock genuinely lives in a basement, a garage or a spare bedroom. Business-use-of-home expenses are deductible under ITA subsection 18(12), with one limit that bites in exactly the year people most want the deduction: the claim cannot exceed the income from that business, and any excess carries forward to a later year rather than being lost. So a first year with heavy buying and little revenue may see the claim capped and carried. The claim is based on the share of the home used for the activity, measured rather than estimated, and we keep the measurement and the reasoning on file, because a round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a review questions. The same discipline applies to your vehicle: the mileage to sourcing trips, the post office and the storage unit is deductible on a properly kept log, and the trips you also did your own shopping on are not wholly business.
I never reported my reselling income. What happens now?
You come forward, and it is usually far less painful than the dread suggests — particularly now that the platform reports your gross to the CRA anyway. Late filing costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month to twelve months, so we file the oldest unfiled year first to stop the penalty compounding. For years beyond the normal reach we bring the position forward on Form RC199 before the CRA contacts you — confirming the disclosure is genuinely voluntary is the single condition that makes the application valid — which cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief. Here is the part that surprises people: the corrected years frequently produce far less tax than feared, because nothing was ever claimed. Your cost of goods, the selling fees, the shipping labels, the packaging, the mileage, the home storage and any import GST were all available all along. We reconstruct the years from marketplace exports and whatever sourcing evidence exists, and we do it in one engagement with no lecture.
