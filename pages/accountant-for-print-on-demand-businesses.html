Accounting That Understands a Business With No Inventory

Four things make print-on-demand different from every other e-commerce file, and a preparer who treats you as a stocked retailer will get at least two of them wrong. At Gondaliya CPA we set them out plainly and say which questions belong to a specialist elsewhere.

📦

No Inventory to Value

Nothing to count or value under ITA subsection 10(1). Cost of sales is a per-order supplier charge, matched to the order rather than to the month the card cleared.

🌍

Your Printer Charges No Tax

A foreign supplier generally charges no GST/HST, so your input tax credits are far smaller than expected and self-assessment on imported supplies comes into play.

🏪

The Platform May Collect

Distribution platform operator rules can put the GST/HST obligation on the marketplace rather than on you, which changes what you register for and remit.

🎨

The Artwork Is the Asset

No warehouse, so the value sits in intellectual property. Routine design work is generally deductible; a lasting intangible asset is a capital expenditure.

Stay Compliant Across Platforms and Borders

The risk on a print-on-demand file is almost never the income. It is the sales tax — registering late, registering in the wrong places, or assuming the platform handled something it did not.

📋

Three Lines We Draw, and Why

First, we state no foreign sales tax rule: no United States rate, threshold or nexus test, and nothing on digital services taxes elsewhere. Those are real obligations and they belong to a specialist in that jurisdiction; naming a number we have not verified would be worse than saying nothing. Second, we do not assert which platform is a distribution platform operator. The rules are specific and the answer turns on the actual arrangement between you, the platform and the buyer, so we check it rather than guess. Third, we give no advice on design, trade mark or copyright clearance. Selling a design that infringes someone's mark is a legal exposure, not a tax one, and an accountant is the wrong person to reassure you about it.

✅

Your CRA and GST/HST Obligations

We manage your Form T2125 or your corporation's T2 with the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, GST/HST registration once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, place of supply rules that set the rate by your customer's province with Ontario at 13%, exports generally zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, input tax credits on the costs that actually carried tax, business-use-of-home under ITA subsection 18(12) limited to the income from the business, instalments, and the six years of records ITA section 230 requires.

📈

The Two Records We Ask You to Keep

Only two, and both are hard to rebuild. The first is a monthly reconciliation from platform payout to gross sales, because a payout is net of fees, refunds, chargebacks and sometimes tax the platform collected, and a business that books the deposit as revenue has understated its sales and its tax in the same stroke. The second is the supplier charge tied to the order it belongs to, so cost of sales lands in the period the revenue did. Those two records are the difference between a defensible margin and a guess, and they are also what a lender or a buyer will ask for first if you ever sell the brand.

Platform Payout Reconciliation

  • A platform payout is not revenue. It is gross sales less platform fees, less refunds, less chargebacks, and sometimes less tax the platform collected and remitted on your behalf.
  • A business that books the deposit as revenue has understated its sales, understated its expenses and quite possibly its tax, all in a single entry repeated every month for years.
  • We build a monthly bridge from payout back to gross sales with every deduction identified, which is the one record the rest of the file genuinely depends on.
  • We handle multiple storefronts and multiple currencies in the same reconciliation, since a seller on three platforms in two currencies is the normal case rather than the complicated one.
  • We keep that bridge in a form a lender or a buyer can read, because it is the first thing either will ask for and the thing most print-on-demand sellers cannot produce.

Export and Place of Supply

  • Sales shipped to customers outside Canada are generally zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, so no GST/HST is charged while the sale still counts as a taxable supply.

Case Studies: Print-on-Demand Businesses

Toronto Designer — Payouts Booked as Revenue

The problem: A Toronto designer selling apparel through two platforms had been recording the monthly deposit as her sales figure for three years. Her reported revenue was therefore net of platform fees, refunds and chargebacks, which understated both her sales and her expenses. She had never registered for GST/HST because her reported revenue looked comfortably under the threshold, and on the real gross figure she had crossed it well over a year earlier.

What we did: We rebuilt a monthly bridge from payout back to gross sales from the platform exports, established the exact quarter she crossed $30,000, registered her effective from the correct date and quantified the unregistered period honestly. We then ran the Quick Method against the regular method on her real numbers, and because her printer charged her no GST/HST, the Quick Method was clearly better.

The result:

  • Gross sales restated properly with fees shown as expenses
  • Late registration fixed from the correct date, not from today
  • Quick Method adopted after the comparison, not assumed

Mississauga Seller — Expecting Input Tax Credits That Did Not Exist

The problem: A Mississauga seller had registered for GST/HST voluntarily on the advice of a friend, expecting to recover tax on his biggest cost line — the per-order charges from his United States printer. That supplier charged no GST/HST at all. He was remitting on almost all of his Canadian sales with virtually nothing to offset, had not set money aside for it, and was three quarters behind on filings by the time he called.

What we did: We explained plainly why the credits he expected were never going to appear, worked through whether the imported taxable supply rules reached his arrangements, brought the outstanding returns up to date and switched him to the Quick Method, which suited a business with almost no taxable cost base. We then built the remittance into his pricing and a monthly set-aside.

The result:

  • Three quarters of outstanding GST/HST returns filed
  • Remittance basis changed to suit a no-credit cost structure
  • Tax built into pricing and a monthly set-aside established

Ottawa Brand — One Rate for Every Canadian Customer

The problem: An incorporated Ottawa merch brand had been charging 13% to every Canadian customer regardless of where the order shipped, and charging the same to customers in the United States and Europe. The first error overcharged some buyers and misreported the remittance; the second charged tax on sales that were generally zero-rated exports. Roughly half of their orders shipped outside Canada, so the second error was not small.

What we did: We applied the place of supply rules so Canadian sales carried the destination province's rate, treated export sales as zero-rated with the fulfilment evidence retained to support the claim, corrected the open filing periods and adjusted the storefront tax settings so the problem stopped recurring at source rather than being fixed at each year end.

The result:

  • Provincial rates applied by destination, not by one default
  • Export sales zero-rated with fulfilment evidence retained
  • Storefront settings corrected so the error stopped at source

Print-on-Demand Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I have inventory in a print-on-demand business?
No, and that is the single most useful thing to understand about your file. Because the printer holds nothing for you and manufactures only when an order arrives, you own no stock. There is nothing to value under ITA subsection 10(1), no year-end count, no shrinkage adjustment and no landed-cost calculation. What replaces inventory is a per-order supplier charge, and the discipline that matters is matching that charge to the order it belongs to rather than to the month your credit card happened to clear. Get the matching wrong across a December spike and your margin looks wonderful in one month and dreadful in the next — which misleads you far more than it misleads CRA. This is also why a preparer who has only done stocked e-commerce will ask you for a stock count you cannot produce and then not know what to do with the supplier statement that actually matters.
Do I need to register for GST/HST?
Once your worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, yes. The trap specific to print-on-demand is that export sales count toward that threshold even though they are generally zero-rated. Sellers whose customers are mostly in the United States routinely assume the threshold does not apply to them, and that assumption is wrong — you can be required to register while charging tax on almost nothing. The second trap is measuring the threshold against the money that landed in your bank. A payout is net of platform fees, refunds and chargebacks, so a seller tracking deposits often crosses the real gross threshold a year before they notice. We pin down the exact quarter you crossed from the platform exports, register from the correct date, and deal with any unregistered period honestly rather than quietly starting the clock from today.
Is Printful or Printify charging me GST/HST?
Usually not, and this is the biggest cash surprise on these files. Where your printer is a non-resident supplier, your largest cost line typically carries no GST/HST at all, which means you have almost no input tax credit to offset what you remit on your sales. Sellers who registered expecting to recover tax on their fulfilment costs discover at the first filing that they are remitting on nearly all of their Canadian sales with virtually nothing coming back. Two consequences. First, that changes the Quick Method answer, often decisively in its favour — see below. Second, where a service or intangible is acquired from a non-resident for use in Canada, the imported taxable supply rules in the Excise Tax Act can require the recipient to self-assess the tax, so there can be an obligation running in the other direction. We check your actual supplier arrangements rather than assuming either way, and we will not tell you what any named platform does without looking.
Who collects the sales tax, me or the platform?
It depends on the platform and the arrangement, and getting it backwards is expensive in both directions. Canada's distribution platform operator rules can make a marketplace responsible for collecting and remitting GST/HST on sales made through it, in which case you are not the one charging tax on those sales. On your own storefront, by contrast, the obligation is normally yours. A seller who assumes the platform is handling it when it is not has under-remitted; a seller who charges tax on top of a platform that is already collecting has overcharged their customers and created a mess to unwind. We will not tell you on a web page which named platform falls where, because the answer turns on the actual arrangement between you, the platform and the buyer. We check it against your agreements and your settlement reports, and we document the conclusion so the position is defensible later.
Do I charge tax on sales to the United States?
For Canadian GST/HST purposes, generally no — sales shipped to customers outside Canada are typically zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, so no GST/HST is charged while the sale still counts as a taxable supply for your registration threshold. The evidence matters: a zero-rating claim depends on being able to show the goods left Canada, and in a drop-printed model that evidence lives in your supplier's fulfilment records rather than in anything you shipped yourself, so we make sure it is retained. What this page does not tell you is anything about United States sales tax. State-level obligations, economic nexus thresholds and marketplace facilitator rules are real, they change, and they are not ours to summarise — we state no foreign rate, threshold or test anywhere on this page and we will tell you plainly when a question needs a specialist in that jurisdiction.
Is the Quick Method good for print-on-demand?
Often yes, and this is one of the few places where print-on-demand gets better treatment than a stocked retailer. The Quick Method lets a smaller registrant remit a set rate on sales instead of tracking input tax credits, and it is capped by an annual taxable supply threshold of $400,000. Whether it beats the regular method comes down to one question: how much input tax credit do you actually have? A business whose largest cost is a foreign printer charging no GST/HST has very little, and for that profile the Quick Method frequently wins — which is the opposite of the advice a business buying taxable Canadian inventory would get. We will not assert a conclusion for you though. We run both methods on your own figures, and you can try the arithmetic yourself with our Quick Method calculator before you even speak to us. We also watch the threshold as you grow.
How do I record cost of goods sold with no inventory?
Order by order. Your cost of sales is the sum of the per-order charges your printer billed for the orders that shipped in the period, and the whole discipline is matching those charges to the revenue they relate to rather than to the date your card was debited. The practical problem is that supplier statements and platform statements almost never align: the printer bills in batches, the platform settles on its own cycle, and both do it in a currency that may not be yours. We build the costing so each order carries its own fulfilment cost, which gives you gross margin per product rather than only in total — and that per-product number is what tells you which designs are worth keeping. We also treat samples and your own test purchases consistently, separating genuine business samples from personal items, because that line gets examined on review more often than sellers expect.
Can I deduct my design software and subscriptions?
Generally yes, where they are used to earn business income, and the ordinary monthly subscriptions a working seller pays for are normally deductible when incurred. The line to watch is between routine design work and a lasting asset. Design produced and used in the ordinary course is generally deductible; acquiring or developing an intangible asset with enduring value is a capital expenditure, depreciable as Class 14.1 property where it applies, and we make that call deliberately rather than letting it default. Commissioned and licensed artwork are treated according to what the contract actually transfers, since buying a design outright and licensing it are different transactions with different answers. Where a subscription or a device has personal use in it, only the business portion is deductible on a reasonable and documented basis. What we will not advise on is trade mark or copyright clearance — that is a legal exposure and an accountant is the wrong person to reassure you about it.
Can I claim my home office?
Yes, and most print-on-demand businesses qualify because they genuinely run from a spare room. Business-use-of-home expenses are deductible under ITA subsection 18(12), but with an important limit: the deduction cannot exceed the income from that business, and any excess carries forward to a later year rather than being lost. That limit bites hardest in exactly the year people most want the deduction — a first year with heavy start-up costs and little revenue. The claim is based on the share of the home used for the activity, measured rather than estimated, and we keep the measurement and the reasoning on file. The same discipline applies to your internet, your phone and any device with personal use in it: the business portion is deductible on a reasonable and documented basis, and a round percentage nobody can account for is exactly what a review will question first.
When should I incorporate my merch business?
When the numbers say so, not when a forum says so. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, the ability to leave profits in the company and taxed at corporate rates, and a cleaner structure if you ever sell the brand. It also brings a corporate return, higher compliance cost, and the loss of some simplicity you may currently rely on. For a seller clearing a few thousand a year the trade is usually poor; for one clearing six figures with profits they do not need to draw, it is usually good. We model it on your actual figures, including salary against dividends and the effect on your RRSP room and on the income record a mortgage lender will want to see. We also pick a fiscal year end deliberately, which for a business with a heavy fourth-quarter gift season is a real lever. And we say plainly when the answer is to wait.
What happens if I never registered for GST/HST?
We fix it properly, which is better than it sounds and much better than waiting. The first step is establishing the real date you crossed the threshold from your platform exports rather than from your bank deposits, because those two figures differ and only one of them is the test. Then we register from the correct effective date and quantify the unregistered period honestly — the tax on Canadian sales in that window does not disappear by being ignored, but it is usually far smaller than sellers fear, particularly where a large share of orders shipped abroad and would have been zero-rated anyway. Late filing on the income side costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month to twelve months, so we file the oldest unfiled year first to stop the penalty compounding. Where it is appropriate we bring the position forward on Form RC199, which when accepted cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief.
What records does CRA actually want from a print-on-demand seller?
Three things, and one of them has a deadline you control. First, the platform exports showing gross sales, fees, refunds, chargebacks and any tax the platform collected — not the payout summary, the detail behind it. Second, the supplier invoices tying fulfilment cost to orders. Third, the evidence of export supporting any zero-rated sales, which in your model lives in the fulfilment records. ITA section 230 requires six years of records, and the deadline you control is this: download and archive your platform exports while the storefront still exists. A shop that closes takes its reporting history with it, and no amount of goodwill afterwards will recover data the platform no longer serves. We have had to reconstruct years from payment processor statements alone because of exactly this, and it is slower, more expensive and less defensible than the five minutes an export would have taken.
