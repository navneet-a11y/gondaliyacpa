The Exempt-or-Taxable Supply Review

  • Part II of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts certain health care services, and almost every founder we meet assumes their product is inside it. That assumption is usually both wrong and unwelcome.
  • Unwelcome because being exempt is bad news. An exempt supplier cannot claim input tax credits, so the tax on your developers, your cloud bill and your software stack becomes a permanent cost.
  • The exemptions turn on both what the supply is and who makes it: an institutional health care service by a facility, or a diagnostic or treatment service rendered to an individual by a listed practitioner.
  • A licence of software to a clinic is generally none of those, which means it is a taxable supply and your credits are available. That is the right answer for most healthtech, and it is worth real money.
  • We will not tell you on a web page which side your product falls on, because it turns on your actual supply and your actual supplier. We work it out, write down the reasoning, and keep it on file.
3

Input Tax Credits and Apportionment

  • For a pre-revenue healthtech company the input tax credit is often the only cash coming in from anywhere other than investors, so getting the registration and the claims right is a cash-flow question first.
  • We register voluntarily before the $30,000 threshold where it pays to, which on a company with a large taxable cost base and little revenue it almost always does.
  • We claim the credits on the costs that actually carried tax: Canadian contractors, cloud and hosting, software subscriptions, office and equipment, and the professional fees that come with a funding round.
  • Where the company makes both exempt and taxable supplies, the credits are apportioned to the extent the inputs are used in commercial activity, on a fair and reasonable basis applied the same way every period.
  • We document the apportionment method when we adopt it rather than reconstructing it under review, because a percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a sales tax auditor takes apart.
4

SR&ED Claims for Clinical Software

  • Qualifying work goes into the expenditure pool under ITA section 37 and generates investment tax credits under ITA section 127, refundable at an enhanced rate for a Canadian-controlled private corporation up to its expenditure limit.
  • We state no rate and no limit on this page. Those figures move, they are specific, and a claim built from a number someone read on a website is the kind that unravels at the technical review.
  • The eligible work is the systematic investigation that resolved a technological uncertainty. Not the feature you shipped, not the clinical trial that confirmed it worked, and not the data you collected along the way.
  • That distinction is where nearly every first healthtech claim we review goes wrong, because clinical validation feels like the hardest part of the project and is usually the part that does not qualify.
  • So we set up contemporaneous records while the work happens: what was uncertain, what was tried, what failed and why. You can model the arithmetic yourself with our SR&ED calculator first.
5

Government Assistance and Your SR&ED Claim

  • This is the healthtech-specific trap. ITA subsection 127(11.1) reduces your qualified expenditures by government and non-government assistance received or entitled to be received, and healthtech takes more assistance than most sectors.
  • Provincial health innovation funding, a hospital partnership payment, accelerator money, a university collaboration grant — each one has to be tested against the agreement to see whether it reduces the claim.
  • A company that claimed the full expenditure and ignored a grant it had already banked gets the reduction applied on review, with the credit clawed back in a year when the cash is long spent.
  • We read the funding agreements before the claim rather than after, and we model the net benefit of taking a particular grant against the credit it will cost you, which is a conversation worth having early.
  • We also keep the claim and the grant reporting consistent with each other, because the same project described two different ways in two different submissions is how a technical review starts badly.
6

Regulatory Spend: Capital or Current

  • Software as a medical device brings a Health Canada class determination, a device licence and a quality management system, and every one of those costs money before a single dollar of revenue arrives.
  • The accounting question nobody asks is whether that spend is deductible now. ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) bars a deduction for a capital outlay, and a licence with enduring value looks a lot like one.
  • Where the expenditure is capital and the asset is an eligible intangible, it generally falls into Class 14.1 and is written off over time rather than claimed in the year the cheque cleared.
  • We make that call deliberately and on the facts, separating ongoing compliance cost — which is ordinary and current — from the one-off spend that bought an approval the company will hold for years.
  • What we do not do is tell you your device class, your licence pathway or your regulatory timeline. That is a regulatory question, we are not regulatory consultants, and we will say so plainly.
7

Revenue Recognition and Deferred Revenue

  • An annual licence invoiced in January is not January's revenue. It is earned across the twelve months it covers, with the unearned balance carried as deferred revenue rather than taken to income on invoicing.
  • This matters beyond tidiness. A healthtech company that books annual contracts on signature looks profitable in the quarter it closes a deal and broke in the three that follow, which misleads its own board.
  • Implementation and setup fees are separated from the subscription rather than lumped in, because they are earned when the work is done and they distort the recurring revenue figure investors actually look at.
  • Pilot agreements get their own treatment. A paid pilot is revenue; an unpaid one that may convert is not, and a letter of intent from a hospital is neither revenue nor a contract however encouraging it reads.
  • We keep usage-based billing reconciled to the underlying meter rather than to the invoice run, so a billing error does not quietly become a revenue error that nobody finds until diligence.
8

Selling to Hospitals and Health Authorities

  • Here is the thing founders get backwards: the customer's status does not exempt the supply. Selling to a public hospital or a provincial health authority does not make a taxable supply exempt.
  • Only the nature of what you supply can do that, so a platform licensed to a hospital is generally taxable even though everything around the transaction feels like the public health system.
  • The second public-sector problem is timing. Procurement cycles run months, purchase orders arrive before contracts, and payment lands long after the revenue was earned and the tax on it became payable.
  • We build the working capital model around that gap rather than discovering it, because a company that recognises revenue on delivery and collects in ninety days needs to have planned for the ninety days.
  • We also treat your data residency and privacy compliance spend as the ordinary deductible operating cost it is, while being clear that whether you are compliant is a question for your counsel and not for us.
9

GST/HST Registration and Place of Supply

  • Registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, and exempt supplies do not count toward that figure while zero-rated ones do.
  • Canadian sales carry the rate for the customer's province under the place of supply rules, with Ontario at 13%, which for software licensed across the country means several rates in the same month.
  • Supplies to customers outside Canada are generally zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, so no tax is charged while the supply still counts as taxable for your credits.
  • If the company is a non-resident supplying Canadian consumers, the simplified registration regime may reach you, and our registration checker will tell you whether it does before you call us.
  • We flag where separate provincial registrations can arise in Quebec, British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba rather than describing those regimes here, and we state no foreign sales tax rule at all.

Payroll, Contractors and Developers

  • We work through the contractor-versus-employee question honestly rather than labelling every developer a contractor for convenience, because a wrong call there is assessed against the company with interest and penalties.
  • That question is sharper in healthtech than elsewhere, because the same person is often a part-time clinical adviser, a contractor on the build and a shareholder, all in one relationship.

Healthtech Accounting & Tax FAQs

Is my healthtech product exempt from HST?
Probably not, and that is good news rather than bad. Part II of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts certain health care services, and the exemptions turn on both what the supply is and who makes it — an institutional health care service supplied by a health care facility, or a consultative, diagnostic, treatment or other health care service rendered to an individual by one of the practitioners the legislation names. A licence of software to a clinic is generally none of those. Founders hope to be exempt because exemption sounds like relief, but for a product company it is the worse outcome: an exempt supplier makes no taxable supplies and therefore cannot claim input tax credits, so the HST on your developers, cloud and software becomes a permanent cost. We will not characterise your product on a web page, because it depends on your actual supply and your actual supplier. We settle it on your facts and keep the reasoning on file.
Can I claim input tax credits as a healthtech company?
If your supplies are taxable, yes — and for a pre-revenue company this is often the only cash arriving from anywhere other than investors. The credits sit on the costs that actually carried GST/HST: Canadian contractors and agencies, cloud and hosting, software subscriptions, equipment, office costs and the professional fees around a funding round. Two points matter. First, you can register voluntarily before crossing $30,000, and on a company with a large taxable cost base and almost no revenue that is usually worth doing immediately rather than waiting. Second, a foreign supplier generally charges you no GST/HST, so the share of your stack bought from outside Canada produces no credit — which is worth knowing before you build a cash forecast around a refund. We work out the real recoverable figure rather than the hoped-for one, and we go back and claim what is still available on the open periods.
What if I make both exempt and taxable supplies?
Then you apportion, and the method matters as much as the arithmetic. Input tax credits are available to the extent an input is acquired for consumption or use in a commercial activity, so where a company makes some exempt and some taxable supplies the credits on shared inputs — the cloud bill, the office, the engineering team — have to be split on a fair and reasonable basis. Hybrid healthtech businesses run into this more than pure software companies: a company that licenses a platform and also employs practitioners delivering a service directly to patients can genuinely be on both sides of the line at once. The practical rule is to choose a method, document why it is reasonable, and apply it the same way every period. A round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a sales tax auditor takes apart, and a method that changes whenever it suits the result is worse than a slightly imperfect one applied consistently.
Should I register for GST/HST before I have revenue?
Usually yes, and earlier than most founders expect. Registration is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, but you may register voluntarily before that if you are making or will make taxable supplies. For a healthtech company the voluntary route is often clearly worth it, because you are spending heavily on inputs that carry HST while invoicing almost nothing — so registering turns a cost into a refund. Two cautions. Exempt supplies do not count toward the threshold and generate no credits, so if your characterisation is genuinely exempt, registering achieves nothing. And zero-rated export sales do count toward the threshold even though no tax is charged on them, which catches companies selling mostly to United States customers who assumed the threshold did not apply. We model it on your actual cost base before registering, not after.
Can I claim SR&ED on clinical software?
Often yes, but not for the part of the project you are proudest of. Qualifying expenditures go into the pool under ITA section 37 and generate investment tax credits under ITA section 127, refundable at an enhanced rate for a Canadian-controlled private corporation up to its expenditure limit — we state no rate and no limit here, because those are specific and a claim built on a figure from a website is the kind that unravels at technical review. What qualifies is the systematic investigation that resolved a genuine technological uncertainty: the algorithm that did not work, the architecture problem with no known solution, the integration nobody could make reliable. What does not qualify is routine development, data collection, quality control and, usually, clinical validation. Running a trial to confirm a product works is confirmation, not investigation. That distinction is where nearly every first healthtech claim we review goes wrong.
Does a grant reduce my SR&ED claim?
Frequently, and this is the trap healthtech falls into more than any other sector. ITA subsection 127(11.1) reduces qualified expenditures by government and non-government assistance received or entitled to be received in respect of the expenditure. Healthtech companies take more of that than most: provincial health innovation funding, hospital partnership payments, university collaboration money, accelerator cheques. Each one has to be read against its agreement to see whether it is assistance and, if so, how much it reduces. The failure mode is painful: a company claims the full expenditure, the credit is paid, the cash goes on payroll, and two years later a review applies the reduction and claws it back in a year when there is nothing to claw it from. So we read the funding agreements before the claim rather than after, and where you are choosing whether to take a particular grant, we model the net benefit against the credit it will cost.
Are Health Canada licensing and regulatory costs deductible?
Some of them, now — and the rest over time, which is the distinction nobody makes. Ongoing regulatory compliance is an ordinary operating cost and currently deductible. But ITA paragraph 18(1)(b) bars the deduction of a capital outlay, and spending that secures an approval the company will hold for years looks a great deal like capital. Where it is capital and the asset is an eligible intangible, it generally falls into Class 14.1 and is written off over time rather than claimed in the year the cheque cleared. So a device licence application, the clinical and documentation work done to support it, and a quality management system build each have to be looked at on their own facts rather than swept into professional fees. What we will not do is tell you your device class, your licence pathway or your timeline. That is a regulatory question, we are not regulatory consultants, and you should use one.
Do I charge HST when I sell to a hospital?
Generally yes, and this is the single most common thing founders have backwards. The customer's status does not exempt the supply. Selling to a public hospital, a provincial health authority, a university or any other public-sector body does not convert a taxable supply into an exempt one — only the nature of what you supply can do that. So a platform licensed to a hospital is normally a taxable supply even though every part of the transaction feels like the public health system, and a company that issued years of invoices without tax on the assumption that hospitals are exempt has a problem to fix rather than a position to defend. Certain public bodies may be entitled to rebates on tax they pay, but that is their claim, not your exemption, and it does not change what you were required to charge. We settle this at the start and fix it where it has already gone wrong.
How do I recognise revenue on an annual licence?
Across the period it covers, not in the month you invoiced it. An annual licence billed in January is earned over twelve months, with the unearned balance sitting as deferred revenue on the balance sheet until it is. This is not bookkeeping fussiness — it is the difference between statements that describe your business and statements that mislead your own board. A company that books multi-year hospital contracts in full on signature shows a wonderful quarter followed by terrible ones for no commercial reason, and the first person hurt by that is the founder trying to plan from it. Three related points. Implementation and setup fees are earned when the work is done and should be shown separately, because they distort the recurring revenue figure investors actually care about. A paid pilot is revenue; an unpaid pilot is not. And a letter of intent from a hospital is neither revenue nor a contract, however encouraging it reads.
Can I deduct cloud, hosting and software subscriptions?
Yes, where they are used to earn business income, and for a healthtech company they are usually the largest cost line after people. Monthly cloud, hosting, monitoring, data storage and the development and security tooling a team runs on are ordinary operating expenses, deductible when incurred. Two refinements worth knowing. Where a cost is incurred to build something with enduring value rather than to run the business day to day, it may be capital rather than current, and the line between configuring a platform and developing an asset is one we draw deliberately rather than by default. And on the sales tax side, a Canadian supplier charges you GST/HST you can generally recover as an input tax credit if your supplies are taxable, while a foreign supplier typically charges none — so the recoverable share of your stack is smaller than the total, and in some cases the imported supply rules can run the other way.
How should I pay my developers?
Correctly, which is not always the same as conveniently. The contractor-versus-employee question is decided on the real working relationship — control, tools, financial risk, integration into the business — and not on what the agreement calls the person or on what both sides would prefer. Labelling a full-time developer a contractor because it is simpler is the error we see most, and it is assessed against the company, with interest and penalties, not against the worker. Healthtech makes this harder than most sectors because the same individual is often three things at once: a part-time clinical adviser, a contractor on the build and a shareholder. We work through it honestly and tell you when the answer is employee. There is also an SR&ED reason to get payroll right: a claim needs time allocated to eligible work as it happens, and an estimate assembled the week before filing is exactly what a reviewer discounts.
When should I incorporate, and what do investors need?
Most healthtech is incorporated from the start because investors require it, so the real question is not whether but whether the structure is right before money comes in. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, the ability to retain earnings at corporate rates, and the share structure a raise needs. We model salary against dividends for the founders, including the effect on RRSP room and on the income record a mortgage lender will want to see — which matters more than founders expect when they have been paying themselves nothing for three years. We also choose the fiscal year end deliberately, because closing the year before or after a round changes what the statements in the data room show. What investors ask for is consistent: two clean years, deferred revenue handled properly, recurring revenue separated from one-off fees, and a defensible SR&ED position. We prepare those before the diligence request arrives.
