2

The Personal Services Business Review

  • This is the first thing we look at and the most expensive thing on your file. ITA subsection 125(7) defines a personal services business, and a consultant embedded with one client is the textbook fact pattern.
  • The test asks whether you would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the client but for your corporation, unless the corporation employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year.
  • If it applies the consequences are severe. The small business deduction is denied and ITA paragraph 18(1)(p) restricts your deductions to little more than your own salary — no rent, no professional fees, no vehicle.
  • There is a particular irony here that is worth naming out loud: you advise clients on worker classification for a living, and this is the same question turned around and pointed at you.
  • We will not tell you on a web page whether the rules reach your arrangement. We assess it on your actual engagements, tell you plainly where the exposure sits, and set out what would have to change.
3

Contractor or Employee: Your Own Status

  • The classification question is decided on the whole relationship rather than on the contract's label: control, who owns the tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and how far you are integrated into the client.
  • For a safety consultant the integration factor is the dangerous one. Being on site daily, using the client's equipment, reporting to their management and appearing on their organisation chart all point the wrong way.
  • So we work on the evidence that points the right way: concurrent clients, your own insurance and equipment, your own invoicing and pricing, work done off site, and the genuine risk you carry on a fixed fee.
  • We document that while the engagements are live rather than assembling it after a letter arrives, because contemporaneous evidence of independence is worth considerably more than a reconstruction.
  • And we review your engagement letters before you sign them where you want us to, because a clause written for the client's convenience can quietly cost you the small business deduction for the year.
4

WSIB for You and Your Clients

  • You deal with WSIB on both sides of every engagement, and almost nobody accounts for the second side. Your clients need classification, premium and claims work, and your own practice has a registration question.
  • Whether you are an independent operator who must register is a real question, particularly where any part of your work sits in construction, and the answer changes what you owe and what you can bill for.
  • The clearance certificate cuts both ways. Your clients will ask you for one, and a client who engages a contractor without obtaining theirs can be left carrying that contractor's unpaid premiums.
  • Where you use subcontract trainers or hire your own staff, we work out the coverage position properly rather than discovering a gap at a premium reconciliation or after somebody is hurt on a site.
  • We handle the reconciliation and the audit support through our WSIB reporting and compliance service, and we state no rate, classification code or premium figure on this page because those are checked against the schedule.
5

Professional Liability and Contingent Claims

  • Your advice carries statutory consequence, so professional liability and errors and omissions cover is not discretionary for this practice. The premiums are deductible when incurred, and that part of it is straightforward enough.
  • The harder question is the claim that has been made but not resolved. Accounting may well call for a provision, and the figure may be perfectly sound, but that does not make it deductible.
  • ITA paragraph 18(1)(e) bars the deduction of a reserve or contingent amount except where the Act expressly permits it, so the deduction waits until the obligation is actual rather than merely probable.
  • That gap between the accounting provision and the tax deduction is a timing difference we track rather than leave as a surprise, and it matters in exactly the year a practice can least afford one.
  • Legal fees incurred to defend the practice's income-earning activity are generally deductible, and we separate those from costs that are capital or personal rather than claiming the invoice as it came.
6

Certification Course Revenue

  • Most of these practices deliver certification training alongside the consulting, and course revenue behaves nothing like a consulting fee. Fees received before delivery are earned as the instruction happens.
  • So a prepaid block of twenty seats bought by an employer in December is a liability in December, not December's revenue, and a multi-session programme is earned across the sessions rather than at the start.
  • Per-certificate fees paid out to an accrediting body are a cost of the delivery and matched to it, not booked when the invoice from the body happens to arrive a quarter later.
  • We treat course material development deliberately rather than by default, because a one-off build of a curriculum or an e-learning module can be a capital asset while ordinary annual updates are current.
  • And we show the training side separately from the consulting side in your statements, because the two have different margins and most practices genuinely do not know which one is carrying the other.
7

Exempt or Taxable Training Supplies

  • Here is the question that decides whether the credits on your training side exist. Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, and some certification instruction falls inside it.
  • The exemption reaches instruction in courses leading to a certificate attesting to competence to practise a trade or vocation, where the supplier is a vocational school as the legislation defines that term.
  • As ever, exemption is not a prize. An exempt supplier claims no input tax credits on the inputs to that activity, so the tax on venue rental, equipment and materials for exempt courses stays with you.
  • Most of these practices are therefore mixed: taxable consulting, possibly exempt instruction, and taxable sales of manuals and equipment. The credits get apportioned on a fair and reasonable basis applied consistently.
  • We will not tell you on a web page whether you are a vocational school or whether a given course is exempt. We settle it on your facts, document the reasoning, and apply it the same way every period.
8

GST/HST Registration and the Quick Method

  • Registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, and exempt instruction does not count toward that figure.
  • Canadian clients are charged at the rate for their province under the place of supply rules, with Ontario at 13%, which for a consultant working across several provinces means more than one rate running.
  • Services supplied to a non-resident client are often zero-rated, which matters for a consultant advising a foreign parent on its Canadian operations, and we check that rather than charging tax by default.
  • We run the Quick Method against the regular method on your figures, capped at $400,000 of annual taxable supplies, which on a service practice with modest taxable costs is frequently the better answer.
  • You can try the arithmetic yourself with our Quick Method calculator before you speak to us, and we flag where separate provincial registrations can arise rather than describing those regimes here.
9

Vehicle, Home Office and Equipment

  • A safety consultant drives for a living, so the vehicle is usually the largest single deduction after the owner's own compensation and the one most likely to be examined on review.
  • Which means the log is not optional. Site visits, client meetings, training venues and the trips that were genuinely personal, recorded as you drive rather than reconstructed from a calendar in April.
  • Monitoring equipment, sampling gear, gas detectors, sound meters and training props are capital property claimed through capital cost allowance over time rather than expensed in full in the year you bought them.
  • Calibration and maintenance are current costs and we keep them separate from the asset, because a reviewer who finds a calibration invoice capitalised starts wondering what else was classified by guesswork.
  • The home office is claimed under ITA subsection 18(12), limited to the income from the business with the excess carried forward, measured rather than estimated and with the measurement kept on file.
10

Subcontract Trainers and Payroll

  • When you bring in another trainer to cover a course, the classification question arrives again — this time with you as the payer, and with the assessment landing on your practice rather than on theirs.
  • We work it through honestly rather than labelling everyone a contractor for convenience, and we prepare the T4A slips on time where that is the right answer and set up payroll where it is not.
  • Where you do hire, we set payroll up properly from the first pay with source deductions remitted on the right schedule, because a new employer that is days late still collects a penalty.
  • There is a second reason to get this right that is specific to you. Employing more than five full-time employees throughout the year takes the practice outside the personal services business definition entirely.
  • That is not a reason to hire people you do not need, and we will say so. It is a reason to understand what your staffing actually does to your tax position before you plan the next year.
11

Incorporation and Client Concentration

  • Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit and the ability to leave profits in the company, which on a good year is a real deferral.
  • But for this practice the incorporation conversation is inseparable from the client concentration one, because a corporation whose income comes from a single embedded engagement may get none of that benefit.
  • So we model it on your actual client mix rather than on your revenue alone, and we say plainly when the honest answer is to stay unincorporated until the practice has more than one real client.
  • We model salary against dividends for the owner, including the effect on RRSP room, on CPP contributions and on whether you are building the income record a mortgage lender will accept.
  • And we choose a fiscal year end deliberately, which for a practice whose training revenue clusters before a compliance deadline is a planning lever rather than an administrative detail.
12

CRA Audit Resolution

  • When the CRA raises the personal services business question, we produce the engagement letters, the client list, the insurance and the evidence of independence — which is why we build that file as we go.
  • When a subcontract trainer is reassessed as your employee, we deal with the source deduction exposure and argue the classification on the real relationship rather than conceding it to save time.
  • When your input tax credits or your exempt-and-taxable apportionment are examined, we produce the invoices showing which costs carried tax and the documented basis on which the split was made.
  • When the vehicle or home office claim is questioned, we produce the log and the measurement rather than a round percentage, which is the difference between a short conversation and a long one.
  • We file the Notice of Objection within 90 days of a reassessment and pursue taxpayer relief on Form RC4288, cancelling penalties and interest where a prior preparer's error was the cause.

Toronto Safety Consultant — Reassessed as a Personal Services Business

The problem: A Toronto consultant had incorporated on a previous accountant's advice and spent two years embedded full time with one construction client, on their site daily, using their equipment and reporting to their project manager. He had claimed the small business deduction, a vehicle, a home office and professional fees. CRA reassessed both years as a personal services business, denying the deduction and most of the expenses under ITA paragraph 18(1)(p).

What we did: We assessed the position honestly and told him the earlier years were weak. We objected on the facts that genuinely supported him, settled where the evidence did not, and then rebuilt the practice's position for the future: a second and third client taken on deliberately, his own equipment and insurance, fixed-fee pricing carrying real risk, and an engagement letter rewritten before the next renewal.

The result:

  • Objection filed on the facts that supported him, settled on those that did not
  • Client mix and contracts restructured so the risk stopped recurring
  • Independence file kept contemporaneously from that quarter on

Mississauga Training Provider — A Year of Revenue in December

The problem: A Mississauga consultancy sold blocks of certification seats to employers, often taking payment in December for courses delivered the following spring. Everything had been booked as revenue on receipt, so the statements showed a spectacular fourth quarter and three poor ones every year. Nobody had separated the training side from the consulting side, and the owner genuinely believed consulting was carrying the business.

What we did: We restated prepaid seat blocks as a liability earned across delivery, matched the per-certificate fees paid to the accrediting body to the courses they related to, and split the statements into two revenue streams with their own direct costs. We then settled the exempt-or-taxable question on the actual courses and set the input tax credit apportionment on a documented basis.

The result:

  • Prepaid seats deferred across delivery, not booked on receipt
  • Training shown to be the higher-margin side, not consulting
  • Credit apportionment documented on the real course mix

Ottawa Consultancy — A Provision That Was Not a Deduction

The problem: An Ottawa consultancy faced a claim from a client following an incident at a site it had audited. Its bookkeeper had accrued a substantial provision on the advice of nobody in particular and deducted it in full, producing a loss for the year and a refund claim on the carryback. The claim had not been resolved, no payment had been made, and liability was denied.

What we did: We explained why ITA paragraph 18(1)(e) bars the deduction of a contingent amount and corrected the return proactively rather than waiting for a reassessment and a penalty. We kept the provision in the accounting statements where it belonged, tracked the timing difference, and separated the deductible defence costs from amounts that were not.

The result:

  • Return corrected before reassessment, avoiding a penalty
  • Provision retained for accounting, timing difference tracked
  • Legal defence costs claimed correctly and separately

Health and Safety Consultant Accounting & Tax FAQs

Am I a personal services business?
It is the question worth asking first, and we will not answer it for you on a web page. ITA subsection 125(7) defines a personal services business as one where an individual performs the services on behalf of the corporation, that individual or a related person is a specified shareholder, and the individual would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the person receiving the services but for the existence of the corporation — unless the corporation employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year, or the services are provided to an associated corporation. A safety consultant embedded full time on one client's site, using their equipment, reporting to their management, is squarely in the territory the provision was written for. The irony is not lost on anyone: you advise clients on worker classification for a living. We assess it on your actual engagements and tell you plainly where the exposure sits.
What actually happens if the CRA says I am one?
Two things, and the second is worse than the first. The small business deduction is denied on that income, so the corporation pays at the full rate rather than the reduced one. Then ITA paragraph 18(1)(p) restricts what the corporation may deduct to the salary and benefits of the incorporated employee and a short list of specified amounts. That means no rent, no professional fees, no vehicle, no office costs, no equipment — the ordinary expenses of running a business simply stop being deductible. Many consultants discover this across two or three reassessed years at once, which is a sum large enough to end a practice. It is also why the answer matters more than anything else on your file, and why we would rather raise it with you in February than have you read it in a letter. There are things that can be changed; almost all of them have to be changed prospectively.
Should I incorporate as a safety consultant?
Only once we have looked at your client mix, because for this practice incorporation and client concentration are the same conversation. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit and the ability to retain profits at corporate rates, which on a good year is a genuine deferral. But a corporation whose income comes from one embedded engagement may get none of that, and may lose deductions it would have had as a sole proprietor. So we model it on your real engagements rather than on your revenue figure, and we say plainly when the honest answer is to stay unincorporated until there is more than one real client. Where incorporation does make sense we model salary against dividends, including the effect on your RRSP room, your CPP contributions and the income record a mortgage lender will actually accept after a few years of paying yourself in dividends.
Do I need to register with WSIB myself?
Possibly, and it is worth settling rather than assuming. You spend your working life on clients' WSIB classification, premiums and claims, and the question of whether your own practice must register is a separate one that turns on what you do and where. It matters particularly if any part of your work sits in construction, where the rules on independent operators are tighter. The answer changes what you owe, what you can bill, and what you can tell a client who asks. Where you bring in subcontract trainers or hire your own staff there is a second layer: who is covered, by whom, and what gap exists if somebody is injured on a client's site. We work that out properly rather than leaving it to a premium reconciliation to reveal. What we will not do is quote you a rate, a classification code or a premium figure on a web page — those are checked against the schedule for your actual activity.
What is a clearance certificate and why do clients ask for it?
It is evidence that a contractor's WSIB account is in good standing, and it protects the party hiring them. The reason your clients ask you for one is the same reason you tell your clients to ask their contractors: a principal who engages a contractor without obtaining a valid clearance can end up exposed to that contractor's unpaid premiums. So the certificate cuts in both directions across your practice. On one side you need to be able to produce one on request, which means your own account has to be registered and current. On the other, it is part of the advice you give, and a client who takes it from you and then does not follow it has a problem you will hear about. We keep your side in order and handle the reconciliation and audit support through our WSIB reporting and compliance service.
Do I charge HST on safety training courses?
Sometimes not, and the answer has consequences you may not want. Part III of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act exempts specified educational services, including instruction in courses leading to a certificate attesting to competence to practise a trade or vocation where the supplier is a vocational school as the legislation defines that term. Some certification instruction falls inside it. But exemption is not a benefit here: an exempt supplier claims no input tax credits on the inputs to that activity, so the HST on venue rental, equipment, materials and the share of your overheads attributable to exempt courses becomes a permanent cost. Most of these practices end up mixed — taxable consulting, possibly exempt instruction, taxable sales of manuals and gear. We settle the characterisation on your actual courses rather than asserting it, and document the reasoning when we adopt it.
Can I claim input tax credits if some of my training is exempt?
On the taxable part, yes, and the method is what matters. Input tax credits are available to the extent an input is acquired for consumption or use in a commercial activity, so where some of your supplies are exempt instruction and the rest are taxable consulting and sales, the credits on shared inputs have to be apportioned. Shared inputs for a practice like yours are the obvious ones: the vehicle, the home or office premises, the phone and software, the professional fees, and any equipment used across both sides. The practical rule is to pick a basis that is genuinely reasonable on your facts, document why, and then apply it the same way every period. A round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a sales tax auditor takes apart, and a basis that moves whenever it suits the result is worse than an imperfect one applied consistently. We set it when we adopt it and keep the reasoning on file.
How do I account for prepaid course fees and seat blocks?
As a liability until you deliver. Fees received before instruction happens are earned as the instruction happens, so a block of twenty seats an employer buys in December for courses you will run in the spring is December's cash and the spring's revenue. The unearned balance sits as deferred revenue in between. This is not bookkeeping neatness — a practice that books prepayments on receipt shows a spectacular fourth quarter and three poor ones every single year, and the first person misled by that is the owner trying to plan from it. The same logic applies to a multi-session programme, which is earned across the sessions rather than at the start. We also match the per-certificate fees you pay an accrediting body to the courses they relate to rather than booking them when the invoice happens to arrive, and we show the training side separately from consulting so you can see which has the better margin.
Can I deduct my professional liability insurance, and an outstanding claim?
The premiums yes, the claim usually not yet. Professional liability and errors and omissions premiums incurred to earn business income are deductible when incurred, and for a practice whose advice carries statutory consequence they are a substantial and entirely ordinary cost. The outstanding claim is different and it catches people. Your accountant may properly record a provision, and the amount may be a careful estimate, but ITA paragraph 18(1)(e) prohibits the deduction of an amount as a reserve or contingent liability except where the Act expressly permits it. So the tax deduction waits until the obligation is actual rather than probable — a settlement reached, a judgment given, an amount payable. That produces a timing difference between your statements and your return, which we track deliberately rather than leave to be discovered. Legal fees to defend the practice's income-earning activity are generally deductible as incurred.
Can I deduct my vehicle and my home office?
Both, on the business portion, and the vehicle is the one to get right. A safety consultant drives for a living, so the vehicle is usually the largest deduction after the owner's own compensation — which also makes it the most likely thing to be examined. That means a contemporaneous log: site visits, client meetings, training venues, and honestly recording the trips that were personal. Recorded as you drive it defends the claim; reconstructed from a calendar in April it does not. The home office is claimed under ITA subsection 18(12), deductible only against the income from that business with any excess carried forward rather than lost, and based on the measured share of the home used for the work rather than a round number. Your monitoring equipment, gas detectors, sound meters and training props are capital property claimed through capital cost allowance over time, with calibration and maintenance as current costs kept separate from the asset.
How should I pay subcontract trainers?
Correctly, and the question is the same one you face about yourself, only now you are the payer and the assessment lands on your practice. Classification is decided on the whole relationship — control, who provides the tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, integration into your business — and not on what the agreement calls the person. A trainer who delivers your courses, to your curriculum, on your schedule, using your materials, exclusively for you, starts to look like an employee however the invoice is headed. We work it through honestly rather than labelling everyone a contractor for convenience, prepare the T4A slips on time where contractor is the right answer, and set payroll up properly where it is not. There is one angle here specific to your own position: a corporation that employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year falls outside the personal services business definition entirely, which is worth understanding before you plan your staffing.
Do I need to register for GST/HST, and is the Quick Method worth it?
Registration is required once worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Exempt instruction does not count toward that figure, which is one more reason the characterisation matters. Canadian clients are charged at the rate for their province under the place of supply rules, with Ontario at 13%, so a consultant working across provinces runs more than one rate. Services supplied to a non-resident client are often zero-rated, which is worth checking rather than charging tax by default when you advise a foreign parent on its Canadian operations. On the Quick Method: it lets a smaller registrant remit a set rate on sales instead of tracking input tax credits, capped at $400,000 of annual taxable supplies, and on a service practice with modest taxable costs it frequently wins.
