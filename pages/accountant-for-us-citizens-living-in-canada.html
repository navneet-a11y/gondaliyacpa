Toronto Household — Fifteen Years of Perfectly Good Canadian Advice

The problem: An American who moved to Toronto in her twenties had done everything a Canadian adviser would tell her to do. She maximised a TFSA every year, held Canadian mutual funds inside and outside it, and opened RESPs for two children. She had also filed a US return every year through an online service that asked her about American income and nothing else. Fifteen years in, none of her Canadian accounts had ever been assessed for what they might be under American rules, and nobody had told her the question existed.

What we did: We built a complete schedule of every plan and fund she held, with the open American question attached to each: whether the TFSA and RESPs are foreign trusts engaging Form 3520 and Form 3520-A, and whether each fund is a PFIC engaging Form 8621. We took no position on the answers and handed the schedule to a preparer licensed in the United States, then rebuilt her Canadian foreign tax credit position.

The result:

  • Every plan and fund documented with its open question identified
  • Three Form T1135 years rebuilt and filed on the Canadian side
  • Investment structure changed before further contributions were made

Mississauga Consultant — A CCPC That Worked Beautifully on One Side

The problem: A US citizen running an IT consulting practice in Mississauga incorporated on ordinary Canadian advice. It worked exactly as intended here: active income taxed near 12.2%, surplus retained in the company, salary and dividends split to manage the personal rate. Nobody had mentioned that a US person holding the shares is generally inside the Form 5471 regime, or that anti-deferral rules behind it can reach undistributed corporate income in the shareholder's hands — which is precisely the deferral the structure was built to achieve.

What we did: We kept the Canadian side running correctly, prepared the year-end package with the figures a Form 5471 preparer would need identified rather than buried, and brought in a US-licensed preparer to reach the American conclusions. We asserted none of them ourselves and reviewed the distribution plan against both sides.

The result:

  • Canadian T2 and Small Business Deduction position unchanged and intact
  • Shareholder reporting package produced annually rather than reconstructed
  • Distribution timing reviewed against both systems before the next year

Ottawa Retiree — Two Preparers Who Never Spoke

The problem: An American who had worked thirty years in Canada was drawing a Canadian pension and a small American one. A Canadian accountant prepared the T1 and an American service prepared the Form 1040, and the two had never exchanged a figure. Each return claimed relief for tax paid in the other country on a basis the other return did not support, the foreign tax credit on the Canadian side was a single estimated number, and Form T1135 had never been filed although the American accounts were well over the cost threshold.

What we did: We rebuilt the Canadian foreign tax credit under ITA section 126 by country and by income type, filed the missing Form T1135 years through a Voluntary Disclosures Program application on Form RC199, and set a sequence so the two returns are now prepared in a defined order with figures exchanged.

The result:

  • Foreign tax credit recomputed on a basis that ties to the other return
  • Missed Form T1135 years filed and penalties cancelled on disclosure
  • Both returns now prepared in sequence rather than in isolation

Do I really have to file a US tax return if I live in Canada and earn nothing American?
Yes. The United States taxes its citizens on worldwide income regardless of where they live, which is the single fact that makes this whole area what it is. You file a Form 1040 reporting your worldwide income, and relief from paying tax twice on the same dollar comes through the foreign tax credit on Form 1116 or, for earned income, Form 2555 — not through the obligation lapsing because you have been away a long time. Account reporting is separate again, through FinCEN Form 114 and Form 8938. We state no filing threshold, exclusion amount or penalty figure for any of those forms on this page, because those numbers move and the current answer belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States. What we can tell you plainly is that the obligation does not expire, length of absence does not end it, and having no American income does not by itself mean there is nothing to file.
Is my TFSA a problem?
It is the question we are asked most, and we will not answer it on a web page, because the answer determines whether you have unfiled information returns and that is not a conclusion to take from a website. Here is what is real. A TFSA is tax-free in Canada. For American purposes, whether it is a foreign trust — which would engage Form 3520 and Form 3520-A — is a live question, and the income inside it is not automatically sheltered on that side of the border just because it is sheltered here. The practical consequence is the awkward one: the account a Canadian adviser would tell every client to maximise may be among the worst things an American in Canada can hold. What we do is list every plan you have with the open question attached to each, hand that to a preparer licensed in the United States for the conclusions, and make sure no further contribution happens while the question is open. The same approach applies to an RESP, an FHSA and an RDSP.
Are my Canadian mutual funds and ETFs a problem too?
Possibly, and for a completely different reason from the registered plans. The issue is whether a Canadian mutual fund, ETF or pooled fund is a passive foreign investment company for American purposes, which would engage Form 8621 and its own reporting and computation regime. There are elections available within that regime, they are technical, and they generally work better when made early than when discovered late. We name the regime and the form so you know the question exists. We do not tell you whether any fund you hold is or is not within it, we do not quote any rate or computation, and we do not advise on the elections, because all of that belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States. The useful thing we can do is practical: identify every pooled investment you hold, inside and outside your registered plans, so that the question is asked about all of them at once rather than one at a time as statements surface.
I want to incorporate my business. Does being American change that?
Substantially, and this is the one place where the standard Canadian answer can be actively wrong for you. On the Canadian side a CCPC is attractive for the usual reasons: roughly a 12.2% Ontario combined rate on the first $500,000 of active income under ITA section 125 against a top personal rate of 53.53%, with surplus retained in the company. The point of that structure is deferral. The complication is that a US person holding the shares is generally inside the Form 5471 regime, and the anti-deferral rules behind it can tax undistributed corporate income in the shareholder's hands — which is the deferral the structure was built to create. We will not tell you whether an inclusion arises for you or in what amount; that is a US-licensed preparer's conclusion. What we will do is model the Canadian side properly, get the American question answered by the right person before you incorporate rather than after, and tell you honestly that for some American clients the answer is to stay unincorporated.
What is Form T1135 and why do people like me keep missing it?
Form T1135 is a Canadian filing, required where your specified foreign property costs more than $100,000, and Americans living here miss it more reliably than almost any other group. The reason is psychological rather than technical: the property in question is usually an American bank account, a brokerage account, a house back home or an interest in an American entity, and none of that feels like foreign property to someone who grew up with it. To CRA it is foreign property, the test is on cost amount rather than market value, and the filing carries its own penalty regime whether or not any tax was owing. Where a foreign affiliate is involved, Form T1134 can apply as well. We build the property list from actual statements rather than memory, file for each year required, and where years were missed we bring them forward through a Voluntary Disclosures Program application on Form RC199, which when accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full.
How do I avoid being taxed twice on the same income?
Through credits claimed properly on both sides, and through preparing the two returns in a sequence rather than in isolation. On the Canadian side you are taxed on worldwide income as a resident under ITA subsection 2(1), and relief for tax paid to the United States comes through the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126, which is computed by country and by income type rather than as one estimated figure. On the American side relief comes through Form 1116 or Form 2555. The failure we see most often is not a rule problem at all: it is two preparers who have never exchanged a number, each claiming relief for tax paid in the other country on a basis the other return does not support. Both returns look fine individually and the combined result is more tax than was ever owed. We prepare the Canadian side, hand your American preparer the figures in the form they need, and review each return's relief against the other before anything is filed.
Are my RRSP and my Canadian pension reportable?
The RRSP is the one piece of good news in this area, in the sense that it is specifically addressed by the Canada–United States Tax Convention rather than left to general principles, including Article XVIII on pensions. We identify the article that governs and we do not assert what it produces for your particular plan, because the mechanics and any election involved are a US-licensed preparer's work and the detail matters. Canadian pensions, a RRIF, CPP and OAS each have their own treatment, and the fact that a payment is taxed in one country does not tell you how it is treated in the other. What we do on our side is make sure every plan and pension you hold appears on the schedule we build, with its character and its payments documented, so your American preparer is working from a complete picture. We state no rate, no inclusion percentage and no threshold for any pension or plan on this page.
I have not filed US returns in years. How bad is it and what do I do?
Common, and far better dealt with now than after a notice. Be aware there are two cleanups, not one, and they are handled by different people. On the Canadian side, we reconstruct and file your unfiled T1 years and any missed Form T1135 filings, and bring you forward through a Voluntary Disclosures Program application on Form RC199, which when accepted under the general program cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief. Late Canadian filing otherwise costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month up to twelve months, so the oldest year goes first. On the American side, routes back into compliance exist — the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures and the delinquent FBAR and international information return submission procedures are the ones people have usually heard of. We name them so you know they exist. We assert no eligibility criterion, lookback period, penalty figure or outcome for any of them, and we coordinate timing with your American preparer, because the two disclosures interact.
Can I just give up my US citizenship and stop all of this?
People ask, and it is the one question on this page we will not take a single step into. Renouncing citizenship has tax consequences under the expatriation rules, including Form 8854 and tests that can apply to someone who has held status for a long time, and it has consequences under immigration and nationality law that have nothing to do with tax at all. Both sets belong to specialists: the tax side to a preparer or counsel licensed in the United States, the status side to immigration counsel. We state no test, no threshold, no figure and no outcome. What we will say is the practical part. Renunciation is not a way to make unfiled years disappear, and it is not a decision to make from a position of not knowing what your compliance position actually is. Get the filings and the schedule of your accounts straight first, then take the question to the right people with real facts in front of them.
My Canadian bank asked me where I was born. Why?
Because Canadian financial institutions report certain account information under FATCA, the intergovernmental arrangement for exchanging financial account information, and identifying whether an account holder may be a US person is part of that. The practical point for you is simple and worth stating plainly: the information about your Canadian accounts is already capable of moving between the two systems, so a compliance position built on the assumption that nobody is looking is not a position at all. We do not state any reporting threshold, any particular institution's obligations, or what any authority will do with the information, because that is not ours to assert. What we do is make sure your reporting on both sides is complete and consistent, so there is no gap between what you have filed and what is reported about you. If a bank has already asked and you have unfiled years, that is a reason to move sooner rather than a reason to worry quietly.
I was born in the United States but left as a baby. Does any of this apply to me?
Possibly, and this situation is common enough to have a nickname people use for it. Whether you hold US citizenship is a question of nationality law, not tax law, and we do not answer it — that belongs to immigration or nationality counsel, and it is worth getting a clear answer rather than assuming in either direction. What we can tell you is the shape of the consequence. If you are a US citizen, the filing obligation follows the citizenship regardless of where you have lived or how little connection you have, and the same questions about Canadian registered plans, Canadian funds and any corporation you own apply to you as to anyone else. We state no threshold, no penalty and no relief criterion. There are procedures that exist for people in exactly this position, including relief procedures for certain former citizens, and we name them without asserting eligibility. The first step is establishing your status with counsel, not filing anything.
Do I still have a state tax obligation from where I used to live?
Possibly, and it is worth asking rather than assuming, because American state tax is a separate system from American federal tax with its own rules about residence and sourcing. A person can have their federal position in good order and a state question still sitting open, usually tied to where they last lived, property they kept, or income still sourced there. We will not tell you which state, what creates an obligation, what any rate or threshold is, or whether a return is required, because we are not licensed to answer it and a guess from a Canadian accountant's web page is worse than no answer. Put it to a preparer licensed in the United States, and raise it specifically — a federal-only review will not surface it. On our side, what matters is that any American tax you actually pay, state or federal, is identified and documented so it is available for the ITA section 126 foreign tax credit computation rather than lost in a general expense line.
