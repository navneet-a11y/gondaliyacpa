Authorities, Bylaws and the Veterinary Line

Pet sitting is not a licensed or regulated profession in Ontario, and nothing on this page suggests otherwise. What is real is this. The Veterinarians Act restricts the practice of veterinary medicine, with the College of Veterinarians of Ontario as regulator, and where that boundary falls for anything you are asked to do during a stay is a question for your own counsel and your client's veterinarian, never for us. The Provincial Animal Welfare Services Act, 2019, enforced by Animal Welfare Services, applies to anyone with custody of an animal. Municipal business licensing and animal bylaws are real and differ by municipality, so they are confirmed with the municipality rather than assumed. The Employment Standards Act, 2000 governs entitlements once you have employees and is employment counsel's domain. Liability and bonding premiums and professional association dues are each a real annual cost that belongs in the ledger.

Toronto Pet Sitting Business — A Fully-Deposited December Taxed a Year Early

The problem: A Toronto sitting business took deposits from September onwards for Christmas and March break coverage, and its fiscal year ended December 31. Every dollar collected had been taken into income on the day it cleared, including deposits for stays in January, February and March that nobody had sat yet, and for bookings that would later be cancelled when a client's trip fell through. The corporation was paying tax on work it had not done, in the year it had the least cash left after paying sitters through the autumn.

What we did: We rebuilt the booking calendar against the deposit ledger, identified the portion of each deposit relating to stays not yet served, reserved it under ITA paragraph 20(1)(m), carried a liability for bookings still cancellable, and restated the prior year on the same basis.

The result:

  • $214,000 of deposits reserved into the year the stays were served
  • $26,108 of corporate tax deferred to the following year
  • Deposits received in advance now reported monthly, not discovered at year-end

Mississauga Multi-Sitter Business — Three Products Billed as One Line

The problem: A Mississauga business sold three genuinely different things: a single drop-in visit, a package of daily visits across a client's trip, and a live-in overnight stay where the sitter slept in the house. All three went into one revenue account, and every sitter payment went into one subcontract account. The owner could not say which of the three products was carrying the company, so every pricing conversation was an argument about instinct rather than a decision made on the company's own numbers.

What we did: We rebuilt the chart of accounts with the three products separated, allocated sitter cost to the product that incurred it, separated insurance and bonding from general overhead, and set up a monthly report that shows each product's revenue and direct cost side by side.

The result:

  • Three revenue streams separated and reported every month
  • $17,400 of sitter cost reallocated to the product that incurred it
  • Pricing decisions now made on the company's own figures

Ottawa House Sitting Business — Nothing to Pledge, So Nothing to Lend Against

The problem: An Ottawa house sitting business wanted to buy a retiring sitter's client list. It had no premises, no leasehold, no kennel and no stock, so when it approached a lender there was nothing on the balance sheet to secure against and the file went nowhere. Its bookkeeping compounded the problem: advance deposits sat inside sales, so the statements showed equity the business did not own and no visible contract book at all.

What we did: We prepared CSRS 4200 compilation engagement financial statements across two fiscal years with deposits received in advance restated as a liability, the recurring client book and its receivable ledger presented explicitly, and the acquired list set up in Class 14.1 at 5% with the goodwill allocation documented in the purchase agreement.

The result:

  • Two years of compiled statements a lender could actually read
  • Acquisition financed against the contract book, not against assets
  • $96,000 client list recorded in Class 14.1 instead of expensed

Pet Sitting Accounting & Tax FAQs

Should I incorporate my pet sitting business?
Incorporating gives you a separate legal entity, which matters in a business that holds client keys and works unsupervised inside occupied homes, plus roughly a 12.2% Ontario combined rate on the first $500,000 of active income under ITA section 125 against a personal rate up to 53.53% when unincorporated. The decision turns on whether you earn more than you withdraw, because that surplus is what a corporation lets you defer. There is a second reason specific to this industry: a client book of repeat households is an asset a corporation can hold, finance and eventually sell in a way a sole proprietor cannot, and it is almost the only asset this business has. When it makes sense, we handle the section 85 rollover on Form T2057.
Do I charge HST on pet sitting, and when do I have to register?
Pet sitting is an ordinary taxable supply. There is no health care exemption for animals, so unlike some human services your work does not sit in an exempting provision, and the rate in Ontario is 13%. Registration becomes mandatory once your taxable supplies pass $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters, measured on a rolling basis rather than on a calendar year, and place of supply generally follows the recipient's address. This is the single event most owner-operators in this field are unprepared for, because many start well below the threshold with a handful of neighbours and then cross it during one busy summer or one heavy Christmas season without noticing. We track it for you and register before the deadline rather than after, and once you are registered the input tax credits on your vehicle, equipment, software and professional fees start coming back.
A client pays a deposit in October for a stay in December. When is that income?
The amount is included in income when you receive it under ITA paragraph 12(1)(a), because it is a payment on account of services not yet rendered, and then reserved out under ITA paragraph 20(1)(m) to the extent the service has not yet been performed by your year-end. In practice that means a deposit for a December stay is reserved at an October or November year-end and brought back into income when the stay happens. This matters more in pet sitting than in almost any other local service, because your calendar fills around other people's travel and the deposits cluster in holidays and school breaks. A year-end that lands immediately beside a fully-deposited holiday season will overstate the year badly unless the reserve is calculated booking by booking rather than estimated.
What happens when a client cancels and I keep part of the deposit?
It is reviewed rather than assumed, and there are two separate provisions in play. ETA section 182 deals with an amount paid or forfeited where an agreement for a taxable supply is breached, modified or terminated, and ETA section 181.2 deals with gift certificates. Whether either reaches a particular charge of yours depends on the facts and on the actual wording of your booking and cancellation terms, so we read the terms and reach a conclusion for your business instead of applying a label by reflex. We do not characterise your deposit as a gift certificate and we do not characterise your cancellation charge as a forfeited deposit without doing that work. Cancellations are endemic here because clients' trips fall through, so it is worth getting the treatment right once and then applying it consistently.
Are my sitters employees or independent contractors?
That is a question of fact and we will not answer it from a distance. Engaging sitters as subcontractors is the norm in this field, and the test is the set of factors in CRA guide RC4110: control over how and when the work is done, who supplies the tools and the vehicle, whether the worker can subcontract or hire helpers, and the chance of profit against the risk of loss. In a sitting business some specific facts carry real weight on those factors, including who sets the visit schedule, who holds the client relationship, who holds the keys and who bears the consequence if something goes wrong. What the invoice says and what the parties called the arrangement do not decide it. We run the analysis, document it, and tell you where your exposure actually sits so you can make the call with your own advisers.
Do I file T4A slips for my sitters?
For the ones who are genuinely contractors, yes. Filing the slips supports the deduction you claimed for those payments and makes the classification question visible to you before a reviewer raises it. Where somebody is in fact an employee, the obligations are different: source deductions withheld and remitted on the PD7A by the 15th of the following month, with CRA's late-remittance penalty on source deductions reaching 10% on a graduated scale, T4 slips and the T4 Summary filed by the last day of February, WSIB coverage from the first hire, and Ontario Employer Health Tax once payroll passes the $1,000,000 exemption. A large subcontract line with no slips behind it is one of the most reliable audit triggers in this industry. Missed slips for past years can be brought forward through a voluntary disclosure.
My business has no premises and almost no assets. Can I still get financing, and what is it worth if I sell?
Yes, but not against assets, because there are not any. With no shop, no kennel, no leasehold and no stock, the balance sheet of a sitting business is close to empty and there is almost nothing to pledge. What a lender can actually underwrite is the contract book of repeat households and the receivable ledger behind it, and that only works if the statements present them properly. A CSRS 4200 compilation engagement across two fiscal years, with deposits received in advance restated as a liability rather than hidden in sales and the recurring client book shown explicitly, is the document that moves the decision. The same logic drives value on a sale: a buyer is buying the client relationships, which land in Class 14.1 at 5% as goodwill and an acquired client list, and deferred bookings are deducted from the price rather than added to it because the buyer inherits those stays. A share sale may access the $1.25M Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption under ITA 110.6 where the shares qualify.
What CCA class is my vehicle, and can I deduct my mileage between client homes?
Travel between client homes is the reason a vehicle is usually the largest capital item in a sitting business, so this is worth getting right. A vehicle generally sits in Class 10. Where it meets the definition of an automobile in ITA subsection 248(1) and cost more than the prescribed limit, it falls into Class 10.1 instead, where each vehicle sits alone in its own class and neither recapture nor terminal loss arises on disposal. We do not assert that your particular vehicle is or is not an automobile for that purpose, and we do not quote the prescribed limit or any per-kilometre figure here, because both are determined on your facts and the current legislation at the time we file. What we will tell you is that the deduction stands or falls on a mileage logbook recording the business and personal kilometres, and a reconstructed log produced after a CRA letter is worth far less than one kept as you drive.
Are my insurance and bonding premiums deductible?
Premiums paid to carry on the business are deductible, and in a sitting business they are a substantial and recurring line rather than an afterthought. General liability cover, care-custody-and-control cover for animals in your charge, and bonding where staff hold client keys are commonly a condition of being engaged at all, which is why we set them up as their own accounts rather than burying them in general overhead. What we will not do is tell you what any policy covers. We state no limit, no deductible, no exclusion, no retroactive date and no period, and we do not tell you what a client or anyone else requires you to carry. That belongs with your broker and, where the stakes justify it, your counsel. Note also that the exposure in this business is not only to the animal: you have sole access to an unoccupied house and to everything in it, and we offer no liability conclusion on any of that.
Can you tell me whether I am allowed to give a client's pet its medication during a stay?
No, and that is a deliberate line rather than an evasion. The practice of veterinary medicine in Ontario is restricted under the Veterinarians Act, with the College of Veterinarians of Ontario as the regulator, and where the boundary falls between looking after an animal and doing something that is on the far side of that restriction is a legal question with real consequences. Administering medication during a stay is one of the most common requests clients make and it is exactly the kind of question we will not answer. We are your accountants. We give no animal-health, medication, behaviour, handling, breed, nutrition or welfare guidance of any kind, and we have no view on what any particular task involves. Settle it with your own legal counsel and with the client's veterinarian, get the answer in writing, and then come to us about how the cost and the risk are recorded.
Do I need a municipal business licence to pet sit?
Municipal business licensing and animal-related bylaws are real, and they differ from one municipality to the next, so we will not tell you what your city requires. We do not state any municipality's licence requirement, animal limit, zoning rule, home-business rule, fee or threshold, because those change and the only reliable answer comes from the municipality itself. Call your municipal licensing office, ask specifically about operating a pet sitting business and about running the administrative side from your home, and get the answer in writing. Two related points that are worth saying plainly: pet sitting is not a licensed or regulated profession in Ontario, so there is no provincial college and no protected title, and nothing we say should be read as implying that any bonding, screening or background check is legally required. Separately, the Provincial Animal Welfare Services Act, 2019 and Animal Welfare Services apply to anyone with custody of an animal, and we assert no standard, requirement, inspection power or penalty under it.
How should I account for overnight and live-in stays differently from drop-in visits?
As three products rather than one, because that is what they are. A single drop-in visit, a package of daily visits across a client's trip, and a live-in overnight stay where the sitter sleeps in the house carry three different rates and three different costs to deliver, and a business that runs them all through one revenue account and one subcontract account genuinely cannot tell which one earns. We separate them in the chart of accounts, allocate the sitter cost to the product that incurred it, and report revenue and direct cost by product every month. The overnight product carries one further consideration. How the time a sitter spends overnight in a client's home is treated and paid engages the Employment Standards Act, 2000 where that person is an employee, and those entitlements are real. We state no rate, no multiplier, no threshold and no overnight or sleeping-time rule here; quantifying them is employment counsel's job, and the answer feeds straight into what the product actually costs you.
What can a pet sitting business write off?
Vehicle costs including fuel, insurance, maintenance and parking supported by a mileage logbook, general liability and care-custody-and-control insurance, bonding premiums, sitter and staff costs, scheduling and booking software subscriptions, advertising, payment processing fees, carriers, crates, leashes and cleaning supplies consumed in the work, mobile phone and data, professional association dues, and accounting and legal fees. On capital, the vehicle goes to Class 10 or Class 10.1, equipment and office fittings to Class 8 at 20%, computers to Class 50 at 55%, software to Class 12 at 100%, and goodwill or an acquired client list to Class 14.1 at 5%, all on Schedule 8. There is no Class 13 leasehold claim here because there is no premises. A bad debt on an unpaid client invoice is deductible under ITA paragraph 20(1)(p). Whether a workspace in your home is deductible is reviewed on your own facts and we assert nothing about it in advance.
