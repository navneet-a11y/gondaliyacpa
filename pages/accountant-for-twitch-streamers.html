The Donation Question

  • This is the question streamers ask most and get answered worst, so we take it seriously instead of giving you the confident one-liner you have probably already been given on Discord.
  • A genuine gift, where the viewer receives nothing in return and is simply being generous, is generally not income. That is the principle, and it is narrower in practice than most streamers hope.
  • A payment tied to something — a shout-out, an on-screen alert, a name in the credits, a request played, access to anything — generally is business income, because the viewer received consideration.
  • The practical consequence is that most of what arrives through a tip button on a monetised channel is business income, and treating all of it as gifts is the position most likely to fail on review.
  • We settle it on your actual setup and document the reasoning at the time, rather than reconstructing an argument after CRA has already formed a view about your channel.

Hobby or Business Review

  • Whether your channel is a business turns on whether there is a source of income at all, judged on how commercially you behave rather than on whether you made a profit this year.
  • This decides something concrete: if there is a source of income, your start-up losses are deductible against your other income; if there is not, they are not available at all.

Form W-8BEN and Treaty Support

  • Twitch pays from a United States payer, which means the cross-border question arises before any money reaches you rather than at tax time when it is too late to fix cheaply.
  • Form W-8BEN is how a Canadian resident claims treaty benefits with a United States payer, and a streamer who never completed one properly is the classic avoidable-withholding case we see.
  • Article XII of the Canada-United States Tax Convention deals with royalties, and whether a given platform payment is characterised that way is the payer's determination rather than ours.
  • We state no United States withholding rate, no threshold and no treaty outcome anywhere on this page, because those are specific and a number from a website is exactly what fails when tested.

Foreign Tax Credit Claims

  • Where United States tax was actually withheld from your payouts, the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126 relieves the double taxation on your Canadian return, and it is routinely never claimed.
  • The credit is based on tax actually paid rather than on any amount that merely appeared on a statement, so we work from the documentation rather than from the gross-to-net difference.
  • This is where we most often find money on a streamer's file: several years of withholding on platform income with no credit ever claimed against the Canadian tax on the same earnings.
  • We compute the credit by country and by income type as the rules require, rather than lumping everything into one figure that cannot be supported if a reviewer asks how it was built.
  • We correct prior years through T1 adjustment requests where they are still open, which frequently produces a refund that more than covers the cost of having the work done properly.

GST/HST for Creators

  • Registration is required once your worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, and sponsorship income counts toward that threshold too.
  • Most streamers cross the threshold through sponsorship and merchandise rather than through subscriptions alone, so we track all of the revenue together rather than watching only the platform payout.
  • Supplies to non-residents are often zero-rated, which matters enormously on these files because a Canadian streamer's audience and sponsors are frequently outside Canada entirely, so you can register while charging tax on very little.
  • Ontario is 13%, and where you are charging a Canadian sponsor the place of supply rules set the rate by their province rather than by yours, which catches creators invoicing across the country.
  • We test whether voluntary registration below the threshold is worth it, which depends on how much of your cost base — equipment especially — actually carried recoverable tax.

Equipment and Home Studio

  • Your PC, camera, microphone, lighting and capture card are capital property claimed through capital cost allowance over time, not expensed in full in the year you bought them.
  • Each is prorated for personal use, because a computer you also game on personally and a camera you also take on holiday are not wholly business assets however much they feel like tools.
  • Business-use-of-home under ITA subsection 18(12) is deductible only against the income from the business, with the excess carried forward, which bites hardest in an early year with little revenue.
  • The home claim is based on the share of the home used for the activity, measured rather than estimated, and we keep that measurement and the reasoning on file for a later review.
  • Games, subscriptions and internet are mixed-use, deductible in their business proportion on a reasonable documented basis, and claiming the whole lot is the most common error on these returns.

Toronto Streamer — Four Years of Withholding, No Credit

The problem: A Toronto streamer had been partnered for four years and reporting her platform deposits as income. United States tax had been withheld from every payout because she had never completed a Form W-8BEN properly, and nobody had ever told her the withheld amount was creditable in Canada. She had also booked the net deposit rather than the gross, so she had understated her revenue and claimed no credit for tax she had definitely paid.

What we did: We rebuilt the gross income from the platform dashboard, identified the United States tax withheld in each year, and claimed the foreign tax credit under ITA section 126 for the years still open through T1 adjustment requests. We then got her W-8BEN position sorted with the platform going forward and set her up to export the dashboard monthly.

The result:

  • Gross revenue restated and the withheld US tax identified
  • Foreign tax credit claimed for the open years, producing a refund
  • W-8BEN position fixed so the withholding stopped recurring

Brampton Creator — Everything Was a Gift

The problem: A Brampton streamer had been told in a Discord server that donations are gifts and therefore never taxable, and had reported nothing from his tip button for two years — a meaningful share of his total income. His setup gave donors on-screen alerts, name credits and song requests. He had also never claimed a single deduction, having been told that if the money was not income the costs were not deductible either.

What we did: We explained plainly why most of those payments were business income, since donors received consideration, and reported them for the open years. We then claimed everything he had been entitled to all along: capital cost allowance on his setup, the business share of internet and subscriptions, and the home studio under ITA subsection 18(12). The deductions absorbed much of the additional income.

The result:

  • Donation income reported correctly on the facts of his setup
  • Equipment, home studio and mixed-use costs finally claimed
  • Net additional tax far below what he had feared

Ottawa Channel — A Hobby Loss That Was Not Deductible

The problem: An Ottawa software developer streamed around his job and had deducted three years of channel losses against his salary — a new computer, a capture card, a full internet bill and the whole cost of every game he played. The channel had almost no viewers and no sponsorship. Nobody had asked whether there was a source of income at all, and the equipment had been expensed in full rather than treated as capital property.

What we did: We assessed the position honestly and told him what he did not want to hear: on those facts the activity did not support a business characterisation, so the losses were not deductible. We corrected the returns proactively before a reassessment arrived, restated the equipment as capital property, and set out what would need to change for the channel to be a business.

The result:

  • Position corrected proactively instead of under reassessment
  • Equipment restated as capital property, not a full expense
  • Clear criteria set out for when the channel becomes a business

Twitch Streamer Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I pay tax on Twitch income in Canada?
Yes, where the channel is a business, and in that case all of it belongs on your return — subscriptions, bits, ad revenue, bounties, sponsorships and affiliate income. The mistake we see most is not failing to report; it is reporting the wrong number. Your platform payout is net of the platform's share and sometimes net of United States tax withheld before the money ever left. A streamer who books the deposit has understated their revenue and, worse, silently thrown away the credit for tax they definitely paid. So the correct starting point is the platform dashboard broken down by revenue type, not your bank statement. Everything is then converted to Canadian dollars on a consistent documented basis, which matters more than people expect across a year of monthly payouts and moving exchange rates. If the channel is genuinely not a business, the answer is different — see the hobby question below.
Are viewer donations taxable?
Usually yes, and we are going to give you the honest answer rather than the comfortable one you may have been given elsewhere. A genuine gift — where the viewer receives absolutely nothing in return and is simply being generous — is generally not income. That principle is real, but it is much narrower in practice than most streamers hope. If the payment is tied to anything at all — an on-screen alert, a shout-out, a name in the credits, a song request, access to a channel or role — the viewer has received consideration, and the payment is generally business income. On a monetised channel with a tip button and alerts, most of what arrives is therefore business income, and treating all of it as gifts is the position most likely to fail on review. We settle it on your actual setup and document the reasoning at the time, rather than building an argument after CRA has formed a view.
Is my streaming a hobby or a business?
It turns on whether there is a source of income, judged on how commercially you behave rather than on whether you turned a profit this year, and the answer has one very concrete consequence: if there is a source of income, your start-up losses are deductible against your other income; if there is not, they are not available at all. A channel with forty viewers, no sponsorship and a new gaming PC is in a genuinely different position from a partnered streamer with contracts, scheduled streams and reinvestment. We will tell you which one you look like, including when the answer is unwelcome — we have had to tell a client that three years of deducted losses were not supportable, and correcting that proactively cost him far less than a reassessment would have. Where the activity genuinely is commercial we document the evidence now, because that file is what defends an early-year loss later.
What is Form W-8BEN and do I need one?
It is how a Canadian resident claims treaty benefits with a United States payer, and Twitch pays from the United States, so it matters before any money reaches you rather than at tax time. A streamer who never completed one properly is the classic avoidable-withholding case: tax comes off every payout that might not have needed to. Article XII of the Canada-United States Tax Convention deals with royalties, and whether a given platform payment is characterised that way, and at what rate, is the payer's determination. What we will not do is state a United States withholding rate, threshold or treaty outcome on this page — those are specific, they are not ours to assert, and a number taken from a website is exactly the kind of position that fails when tested. We frame the question for the platform and, where it genuinely matters, for a preparer licensed in the United States.
Twitch withheld US tax from my payout. Can I get it back?
You can generally get relief for it in Canada, which is not quite the same as getting it back from the United States, and it is the single most common place we find money on a streamer's file. Where United States tax was actually withheld, the foreign tax credit in ITA section 126 relieves the double taxation on your Canadian return. The credit is based on tax actually paid rather than on anything that merely appeared on a statement, so we work from the documentation. It is computed by country and by income type as the rules require, not lumped into one figure. We routinely find several years of withholding with no credit ever claimed, and where those years are still open we correct them through T1 adjustment requests — which frequently produces a refund that more than covers the cost of the work. Separately, we get the W-8BEN position fixed so the withholding stops recurring.
Do I need to register for GST/HST as a streamer?
Once your worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, yes — and most streamers cross that line through sponsorship and merchandise rather than through subscriptions, so we track everything together rather than watching only the platform payout. Two points specific to this industry. First, supplies to non-residents are often zero-rated, which matters enormously because a Canadian streamer's viewers and sponsors are frequently outside Canada entirely; you can be required to register while charging tax on very little. Second, where you invoice a Canadian sponsor, the place of supply rules set the rate by their province rather than yours, with Ontario at 13%. We also test whether voluntary registration below the threshold is worth it, which depends on how much of your cost base — a studio build especially — actually carried recoverable tax.
Can I deduct my gaming PC and camera?
Yes, but not all at once and not in full. Your PC, camera, microphone, lighting and capture card are capital property, claimed through capital cost allowance over time rather than expensed entirely in the year you bought them — which is the error we correct most often on self-prepared streamer returns. Each item is then prorated for personal use, because a computer you also game on recreationally and a camera you also take on holiday are not wholly business assets however much they feel like work tools. The proration needs to be reasonable and documented rather than a round number you picked. The upside is that this is a real and often substantial deduction that many streamers never claim at all, particularly those who were told that because their donations were gifts their costs were not deductible either. Both halves of that advice were wrong.
Can I deduct the games I play on stream?
In their business proportion, yes — and that proportion is almost never one hundred per cent. Games, subscriptions and your internet connection are mixed-use: genuinely needed for the channel, and also genuinely enjoyed personally. The business share is deductible on a reasonable and documented basis, and claiming the entire cost is the most common single error on these returns. A game you bought, streamed once and then played for sixty hours privately is not a wholly business expense, and a reviewer will see that pattern in your own stream history. The practical approach is to keep a simple record as you go rather than reconstructing it in April: what you bought, when, and whether it was streamed. The same logic applies to your internet, your phone and any device with personal use in it. Done properly this is still a meaningful deduction; done carelessly it is the thing that invites a closer look at everything else.
Can I claim a home office for my streaming setup?
Usually yes, with one limit that catches people in exactly the year they most want the deduction. Business-use-of-home expenses are deductible under ITA subsection 18(12) only against the income from that business, and any excess carries forward to a later year rather than being lost. So in a first year with a big studio build and little revenue, the claim may be capped and carried rather than reducing your other income — which is a different outcome from the one most streamers assume. The claim is based on the share of your home used for the activity, measured rather than estimated, and we keep the measurement and the reasoning on file because a round percentage nobody can account for is the first thing a review questions. If the setup is in a bedroom you also sleep in, the share reflects that honestly.
Are free products sponsors send me taxable?
Generally yes, at fair market value, and this surprises almost everyone. Where a company sends you a keyboard, a chair or a headset in exchange for promotion, coverage or a mention, you have received consideration for a service and the value is business income even though no money moved. The same item may then be deductible as a business expense or claimed as capital property depending on what it is and how it is used, so the net effect is often modest — but reporting nothing at all is not the right answer. Genuinely unsolicited gifts with no expectation of anything in return sit differently, and that distinction is the same one that runs through the donation question above. We work through what the arrangement actually required of you, keep a record of what arrived and what it was worth, and treat it consistently year to year rather than only when it is convenient.
Should I incorporate my channel?
Only when your numbers say so. Incorporation brings the small business deduction under ITA section 125 against the $500,000 limit, the ability to leave profits in the company taxed at corporate rates, and a cleaner structure if the channel becomes a real business with staff. It also brings a corporate return, meaningfully higher compliance cost, and a structure that is harder to unwind than to set up. For a streamer earning a few thousand a year the trade is poor; for one earning six figures with profits they do not need to draw personally, it is often good. We model it on your actual figures, including salary against dividends, the effect on your RRSP room, and the income record a mortgage lender will want to see — which for a self-employed creator is a real consideration that rarely gets mentioned. We say plainly when the answer is to wait, and we revisit it annually.
I never reported my streaming income. What happens now?
You come forward, and it is usually far less painful than the dread suggests. Late filing costs 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per month to twelve months, so we file the oldest unfiled year first to stop the penalty compounding. For years beyond the normal reach we bring the position forward on Form RC199 before CRA contacts you — confirming the disclosure is genuinely voluntary is the single condition that makes the application valid — which cancels penalties in full and gives roughly 50% interest relief. Here is the part that surprises people: the corrected years frequently produce far less tax than feared, because two things were available all along and never claimed. The deductions — equipment, home studio, the business share of internet and subscriptions — and the foreign tax credit for United States tax withheld from your payouts.
