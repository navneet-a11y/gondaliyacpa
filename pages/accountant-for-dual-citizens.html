Renunciation & Form 8854 Coordination

Children, Citizenship & the SSN Question

  • American citizenship can pass to a child born in Canada to an American parent, and the practical consequence is that a child may carry a filing obligation from birth with nobody in the family aware of it.
  • The document that evidences it is a Consular Report of Birth Abroad, and whether citizenship actually passed in your family's case depends on the parent's own history and is a State Department determination.
  • Here is the point that saves real money: a United States citizen cannot obtain an ITIN. An ITIN exists for people not eligible for a Social Security Number, and a citizen is eligible.
  • So the Form W-7 route used by non-citizens is simply wrong for this group, and we have seen families waste months and fees applying for a number they were never entitled to receive.
  • On the Canadian side we keep the family's filings clean, including RESP reporting, the Canada Child Benefit position and each child's own return once they have income of their own.

FATCA, Part XVIII & Your Bank's Questions

Dual Citizen Accounting & Tax FAQs

Do I have to file US taxes if I live in Canada and have never lived there?
The United States taxes its citizens on worldwide income wherever they live, so citizenship rather than residence is what creates the obligation. That is the general rule, and it is the part most people in your position have never been told. What we will not do is tell you what you personally must file, because that depends on your income, your accounts and your own history, and it belongs to a preparer licensed in the United States. What we can tell you plainly is the Canadian half, which is usually the half people have got wrong in the anxious direction: your Canadian return does not change at all because of American citizenship. Canada taxes you because you live here, under ITA subsection 2(1), and Canadian tax law has nothing to say about your citizenship in either country. We build the account inventory a licensed American preparer needs and hand the question to them properly framed.
Does my child born in Canada have US citizenship?
Possibly, and we will not answer it, because the honest answer is that it is not ours to answer. American citizenship can pass to a child born in Canada to an American parent, but whether it did in your family's case depends on the parent's own history, and it is a determination for the State Department rather than for an accountant. The document that evidences it is a Consular Report of Birth Abroad. What we do is put the question in front of the people entitled to decide it, rather than guessing in either direction — and both directions cause harm, because a child wrongly assumed to be American gets filings they never needed, while a child wrongly assumed not to be accumulates years of unmet obligations. One practical point that follows: a child who is a United States citizen needs a Social Security Number, not an ITIN.
Do I need an ITIN?
No, and this is worth reading carefully, because it is the single most expensive misunderstanding we see on dual-citizen files. An ITIN is an individual taxpayer identification number for people who need to interact with the American tax system but are not eligible for a Social Security Number. A United States citizen is eligible for a Social Security Number. So a citizen does not get an ITIN, and the Form W-7 application route used by non-citizens is simply the wrong door. We have seen families spend months and real fees pursuing a number they were never entitled to receive, then have to start again. If you are a citizen and have no Social Security Number — common for someone born there who left as an infant, and for a child documented later — the step is a Social Security Number application, which is a United States process and not ours to run.
Why did my bank ask whether I am a US person?
Because Canadian law requires them to, and it is not about you specifically. Canada and the United States entered an intergovernmental agreement on account reporting, and Canada implemented it as Part XVIII of the Income Tax Act. The separate common reporting standard, which covers many other countries, sits in Part XIX. The obligation the form discharges is the financial institution's, not yours: the bank is identifying accounts it has to report, it is not assessing you, and it is not the IRS asking. Two practical notes. People sometimes refuse to answer out of alarm, which creates problems with the account rather than solving anything. And people sometimes tick a box carelessly without reading what the self-certification says. We go through it with you before you sign. We do not advise answering inaccurately, and the honest answer is almost always the cheapest over any horizon that matters.
Is my TFSA a problem?
It is a question, and it is the most common one on these files. A TFSA is a Canadian creation with no American equivalent, and for a United States person it can raise trust reporting on Form 3520 and Form 3520-A, while Canadian mutual funds or exchange-traded funds held inside it can raise Form 8621. Whether any of that applies to your plan is for a preparer licensed in the United States to determine, and we assert nothing about the outcome. Article XVIII is the treaty provision dealing with pensions and registered plans, and we name it without claiming what it does for your TFSA. The full treatment, including the RESP and RDSP equivalents and how people usually restructure, is on our page for US citizens living in Canada — we link there rather than repeat it, because the machinery is identical for a dual citizen.
Is my RESP a foreign trust?
It may be treated as one for American purposes, which is why an RESP opened in the ordinary course of saving for a child's education can raise Form 3520 and Form 3520-A questions for a United States person. We name that honestly and we stop there: whether a particular plan is a trust for American purposes, and what reporting follows, is a determination for a preparer licensed in the United States, and we assert nothing about it. What we do on the Canadian side is straightforward and useful. We keep the RESP reporting correct here, including the Canada Education Savings Grant position and the tax treatment of withdrawals when your child starts school. We also raise the question before a plan is opened where we can, because the awkward cases are almost always plans set up years ago by a parent who had no idea a second country might have a view about them.
How do I renounce, and does that end the tax side?
Those are two separate acts, and treating them as one is the most consequential mistake on these files. Renouncing citizenship is a consular act: you appear before a consular officer abroad, and the document produced is a Certificate of Loss of Nationality, issued by the State Department. That ends the citizenship. It does not file anything. The tax side is a separate filing, the expatriation statement on Form 8854, handled by a preparer licensed in the United States, and we assert no condition, consequence or outcome for it. Two things people get wrong in the other direction as well. Renunciation does not change your Canadian residency or your T1 obligation if you continue to live here. And the order matters: the American side is usually addressed before the appointment rather than after. We sequence the Canadian work around whatever your American preparer advises.
What is the saving clause, and does the treaty stop double taxation for me?
The Canada-United States Tax Convention does relieve double taxation in the ordinary case — Article XXIV is the double-tax relief provision, Article IV governs residence, and a treaty-based position is disclosed on Form 8833. But the treaty also contains a saving clause, in Article XXIX, which limits how far a country's treaty obligations go in relation to its own citizens. We name it because you will encounter the term and should know where it lives, and we deliberately do not tell you what it does to your position. Its effect is specific, it is the sort of thing that is argued rather than looked up, and an accountant who summarises it for you on a web page is doing you a disservice. The practical answer is that double taxation is usually addressed, and the mechanism that addresses it in your case is a question for a preparer licensed in the United States working alongside us.
I have never filed a US return. What do I do?
Start with records, not forms, and do not panic — this is the most common situation we see and it is almost always more manageable than it feels in the first week. Build a written inventory of every account and plan in your name: what it is, roughly when it opened, what it holds, and whether a Canadian corporation is in the picture. That one document is what a licensed American preparer needs before they can tell you anything useful, and it is the thing clients can actually produce themselves. Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures is the route commonly discussed for someone who has not filed; we name it so you know the term, and every eligibility condition, requirement and outcome belongs to a preparer licensed there. On our side we confirm the Canadian years are correct, fix any Form T1135 or T1134 that was missed, and bring anything genuinely unfiled forward on Form RC199.
I own a Canadian corporation. What does that mean?
It means your citizenship reaches your company, and the usual way owners find out is an adviser asking a question they cannot answer. For a United States citizen shareholder, a Canadian-controlled private corporation generally brings an American information return, Form 5471, with shareholder-level regimes sitting behind it. We name that and cross-link rather than re-developing it, because our page for US citizens living in Canada covers it at length and the position is the same for a dual citizen. What we do here is the Canadian work, properly: the corporation's bookkeeping and T2, the small business deduction under ITA section 125 with the $500,000 limit tracked across associated corporations, and the salary-versus-dividend comparison on the Canadian numbers — flagged, because that comparison can look different once a second country's treatment is in the picture.
What happens to my Canadian taxes if I move to the United States?
You emigrate, for Canadian purposes, even though you are a citizen of the country you are moving to and it feels like going home rather than leaving. That catches dual citizens harder than anyone. Departure triggers a deemed disposition of most property at fair market value under ITA subsection 128.1(4), which can produce a Canadian tax bill in a year you have no cash to pay it. The departure-year reporting goes on Form T1161, and where the tax would otherwise be payable immediately there is an election to defer it on Form T1244, with security arranged as required. Your departure date is a question of fact on residential ties per Income Tax Folio S5-F1-C1, determinable on Form NR73 where it is genuinely unclear. The planning point is timing: a deemed disposition landing in the same year as a bonus or a business sale is a choice, not an accident.
Should I renounce?
We do not answer that, and we want to be clear the reason is not caution for its own sake. Renunciation is irreversible, and it reaches things that have nothing to do with tax: how easily you can live or work there later, whether you can care for an aging parent across the border, how an inheritance from an American relative is handled, your children's position, and for many people a sense of who they are. An accountant who pushes that decision because the annual compliance is tiresome is substituting their own judgement for yours on a matter that is not theirs. What we do is make the tax consequences legible so you can decide with real information: the Canadian consequences, which are usually smaller than people expect, and the sequence if you go ahead. Then we support the decision you make, either way, without comment.
