## Quick Answer — TL;DR

A new corporation's first-year calendar is set by its incorporation date, its chosen year-end and the CRA program accounts it opens.

- The first tax year starts on the incorporation date and cannot run longer than 53 weeks [1].
- The first T2 is due six months after that first year-end, even with no income and no tax owing [2].
- Any tax owing is due two months after year-end, or three months for an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) [2].
- A new corporation generally does not pay instalments in its first tax year, because there is no prior-year tax base [1][3].
- GST/HST starts once you register, either voluntarily or because you pass the $30,000 small supplier limit [4][5].
- Payroll remittances start with the first salary and are generally due on the 15th of the following month [6].
- T4 and T5 slips are due by the last day of February after the calendar year in which you paid salary or dividends [7][8].
- The Ontario annual return is not due until after the first anniversary, within six months of that date [9].

## What Are the First-Year Corporation Filing Requirements in Canada?

A new corporation must file a T2 return every year, keep its registry information current, and file GST/HST, payroll and slip returns for each program account it opens.

The base of the new corporation filing deadlines Canada sets is the T2 return. Every resident corporation files a T2 for every tax year, even if it had no income, no activity and no tax owing [2]. The first T2 also includes Schedule 24, the first-time filer schedule [2].

Other filings depend on what the corporation does. Registering for GST/HST adds returns. Paying a salary adds remittances and T4 slips. Paying a dividend adds T5 slips. Doing business in a second province adds an extra-provincial registration and that province's filings.

The registry side is separate from the CRA. An Ontario corporation reports to the Ontario Business Registry, and a federal corporation reports to Corporations Canada [9][12]. Changes to directors or the registered office have their own short deadlines.

> **Statutory Requirement:** Every resident corporation must file a T2 return for every tax year, including years with no income and no tax payable [1][2].

## How Does Your Fiscal Year-End Set the First-Year Calendar?

Your fiscal year-end fixes the T2 due date, the balance-due day and most other deadlines, so it is the first decision on a first-year calendar.

A corporation's first tax year starts on the incorporation date. You choose the year-end when you file the first T2, as long as the first year does not run longer than 53 weeks [1]. A corporation incorporated on April 15 could choose December 31, March 31 or any month-end up to the following April.

Changing the year-end later generally needs written approval from the CRA [2]. Please choose carefully the first time.

Common choices:

- **December 31.** It lines up with T4 and T5 slips, which follow the calendar year, so salary and dividend planning is simpler.
- **A quieter month.** Seasonal businesses often pick a slow month so the year-end count and records are easier.
- **A short first year.** This brings the first T2 forward. It can suit an owner who wants an early filing routine, but every tax year still needs its own return.

> **Pro Tip:** Pick a year-end at least two months away from your busiest season, so your books can be closed while you are not at peak workload.

## When Is the First T2 Corporate Tax Return Due?

The first T2 corporate tax return is due six months after the first tax year-end, the same rule that applies to every later year [1][2].

The first year T2 filing deadline counts from the year-end, not from the incorporation date. If the year ends on the last day of a month, the T2 is due on the last day of the sixth month after it [2]. A December 31 year-end means a June 30 filing date.

Since 2024 tax years, corporations must file their T2 electronically, apart from a few listed exceptions [2][13]. **2026 update:** mandatory T2 e-filing applies to all 2026 tax years, so a paper first return is no longer the default for a new corporation [EDITOR: verify the 2026 list of exempt corporations at the CRA source]. Not e-filing when required can draw a $1,000 penalty [13].

## When Is First-Year Tax Payable, and Do You Need Instalments?

First-year corporate tax is payable two months after the year-end, or three months for an eligible CCPC, and instalments generally do not apply in the first tax year [1][2][3].

Payment and filing have different deadlines. The balance-due day comes before the T2 deadline. Interest runs on any balance not paid by the balance-due day, even if the T2 is filed on time [2].

The three-month balance-due day applies to a CCPC that claims the small business deduction for the year or the previous year and meets the taxable-income condition with associated corporations [2]. [EDITOR: confirm how CRA applies the previous-year taxable income condition to a first tax year.] If you are unsure, please plan to pay by the two-month date.

Instalments are not required when tax payable for the current year or the previous year is $3,000 or less [1][3]. A new corporation has no previous year, so it generally does not pay instalments in its first year. In year two, if first-year tax was more than $3,000, instalments usually start. They are monthly, or quarterly for an eligible small CCPC [3].

Rates matter for budgeting the first payment. The combined Ontario small business rate is 12.2% on active business income up to the $500,000 business limit: 9% federal plus 3.2% Ontario. The combined general rate is 26.5% [14].

**Verdict:** Budget the first balance due at the small business rate only after confirming CCPC status and the business limit share. Otherwise use the general rate.

> **Risk Warning:** Many new owners send the tax with the T2 at month six. The balance was due at month two or three, so interest is charged for the gap [2].

## When Does a New Corporation Register for and File GST/HST?

A new corporation must register for GST/HST once it is no longer a small supplier, meaning taxable supplies over $30,000, and it may register voluntarily before that [4][5].

First-year GST/HST filing works on two tests. The small supplier limit is $30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in one calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters [4][5]. If one quarter goes over $30,000, the corporation stops being a small supplier from the sale that crossed the line. It must collect GST/HST on that sale and register within 29 days [5].

Filing frequency follows annual taxable supplies. A corporation with $1,500,000 or less can file annually. Above $1,500,000 up to $6,000,000 means quarterly, and above $6,000,000 means monthly [5]. Annual filers file within three months after the fiscal year-end. Monthly and quarterly filers file within one month after each period [5].

Voluntary registration lets a new corporation claim input tax credits on start-up costs. In return, it must charge GST/HST and file returns from then on.

> **Key Stat:** **$30,000.** Past this level of taxable supplies in one quarter or four consecutive quarters, a new corporation must register for GST/HST [5].

## What Payroll and Slip Deadlines Apply After Your First Paycheque?

Once a corporation pays a salary, it must open a payroll account, remit source deductions by the 15th of the next month as a regular remitter, and file T4 slips by the last day of February [6][7].

Payroll remittance deadlines for new corporations start with the first pay date. Source deductions include income tax, CPP contributions and EI premiums. Paying yourself dividends instead of a salary skips payroll. You still file T5 slips and the T5 Summary by the last day of February after the calendar year in which the dividends were paid [8].

Ontario adds two possible items. The Employer Health Tax (EHT) applies above the exemption, which is $1,000,000 of annual Ontario payroll for eligible employers [15]. WSIB registration is required within 10 days of hiring the first worker in a covered industry [16].

> **Key Stat:** **The 15th of the following month.** That is when a regular remitter's payroll source deductions are due, starting with the first pay date [6].

## What Corporate Registry Filings Are Due After Incorporation?

After incorporation, a corporation files an annual return with its registry each year and reports changes to directors, officers or the registered office within short statutory deadlines [9][12].

**Ontario (OBCA).** The annual return is filed through the Ontario Business Registry within six months after each anniversary of incorporation [9]. A corporation incorporated on April 15 files its first return between April 15 and October 15 of the following year. Changes to directors, officers or the registered office are reported within 15 days [9]. Ontario corporations must also keep a register of individuals with significant control (ISC) in the minute book [11].

**Federal (CBCA).** The annual return is filed with Corporations Canada within 60 days after each anniversary date, for a fee of $12 online [12] [EDITOR: verify 2026 figure]. CBCA corporations also file ISC information with Corporations Canada every year and within 15 days of a change [12].

**Minute book.** Organizational resolutions, by-laws, share issuances and annual resolutions belong in the minute book. Registries do not collect it, but banks, lenders, buyers and the CRA may ask to see it.

> **Key Stat:** **Within 6 months after the anniversary.** That is when an Ontario corporation files its annual return, not with the T2 and not in year one [9].

## How Do First-Year Calendars Differ Across the 10 Industries We Serve?

First-year calendars differ by industry because payroll, GST/HST status, regulator rules and cross-border activity change which filings each corporation triggers.

**Technology Startups & SaaS**
- Often voluntary GST/HST registration to recover start-up input tax credits.
- SR&ED claims may sit on the first T2 if eligible work started early.
- Foreign customers raise place-of-supply questions for GST/HST.

**E-commerce & Online Retailers**
- Can cross $30,000 in one quarter during a seasonal peak.
- Marketplace and payment-processor reports must reconcile to the books.
- Inventory count at year-end affects the first T2.

**Professional Services & Consulting**
- Personal services business review if one client controls the work.
- Year-end often December 31 to match T4 and T5 slips.
- HST on fees once past the small supplier limit.

**Health Tech, Medical Clinics & Physician Startups**
- A professional corporation needs the regulator's certificate before operating.
- Most physician services are GST/HST exempt; health tech products may not be.
- Dividends to family members need a TOSI review.

**Daycare, Childcare & CWELCC**
- CWELCC funding agreements carry their own reporting dates.
- Childcare services are generally GST/HST exempt.
- Payroll starts early because staff ratios apply from day one.

**Real Estate, PropTech & Holding Companies**
- Rental income may be specified investment business income, outside the small business deduction.
- Commercial rent is taxable for HST; residential rent is generally exempt.
- Associated corporations share one $500,000 business limit.

**Construction, Trades & Contractors**
- Contract payment reporting (T5018) is due six months after the reporting period end.
- WSIB registration within 10 days of hiring the first worker.
- HST usually starts quickly because job values are large.

**Restaurants + Food & Beverage**
- Payroll and tips from the first week.
- HST on most prepared food sales.
- Monthly or quarterly HST may suit cash flow better than annual.

**Transportation & Logistics + Trucking Owner-Operators**
- Personal services business risk if driving for one carrier.
- Vehicle and fuel records drive the first T2.
- HST input tax credits on fuel and equipment.

**Cross-Border & Internationally Expanding Startups**
- NR4 slips by March 31 and Part XIII withholding on payments to non-residents.
- T106 if transactions with related non-residents pass $1,000,000.
- T1134 for foreign affiliates, due 10 months after year-end.

## People Also Ask

**What does a new corporation have to file in its first year?**
A new corporation files a T2 return for its first tax year, even with no income. It also files GST/HST returns if registered, payroll remittances and T4 slips if it pays salary, and T5 slips if it pays dividends. Its first registry annual return comes after the first anniversary.

**When is a new corporation's first T2 due?**
The first T2 is due six months after the end of the corporation's first tax year. If the year ends on December 31, the T2 is due June 30. The deadline counts from the year-end you choose, not from the incorporation date.

**When do I pay tax for my corporation's first year?**
Tax is due two months after year-end, or three months for an eligible CCPC claiming the small business deduction. The payment date comes before the T2 filing date. Interest applies to any balance paid after the balance-due day.

**Does a new corporation pay instalments in year one?**
Generally no. Instalments are not required when tax for the current or previous year is $3,000 or less. A new corporation has no previous year, so the condition is usually met. Instalments usually start in year two if first-year tax was over $3,000.

**How long can a corporation's first fiscal year be?**
A corporation's tax year cannot be longer than 53 weeks. The first year starts on the incorporation date, so the year-end must fall within 53 weeks of it. The year-end is set when the first T2 is filed.

**Can I change my corporation's year-end later?**
Changing a corporation's year-end after the first T2 generally needs written approval from the CRA, with a valid business reason. Tax savings alone are usually not accepted as a reason. Please choose the year-end carefully before filing the first return.

**When must a new corporation register for GST/HST?**
A corporation must register once its taxable supplies pass $30,000 in one calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters. It must register within 29 days after it stops being a small supplier. It can also register voluntarily before that to claim input tax credits.

**How often does a new corporation file GST/HST?**
Filing frequency depends on annual taxable supplies. Corporations with $1,500,000 or less can file annually. Above $1,500,000 up to $6,000,000 is quarterly. Above $6,000,000 is monthly. Annual returns are due three months after the fiscal year-end.

**When are payroll remittances due for a new corporation?**
Regular remitters send source deductions by the 15th of the month after the pay date. That covers income tax withheld, CPP contributions and EI premiums where they apply. The payroll account must be open before the first remittance is due.

**When are T4 and T5 slips due?**
T4 slips for salary and T5 slips for dividends are due by the last day of February after the calendar year in which the amounts were paid. This applies whatever the corporation's fiscal year-end. Each slip goes to the recipient and the CRA.

**When is the Ontario annual return due?**
An Ontario corporation files its annual return through the Ontario Business Registry within six months after each anniversary of its incorporation date. The first return is due after the first anniversary, so it does not fall inside the first fiscal year for most corporations.

**What happens if my corporation files nothing in year one?**
Not filing the T2 can lead to late-filing penalties if tax is owed, and to CRA requests for the return. Not filing annual returns can lead to non-compliance status and, over time, dissolution. A nil-activity year still needs a T2.

**Do I need a minute book for a new corporation?**
Yes. Corporate statutes require records such as by-laws, resolutions, share registers and the register of individuals with significant control. Registries do not collect the minute book, but banks, buyers and the CRA may ask to see it.

**Is a T2 needed with no income?**

Yes. Every resident corporation files a T2 for every tax year, whether or not it had income or owes tax [2].

A nil return is shorter, but it still includes the identification and GIFI schedules. The first return also includes Schedule 24.

Filing a nil return on time keeps the corporation's account in good standing and avoids CRA requests for the return.

**When is the first T2 due?**

The first T2 is due six months after the first tax year-end [1]. If the year ends on the last day of a month, the due date is the last day of the sixth month after it.

The incorporation date does not set the deadline. The year-end does.

**When is first-year tax due?**

Tax is due on the balance-due day: two months after year-end, or three months for an eligible CCPC [2].

The balance-due day comes before the T2 deadline. Interest applies to any amount paid after it.

If you are unsure whether the three-month rule applies, please plan to pay by the two-month date.

**Instalments in year one?**

Generally no. Instalments are not required when tax for the current or previous year is $3,000 or less [1][3].

A new corporation has no previous year. In year two, instalments usually apply if first-year tax was over $3,000.

**Longest first year?**

A tax year cannot be longer than 53 weeks [1]. The first year starts on the incorporation date.

Choose the year-end with the T2 schedule, your busy season and your salary and dividend plans in mind.

**GST/HST registration point?**

Registration becomes mandatory once taxable supplies pass $30,000 in one calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters [5].

You must register within 29 days after you stop being a small supplier. Voluntary registration is allowed earlier.

**Payroll remittance due?**

Regular remitters send source deductions by the 15th of the month after the pay date [6].

Open the payroll account before the first remittance is due, and keep the remittance dates in your payroll software.

**T4/T5 due?**

T4 and T5 slips, with their summaries, are due by the last day of February after the calendar year in which the salary or dividends were paid [7][8].

This applies whatever the corporation's fiscal year-end is.

**Ontario annual return?**

An Ontario corporation files within six months after each anniversary of incorporation, through the Ontario Business Registry [9].

The annual return is no longer filed with the T2. It is a separate registry filing.

**CBCA annual return?**

A federal corporation files with Corporations Canada within 60 days after each anniversary date [12].

CBCA corporations also file information on individuals with significant control every year and within 15 days of a change.

## Glossary

- **Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC):** A private corporation resident in Canada that is not controlled by non-residents or public corporations, and can claim the small business deduction.
- **Small business deduction (SBD):** A reduction in corporate tax on active business income up to the business limit, giving the 12.2% combined Ontario rate.
- **Business limit:** The $500,000 of active business income eligible for the small business deduction, shared among associated corporations.
- **Balance-due day:** The date corporate tax for a year must be paid: two or three months after year-end.
- **Instalments:** Advance payments of corporate tax, monthly or quarterly, required when tax is over $3,000.
- **T2:** The corporation income tax return, due six months after each tax year-end.
- **Schedule 24:** The T2 schedule a corporation files with its first return after incorporation.
- **Business number (BN):** The nine-digit CRA identifier to which every program account attaches.
- **Program account:** A CRA account under the BN, such as RC (income tax), RT (GST/HST) or RP (payroll).
- **Small supplier:** A person whose taxable supplies are $30,000 or less, and who need not register for GST/HST.
- **T4:** The slip reporting salary and source deductions, due by the last day of February.
- **T5:** The slip reporting dividends and certain investment income, due by the last day of February.
- **Personal services business:** A corporation earning income from services that would otherwise be employment, which cannot claim the small business deduction.
