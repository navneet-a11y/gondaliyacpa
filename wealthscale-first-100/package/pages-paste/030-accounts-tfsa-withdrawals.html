TFSA Withdrawal Rules

TFSA withdrawal rules are simple at their core: you can take money out of a tax-free savings account at any time, for any reason, and pay no tax on it, and the amount you withdraw is added back to your contribution room on January 1 of the next year. The catch is timing. If you put the money back in the same calendar year without enough unused room, the redeposit is an over-contribution taxed at 1% a month. This guide covers the tax, the January 1 rule, safe re-contributions, transfers, withdrawals while living abroad, the effect on benefits and how to time a withdrawal, with worked examples.

At a glance

Tax on a TFSA withdrawal. None. Withdrawals are not income, whether the money is your original deposit or the growth on it, and nothing is withheld at source.

When the room comes back. The amount you withdraw is added to your contribution room on January 1 of the following year, together with the new annual limit. The 2026 limit is $7,000; the 2027 limit has not been published yet.

Putting money back early. A redeposit in the same year uses your current room. If you do not have enough, the excess is taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess in each month.

Switching institutions. A direct transfer between TFSAs does not affect your room. Withdrawing and redepositing yourself counts as a new contribution.

Living abroad. Withdrawals made while you are a non-resident are not taxed in Canada, but the amount comes back as room only once you are a resident again.

Benefits. Because withdrawals are not income, they do not raise the net income used for the OAS recovery tax and other income-tested benefits.

1. TFSA Withdrawal Rules: How a Withdrawal Works

Any amount, any time. You can withdraw part or all of your TFSA whenever you choose. The tax rules put no conditions on what the money is for: a car, a trip, a home, a business or an emergency are all treated the same way.

From a savings TFSA. If your TFSA holds cash in a savings account, a withdrawal is simply a transfer from the TFSA to your chequing account at the same or another institution.

From an investment TFSA. If your TFSA holds stocks, ETFs or funds, they normally have to be sold first. The cash is paid out after the sale settles and the institution processes the request. Selling to raise cash locks in whatever the investments are worth that day, so a forced sale during a market drop turns a temporary loss into a real one.

From a GIC. A non-redeemable GIC usually cannot be cashed before it matures. Please check the terms of any GIC in your TFSA before you count on that money for a fixed date.

Withdrawing investments in kind. Some institutions can move investments out of a TFSA into a taxable account without selling them. The value moved counts as a withdrawal, and it is added back to your room the next January 1 like a cash withdrawal.

How long a withdrawal takes. It depends on the institution, what you hold and how the money is sent. Cash in a savings TFSA moves fastest. Investments add time for the sale to settle. If you need the money by a fixed date, such as the closing date on a home, start early and confirm the timeline with your institution. The tax rules charge nothing for a withdrawal, but your institution may have its own fees, so check its fee schedule.

2. TFSA Withdrawal Tax: None, With a Few Exceptions

Not income. A TFSA withdrawal is not taxable income. You do not report it as income on your tax return, and it does not matter whether the money is your original contribution, interest, dividends or capital gains. A $20,000 withdrawal from an account that started with $8,000 is still $20,000 of tax-free cash.

Nothing withheld. Because no tax is owed, nothing is withheld at source. You receive the full amount, less any fee your institution charges.

Exception 1: non-qualified or prohibited investments. Income from a non-qualified investment is taxed in the hands of the trustee, and income from a prohibited investment is taxed at 100% as an advantage. Withdrawing that income does not undo the tax. Our complete TFSA guide lists what a TFSA may hold.

Exception 2: business income. If CRA decides you traded with the frequency and experience of a professional, it may deregister the account and tax the income as business income. The withdrawals are then no longer protected.

Exception 3: earnings after the holder's death. A designated beneficiary receives the account's value at the date of death tax-free, but earnings after that date are taxable.

US withholding happens earlier. The 15% US tax on US dividends in a TFSA is taken when the dividend is paid, not when you withdraw. Our guide to US stocks in a TFSA explains why it cannot be recovered, and the best TFSA accounts for US stocks list compares the currency costs.

3. How Long Until Your TFSA Room Comes Back

The rule. Every dollar you withdraw is added back to your contribution room on January 1 of the following calendar year. It arrives at the same moment as the new annual limit. Withdrawals do not change the current year's room at all.

The wait depends on the date. Because the reset is on a fixed date, the same withdrawal can wait one day or almost a year for its room to return. The table counts the days for withdrawals made in 2026.

A withdrawal one week later. If the December 31, 2026 withdrawal slips to January 4, 2027, its room returns on January 1, 2028, a wait of 362 days instead of 1. If the money must come out around the new year, aim for the end of December, and leave time for the sale and transfer to finish before December 31.

The amount that returns. The full amount withdrawn comes back, including growth. If you put in $10,000 and withdraw $16,000, your room rises by $16,000. If your investments fell and you withdraw $7,000 from a $10,000 contribution, only $7,000 returns, so a loss inside a TFSA also costs you room.

Your January 1, 2027 room. It will equal your unused room at the end of 2026, plus everything you withdrew in 2026, plus the 2027 limit. The 2027 limit has not been published yet. Our TFSA contribution limit page will show it once CRA releases it.

4. How to Re-Contribute to a TFSA Without a Penalty

Two safe moments. You can put withdrawn money back without any penalty in two cases: at any time after the January 1 that follows the withdrawal, or in the same year up to the amount of unused room you still have.

Check unused room first. Your unused room right now equals your room on January 1 of this year minus everything you have contributed so far this year. Withdrawals made this year are not part of that figure. If the redeposit is no larger than this number, it is safe.

Two people, one withdrawal. This example is hypothetical. Alex and Sam each withdraw $10,000 in April 2026 and redeposit it in June 2026. Alex had $25,000 of unused room, so the redeposit fits and leaves $15,000 ($25,000 - $10,000). Sam had only $3,000 of unused room, so $7,000 of the redeposit is an excess ($10,000 - $3,000). Left from June to December, it costs Sam $490 ($7,000 x 1% x 7 months).

The split fix. Sam could have redeposited $3,000 in June, using exactly the unused room, and the other $7,000 on or after January 1, 2027, when the April withdrawal comes back as room. No tax would apply.

After January 1. Once the new year starts, the full withdrawal plus the new annual limit is available. A redeposit made on January 2 is as safe as one made in March.

Use the calculator. The room calculator above takes this year's contributions and withdrawals separately and shows both your room now and on January 1. Run it before any redeposit you are unsure about.

5. The Re-Contribution Trap: What the Penalty Costs

How the tax works. When a redeposit takes you over your room, the excess is taxed at 1% for each month it stays in the account. The tax is based on the highest excess amount in each month, and a month counts in full even if the excess existed for only a few days of it. There is no grace amount: unlike an RRSP, a TFSA has no $2,000 cushion.

A costly example. This example is hypothetical. Jordan has no unused room, withdraws $20,000 in March 2026 to cover a tax bill, and puts it back in April when a payment comes in. The full $20,000 is an excess from April. Left until January 1, 2027, the tax is $1,800 ($20,000 x 1% x 9 months, April to December).

Catching it early. If Jordan notices in May and withdraws the $20,000 excess that month, the excess existed in April and May. The tax falls to $400 ($20,000 x 1% x 2 months). Each month of delay adds $200.

Reporting. Withdraw the excess as soon as you find it. Then file the TFSA Return and pay the tax by June 30 of the following year. For an excess in 2026, that is June 30, 2027.

The size of the risk. The penalty is small for a small slip and painful for a large one left for months. A $1,000 excess for 2 months costs $20 ($1,000 x 1% x 2). A $30,000 excess for 10 months costs $3,000 ($30,000 x 1% x 10). Use this calculator to see the tax on your own excess and the saving from removing it a month sooner.

6. Moving a TFSA Between Institutions

A direct transfer is not a withdrawal. To move a TFSA, open a TFSA at the new institution and ask it to transfer the old account directly. A direct transfer between your own TFSAs has no effect on your contribution room. The money never leaves the TFSA system, so nothing counts as withdrawn or contributed.

The do-it-yourself mistake. If you withdraw the money and deposit it at the new institution yourself, CRA treats it as a withdrawal and a new contribution. The withdrawal returns as room only next January, so the new deposit can create an excess. This example is hypothetical: with no unused room, moving a $40,000 TFSA this way in May 2026 creates a $40,000 excess. Left until January 1, 2027, the tax is $3,200 ($40,000 x 1% x 8 months, May to December).

Transfer fees. The institution you leave often charges a fee. Among the 13 brokers we track, eight charge $150 to transfer a full account out, Wealthsimple lists $0, and moomoo charges $75 per currency plus tax. Some receiving firms rebate transfer fees, so ask before you start.

Cash or investments. A transfer can move cash, or at many institutions the investments themselves, so you do not have to sell. The process takes longer than a simple withdrawal, because two institutions are involved. Our step-by-step guide on how to transfer a TFSA covers the forms and the timeline to expect. How we gather and check fee figures is set out in our editorial standards.

7. Withdrawals While You Live Outside Canada

Your TFSA can stay open. Becoming a non-resident does not close your TFSA. The account can stay invested while you live abroad.

No Canadian tax on withdrawals. Withdrawals you make while you are a non-resident are not taxed in Canada. The country where you now live may tax TFSA income or withdrawals under its own rules, because it may not recognize the TFSA. Please get advice in that country before you move or withdraw.

Room comes back only when you do. Amounts withdrawn while you are a non-resident are added back to your room only once you become a resident of Canada again. In the meantime, you cannot put money back without a penalty.

No new deposits. A contribution made while you are a non-resident is taxed at 1% a month until you withdraw it or become a resident again. If it also exceeds your room, a second 1% monthly tax applies, for 2% a month in total.

No new room while away. You earn no TFSA room for any full calendar year of non-residence. The year you leave and the year you return each count in full.

Before you leave. Decide what you need before you go. Any room you want to use is best filled while you are still a resident, and any money you will need abroad can be withdrawn either before or after you leave without Canadian tax.

8. TFSA Withdrawals and Government Benefits

No effect on income-tested benefits. Because TFSA withdrawals are not income, they do not raise your net income. Benefits and credits that shrink as income rises, such as Old Age Security through the recovery tax, the Guaranteed Income Supplement, the Canada child benefit and the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit, are not reduced by a TFSA withdrawal.

The OAS recovery tax in numbers. For the 2026 income year, the OAS recovery tax takes 15% of net world income above $95,323. Full recovery is reached at $155,320 for people aged 65 to 74 and $161,320 for people 75 and older.

TFSA vs RRIF for a retiree. This example is hypothetical. A retiree aged 70 has $100,000 of net income in 2026 and needs another $10,000. From a RRIF, the $10,000 is income: net income becomes $110,000 and the OAS recovery tax rises from $701.55 (15% x ($100,000 - $95,323)) to $2,201.55 (15% x ($110,000 - $95,323)), an extra $1,500. From a TFSA, net income stays at $100,000 and the recovery tax stays at $701.55. The $1,500 is in addition to the income tax on the RRIF withdrawal itself.

9. Timing a TFSA Withdrawal

December beats January for planned needs. If you will need money early next year, withdrawing in late December means the room returns on January 1, days later. Waiting until January means waiting almost a year. The amount and the tax are the same; only the room timing changes.

Allow for processing near year-end. A withdrawal counts in the year the money actually leaves the TFSA. Sales need time to settle and transfers need time to process. Ask early in the month so the withdrawal is complete before December 31.

Do not sell investments at a bad moment if you can avoid it. If you hold both cash and investments in your TFSA, take short-term needs from the cash first. Keeping a cash or GIC portion for spending you can see coming means a withdrawal never forces a sale after a market drop.

Large one-off needs. For a planned expense such as a home down payment or a car, you can split the withdrawal: part in December and part in January. The December part returns as room within weeks, and the January part waits a year, so your room recovers sooner overall.

No need to wait for a "safe" month. There is no tax reason to delay a withdrawal you need. The only timing question is when the room comes back, and that matters only if you plan to put the money back soon.

10. Death, Separation and Exempt Contributions

A successor holder does not need to withdraw. When the holder dies and their spouse or common-law partner is named successor holder, the survivor becomes the new holder right away. The account keeps its tax-free status, no withdrawal is needed, and the survivor's own room is not affected as long as the deceased's TFSA had no excess.

Payments to a designated beneficiary. Anyone else named receives a payment of the account's value. Up to the value at the date of death it is tax-free; earnings after that date are taxable. The account's exempt period ends on December 31 of the year after the death.

The survivor's exempt contribution. A surviving spouse or partner who receives a payment from the deceased's TFSA can contribute it to their own TFSA without using any room. The deposit must be made by December 31 of the year after the death, and Form RC240 must be filed within 30 days of the contribution. This is the one case where money can go into a TFSA without room.

Separation or divorce. When partners live separate and apart, a direct transfer from one partner's TFSA to the other's under a court order or written separation agreement does not change either person's room. A withdrawal by one partner followed by a deposit by the other does not get this treatment: it uses the receiving partner's room.

Quebec. CRA notes that Quebec does not recognize successor holder designations for TFSAs, so Quebec residents should plan how their TFSA passes at death with that in mind.

11. TFSA Withdrawals vs RRSP and FHSA Withdrawals

The RRSP. An RRSP withdrawal is fully taxable income. The institution withholds tax at source: outside Quebec, 10% on $5,000 or less, 20% on amounts over $5,000 up to $15,000, and 30% on amounts over $15,000. In Quebec the federal part is 5%, 10% or 15%, plus 14% Quebec tax. The withholding is only a prepayment; the final tax depends on your total income. Two programs allow tax-free RRSP withdrawals that must be repaid: the Home Buyers' Plan, up to $60,000, and the Lifelong Learning Plan, up to $10,000 a year and $20,000 in total.

The FHSA. A qualifying withdrawal to buy a first home is tax-free, and the contributions were deductible, so FHSA money used for a first home gets a deduction going in and no tax coming out. A withdrawal that does not qualify is taxable. If you never buy, the balance can move to an RRSP or RRIF without tax.

12. Using TFSA Withdrawals in Your Plans

Emergency money. Because withdrawals are tax-free and the room returns, a TFSA works well as an emergency reserve. Holding that part in a savings account or short GIC inside the TFSA keeps it stable. Compare high-interest savings rates and GIC rates for the cash portion.

A first home. First-time buyers can combine an FHSA qualifying withdrawal, a Home Buyers' Plan withdrawal of up to $60,000 from an RRSP, and TFSA savings for the same purchase. The TFSA portion has no repayment schedule and no conditions.

Retirement income. Once a RRIF starts, its minimum withdrawal is set by a factor based on age, such as 5.28% at 71. Topping up spending from a TFSA, rather than taking more than the RRIF minimum, keeps taxable income lower. See our RRIF minimum withdrawal table.

A child's education. An RESP comes first for education because of the 20% federal grant. A parent's TFSA can cover costs the RESP does not, without tax and without conditions on how the money is spent.

Long-term growth. Every withdrawal stops that money compounding tax-free until you put it back. Our guide to investment accounts in Canada shows how the TFSA fits with every other account. The compound interest calculator shows what a balance left untouched could grow to, which helps you weigh a withdrawal against leaving the money in place.

What You Need and What You Get

What you need before you withdraw

  • Your TFSA account number and online access or a branch appointment
  • The bank account that will receive the money
  • The amount, and which holdings to sell if the TFSA is invested
  • The maturity date of any GIC you plan to use
  • Your unused room, if you may put the money back this year
  • Your institution's fee schedule for withdrawals or closures
  • For a move to another institution, a new TFSA and a direct transfer request
  • If you live abroad, advice on how your new country taxes the payment

What you get from a TFSA withdrawal

  • The full amount with no Canadian tax and nothing withheld
  • No effect on OAS, GIS or other income-tested benefits
  • The withdrawn amount restored as room next January 1
  • Growth included in the room that returns
  • Freedom to use the money for any purpose
  • No repayment schedule, unlike the Home Buyers' Plan
  • No Canadian tax even if you withdraw while living abroad
  • The option to redeposit early, up to your unused room

How to Withdraw From a TFSA, Step by Step

  1. Decide the amount and the date you need it. Work back from the date to allow for any sale and the transfer to your bank.
  2. Choose what to sell. Use cash first, then investments you would sell anyway. Check that any GIC has matured.
  3. Ask for the withdrawal. Use your institution's app, website or branch. If you are moving the TFSA, ask the new institution for a direct transfer instead.
  4. Record it. Note the date and amount. It returns as room on January 1 of next year.
  5. Check room before any redeposit. This year, you can put back only up to your unused room. After January 1, the full amount is available again.
  6. Fix any excess at once. If a redeposit went over, withdraw the excess right away and file the TFSA Return by June 30 of next year.

10 TFSA Withdrawal Strategies

1. Leave a buffer of unused room

If you often dip into your TFSA and put money back, keep part of your room unused. A buffer means a same-year redeposit up to that amount never triggers the 1% tax.

2. Park the money outside until January

If you withdraw and later have the cash to put back, hold it in a savings account outside the TFSA until January 1. The interest is taxable for those few months, but that cost is usually far below 1% a month on an excess.

3. Split a large redeposit around January 1

Put back up to your unused room this year and the rest on or after January 1. You get most of the money working again quickly without any penalty.

4. Withdraw late in December for early-year needs

A withdrawal completed by December 31 gets its room back on January 1. Ask early in December so the sale and transfer finish in time.

5. Keep a cash sleeve for planned spending

A savings or short GIC portion inside the TFSA covers known expenses, so a withdrawal never forces you to sell investments at a low.

6. Draw on the TFSA before the RRSP for surprises

An unplanned RRSP withdrawal is taxable income, with 10% or more withheld at source, while a TFSA withdrawal costs nothing in tax and its room comes back.

7. Blend TFSA and RRIF income in retirement

Take the RRIF minimum and top up from the TFSA in years when more RRIF income would push net income above the $95,323 OAS recovery threshold for 2026.

8. Transfer, never withdraw, to switch providers

Ask the new institution for a direct transfer. It protects your room, and many receiving firms will rebate the fee the old one charges.

9. Settle your plans before leaving Canada

Fill any room you want to use while you are still a resident, because deposits made after you leave are taxed at 1% a month.

10. Log every withdrawal

Write down the date and amount each time. On January 1, add the year's withdrawals to your unused room and the new limit, and you will always know your figure.

Illustrative Example: A Withdrawal, a Bonus and the January Reset

This example is hypothetical.

Priya has used all her TFSA room by the start of 2026. In June she needs $15,000 for a home repair, and she expects a $15,000 work bonus in September.

  1. June 2026 withdrawal. She withdraws $15,000. Tax: $0. Her room for the rest of 2026 stays at $0, because the withdrawal returns only on January 1, 2027.
  2. The tempting move. If she deposits the $15,000 bonus into her TFSA in September, it is a $15,000 excess. Left until January 1, 2027, the tax is $600 ($15,000 x 1% x 4 months, September to December).
  3. The better move. She holds the bonus in a savings account outside her TFSA from September to December. The interest is taxable, but there is no 1% monthly tax.
  4. January 1, 2027. Her room becomes $15,000 (the June withdrawal) plus the 2027 limit, once CRA publishes it.
  5. January 2027 deposit. She deposits the $15,000 with no excess. Waiting four months saved her $600.

TFSA Withdrawal Rules FAQ

Are TFSA withdrawals taxed?

No. TFSA withdrawals are not taxable income, and nothing is withheld at source. It does not matter whether you take out your original contributions or the interest, dividends and gains earned on them. The full amount withdrawn is added back to your contribution room on January 1 of the following year.

When can I put money back after a TFSA withdrawal?

You can put it back without penalty on or after January 1 of the year after the withdrawal, when the amount is added back to your room. In the same year, you can redeposit only up to the unused room you still have. Anything above that is an excess taxed at 1% a month.

What happens if I re-contribute to my TFSA in the same year?

If you have enough unused room, nothing happens: the redeposit simply uses that room. If you do not, the amount above your room is an excess, taxed at 1% per month on the highest excess in each month until you withdraw it or new room arrives on January 1. You then file the TFSA Return by June 30.

How long does a TFSA withdrawal take?

It depends on your institution and what you hold. Cash in a savings TFSA moves fastest. Investments must be sold first and the sale must settle, and a non-redeemable GIC may not be cashable until it matures. If you need the money by a fixed date, start early and confirm the timeline with your institution.

Is there a limit on how much I can withdraw from my TFSA?

No. You can withdraw any amount up to the full value of your TFSA, and as often as you like. The tax rules put no conditions on how you use the money. Your institution may charge its own fees for some withdrawals, and a locked-in GIC may not be available until it matures.

Do I need to report a TFSA withdrawal on my tax return?

No. A TFSA withdrawal is not income, so you do not report it as income on your return. You file a TFSA Return when you owe one of the TFSA taxes, such as the 1% monthly tax on an excess. That return and payment are due by June 30 of the following year.

Do TFSA withdrawals affect OAS or GIS?

No. Because TFSA withdrawals are not income, they do not raise the net income used for the OAS recovery tax or the Guaranteed Income Supplement. For the 2026 income year, the OAS recovery tax takes 15% of net world income above $95,323, so drawing from a TFSA rather than a RRIF can lower it.

Is moving my TFSA to another institution a withdrawal?

Not if it is done as a direct transfer. Open a TFSA at the new institution and ask it to transfer the old account. A direct transfer between your own TFSAs does not affect your room. If you withdraw and redeposit the money yourself, it counts as a withdrawal and a new contribution.

Can I withdraw from my TFSA if I move abroad?

Yes. Withdrawals made while you are a non-resident are not taxed in Canada, though your new country may tax them. The amount is added back to your room only once you are a resident of Canada again. Do not make new deposits while abroad, because they are taxed at 1% a month.

Does withdrawing investment growth give me more TFSA room?

Yes. The full amount you withdraw comes back as room on January 1 of the next year, including growth. If you contributed $10,000, it grew to $16,000 and you withdrew all of it, your room rises by $16,000. If your investments lost value, only the smaller amount you withdraw comes back.

Can I use a TFSA withdrawal to buy a home?

Yes. A TFSA withdrawal can be used for anything, including a down payment, with no tax and no repayment. First-time buyers can combine it with an FHSA qualifying withdrawal and a Home Buyers' Plan withdrawal of up to $60,000 from an RRSP for the same home.

What is the best time of year to make a TFSA withdrawal?

For a planned need, late December is usually best, because the room returns on January 1, days later. A withdrawal made in early January waits almost a year for its room. If you do not plan to redeposit soon, the date makes no difference to the tax, which is zero either way.
