Norbert's Gambit: Step-by-Step Guide

Norbert's Gambit is a way to turn Canadian dollars into US dollars, or back, by buying an interlisted ETF in one currency and selling it in the other, instead of paying your broker's currency conversion fee. That fee is 1.5% at Questrade and Wealthsimple, or $150 on $10,000, and 1.26% at TD on amounts under $25,000, as of October 22, 2026. The gambit swaps it for two trades and a journal request. This guide shows the break-even amount at each major Canadian broker, every step with DLR and DLR.U, the timing, the tax rules and the mistakes that erase the saving.

At a glance

What it is. Norbert's Gambit converts currency through a security that trades in both Canadian and US dollars. You buy it with Canadian dollars, ask your broker to journal it to the US-dollar side of the same account, then sell it for US dollars. The usual tool is the US Dollar Currency ETF, which trades as DLR in Canadian dollars and DLR.U in US dollars. We name the pair only to explain the method; it is not a recommendation.

What it replaces. As of October 22, 2026, converting $10,000 costs $150.00 at Questrade, Wealthsimple (US trades from a Canadian-dollar account) and Webull at 1.5%, $126.00 at TD at 1.26%, $160.00 at CIBC, RBC and BMO at about 1.6%, and $170.00 at National Bank Direct Brokerage at about 1.70%. Break-even. At a broker with $0 commissions, the two trades cost $0 in commissions, so the gambit costs less in published fees at any amount. With $9.99 trades at TD, it breaks even at $1,585.71. At RBC ($9.95 trades) the figure is $1,243.75, and at CIBC ($6.95 trades) it is $868.75.

What still costs money. Each trade crosses the bid-ask spread, the two lines can trade at slightly different implied exchange rates, and some brokers charge to journal. Wealthsimple's fee schedule lists journaling at $9.95.

Timing. The journal is a request you make to your broker. How long it takes varies by broker, and most brokers do not publish a processing time, so ask before you buy.

Tax. Inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA there is no gain or loss to report. In a non-registered account the sale of DLR.U is a disposition, and the superficial loss rule can deny a loss if you or your spouse hold identical units 30 days after the sale.

1. What Norbert's Gambit Is and How It Works

The short definition. Norbert's Gambit is a currency conversion done with a security instead of a currency trade. Some securities trade on a Canadian exchange under two symbols, one priced in Canadian dollars and one priced in US dollars. Both symbols represent the same units. If you buy the Canadian-dollar line and then sell the US-dollar line, you finish with US dollars, and your broker never runs a currency conversion on your behalf.

The three moves. First, you buy units of the Canadian-dollar line (DLR) with Canadian cash. Second, you ask your broker to journal those units to the US-dollar line (DLR.U), which moves them to the US-dollar side of the same account. Third, you sell DLR.U and receive US dollars. Each move is covered in detail in the step-by-step section below.

What you pay instead of the conversion fee. You pay a commission on each of the two trades, if your broker charges one, plus the bid-ask spread on each trade, plus any journal fee. At brokers such as Questrade and Wealthsimple, which charge $0 commission on Canadian-listed ETFs, the published cost of the trades is $0. The spreads and any journal fee are what remain.

It works in both directions. To turn US dollars back into Canadian dollars, you reverse the order: buy DLR.U with US cash, journal the units to DLR, then sell DLR for Canadian dollars. The same break-even logic applies, because your broker charges its conversion fee in both directions.

Who it suits. The gambit is built for people who convert a lump sum and then hold US dollars for a while, for example to buy US-listed stocks or ETFs in an RRSP. That account matters: under the Canada-US tax treaty, US dividends paid to an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF holding US-listed securities directly are exempt from US withholding, while a TFSA, RESP or FHSA pays the 15% treaty rate. Our guide to US stocks in a TFSA explains that difference.

What it is not. The gambit is not a trading strategy and not a bet on the dollar. It only changes how you pay to convert. If you convert small amounts often, a broker with a lower conversion fee or a US-dollar account may do more for you than the gambit, which is why the next two sections start with what each broker charges.

3. When Norbert's Gambit Saves Money: Break-Even by Broker

The break-even formula. The gambit costs less in published fees once the conversion fee on your amount is larger than what you pay for the two trades and any journal fee. Written as a formula: break-even amount = (commission to buy + commission to sell + journal fee) ÷ conversion fee rate. At TD, that is ($9.99 + $9.99) ÷ 1.26% = $1,585.71. Above that amount, the gambit's published fees are lower than TD's conversion spread.

4. DLR and DLR.U: How the Interlisted Pair Works

One ETF, two symbols. The US Dollar Currency ETF trades on a Canadian exchange under two symbols. DLR is priced in Canadian dollars and DLR.U is priced in US dollars. They are the same units of the same fund, so your broker can move units from one line to the other by journal without selling them. That is the whole trick behind Norbert's Gambit. We name DLR and DLR.U only to explain how the method works. This is not a recommendation to buy either line, and you should decide for yourself whether any security fits you.

Why a currency ETF is used. As its name says, the fund is built to track the US dollar. While your units wait for the journal, their US-dollar value should move far less than a stock's would. That limits the risk of holding a security for the days between your purchase and your sale. A company whose shares are listed in both Canada and the US can also be journaled at some brokers, but a stock's price can move a lot while you wait, which adds risk the currency ETF largely avoids.

The exchange rate you actually get. Because the two lines represent the same units, the ratio of their prices is an exchange rate. Divide the DLR price by the DLR.U price to get the Canadian dollars you pay per US dollar. Use the ask price of DLR (what you pay) and the bid price of DLR.U (what you receive) to see your real rate before you trade, then compare it with the rate your broker quotes for a direct conversion.

The fund's own fee. Like any ETF, the fund charges an annual management expense ratio. You bear it only for the short time you hold the units, so on a gambit it is a very small cost next to the conversion fee you avoid. Your trade confirmations show the prices you paid and received, which is all you need for the comparison.

Liquidity and order size. Large orders can move through several price levels, which widens the effective spread. Splitting a very large conversion into a few limit orders, or trading when both lines are active, can keep the spread smaller. Market orders hand control of the price to whoever is on the other side, so the steps below use limit orders throughout.

6. Norbert's Gambit Step by Step

Step 1: Check both quotes. Look up the bid and ask prices for DLR and DLR.U during regular market hours. Divide the DLR ask by the DLR.U bid to get the Canadian dollars you will pay for each US dollar. Compare that with your broker's quoted conversion rate. If the gap is smaller than you expected, run the numbers in the calculator above before you go further.

Step 2: Buy DLR with a limit order. Work out how many whole units your Canadian cash buys at the ask price and round down. Place a limit order at or near the ask. A limit order sets the most you will pay; a market order does not, and it can fill at a worse price if the quote moves. Keep the trade confirmation, which shows the units bought, the price and the settlement date.

Step 3: Request the journal. Ask your broker to journal the units from DLR to DLR.U in the same account. Give the account number, the symbol, the number of units and the direction: Canadian-dollar line to US-dollar line. Ask whether the purchase must settle first. Section 7 covers what happens during this wait.

Step 4: Wait until the units show as DLR.U. Do not place the sale until DLR.U appears on the US-dollar side of your account. Selling a position you do not yet hold on that side can lead to a rejected order or an unwanted short position.

Step 5: Sell DLR.U with a limit order. Place a limit order at or near the bid. When it fills, the proceeds settle as US-dollar cash in your account. You can then buy US-listed securities with that cash, or leave it in US dollars.

Step 6: Check the result. Compare the US dollars you received with what a direct conversion would have produced on the day you bought DLR. Keep both trade confirmations and the journal confirmation together. In a non-registered account you will need them for tax, as section 11 explains.

7. The Journal Step, Timing and Settlement

What a journal is. A journal is an internal transfer inside your broker. Nothing is bought or sold: the broker relabels your units from the Canadian-dollar line to the US-dollar line and moves them to the US-dollar side of your account. Because it is a back-office task, it usually needs a request from you. Do not assume it happens on its own; ask your broker how it handles interlisted units before you buy.

How long it takes. Processing time varies by broker, and most brokers do not publish it in their pricing, so we do not quote a number of days here. Plan for a wait between your purchase and your sale, and ask your broker for its usual turnaround when you send the request. If the units have not moved after the time your broker gave you, follow up with the request date and your trade confirmation in hand.

Settlement may come first. Every trade has a settlement date, printed on your confirmation, when the cash and units change hands for good. Ask whether your broker needs the units to settle before it journals them, because the answer decides how early you can send the request.

What happens to your money while you wait. Once you own DLR, you hold units of a fund built to track the US dollar. The Canadian-dollar price of DLR will rise or fall with the exchange rate, but the US dollars you will get when you sell DLR.U should change far less. In effect, you fixed most of your exchange rate when you bought. If you change your mind, you can sell DLR for Canadian dollars like any other holding, paying the spread again.

Journal fees. Wealthsimple's fee schedule lists journaling at $9.95. The other brokers in our tables did not list a journal fee in the pricing we checked, which does not prove there is none. Please ask before you start, and add any fee to the break-even formula in section 3. Also place both trades online where you can: Wealthsimple, for example, charges $45 for a trade placed by phone, waived for Active Traders.

Converting back later. The reverse gambit has the same wait. Buy DLR.U with US cash, ask for a journal to DLR, and sell DLR once the units show on the Canadian-dollar side. The timing questions above apply in the same way.

11. Tax Rules: Superficial Loss, ACB and Registered Accounts

Inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA. Trades inside a registered account are not reported as capital gains or losses, so a gambit there has no tax filing step. One caution for the TFSA: the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) can tax a TFSA's income as business income when the holder trades with the frequency and experience of a professional trader. It gives no trade-count test. An occasional currency conversion is not that kind of activity, but a long series of rapid trades in a TFSA can draw attention.

In a non-registered account, it is a purchase and a sale. Buying DLR is a purchase. Selling DLR.U is a disposition of the same units. Your adjusted cost base (ACB) is what you paid in Canadian dollars for the units. Your proceeds are the US dollars you received, expressed in Canadian dollars for your return, because your return is filed in Canadian dollars. The difference is a capital gain or loss. Because the two lines are the same units and the holding period is short, the amount is usually small, but it still has to be reported.

How gains are taxed. Only part of a capital gain is included in income. For 2001 to 2025 the inclusion rate was one-half. A net capital loss can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, and it can only offset taxable capital gains. Our capital gains tax calculator shows the tax on a gain at your income.

The superficial loss rule. If you sell DLR.U at a loss, the loss is denied when you, or an affiliated person such as your spouse or common-law partner, acquire identical property in the period that starts 30 calendar days before the sale and ends 30 calendar days after it, and still hold it at the end of that period. The denied loss can usually be added to the ACB of the substituted property, so it is deferred rather than gone.

When it bites on a gambit. A single gambit where you sell every unit you bought does not leave you holding identical property 30 days later. The risk comes from repeats: buying DLR again within 30 days for a second conversion, holding DLR in another of your accounts, or a spouse buying it at the same time. If you run several gambits close together in a non-registered account, space them out or accept that a small loss may be deferred.

Keep records. Save the two trade confirmations, the journal confirmation and the Canadian-dollar value of the US-dollar proceeds on the sale date. Keep these with your tax papers for the year. Our editorial standards explain how we check tax rules like these.

12. Norbert's Gambit Risks and Common Mistakes

Using market orders. A market order fills at whatever price is available, which can be far from the last quote if the market is thin. On a currency gambit the whole saving can sit in a few cents per unit, so use limit orders on both legs.

Selling before the journal finishes. If you place the DLR.U sale before the units appear on the US-dollar side, the order can be rejected or create a short position. Wait until the units show as DLR.U.

No US-dollar side. If the account cannot hold US dollars, the proceeds can be converted straight back to Canadian dollars at the broker's rate. You pay the spread twice and gain nothing. Check the table in section 5 first.

Converting amounts too small to matter. At brokers that charge commissions, amounts below the break-even cost more by gambit than by direct conversion: below $1,585.71 at TD, $1,243.75 at RBC and $868.75 at CIBC. Spreads push the real break-even higher.

Forgetting the extra fees. A journal fee, a USD account fee or a phone-trade fee changes the arithmetic. At Wealthsimple, the $9.95 journal fee and, on Core, the $10 a month USD account after the free trial both count.

Doing it where conversion is already cheap. At Interactive Brokers, and at Wealthsimple on single conversions of $100,000 or more inside a USD account, direct conversion costs less than the gambit.

Converting back and forth. Each round trip pays two conversions or two gambits. If you will spend or invest the US dollars later, keep them in US dollars instead of converting them home in between. New to investing? Start with how to invest in Canada or the full investing for beginners hub.

9 Strategies to Cut Currency Conversion Costs

1. Convert once, then hold US dollars

The cheapest conversion is the one you do not repeat. Questrade holds US dollars in registered accounts at no extra cost, so a single gambit can fund years of US-dollar purchases and dividends. Cash you will not invest yet can sit in one of the USD savings accounts we compare.

2. Batch small amounts

At brokers that charge commissions, the gambit only pays above its break-even: $1,585.71 at TD, $1,243.75 at RBC and $868.75 at CIBC. Saving up and converting one larger amount clears the break-even easily and cuts the number of spreads you pay.

3. Put US dividend payers in the RRSP

Under the Canada-US tax treaty, an RRSP holding US-listed securities directly is exempt from US withholding on dividends, while a TFSA pays 15%. If you convert for US dividend payers, the RRSP is often the account where the converted dollars do the most work. The best TFSA accounts for US stocks page covers the TFSA side.

4. Check Wealthsimple's tier first

Inside a Wealthsimple USD account, a single conversion of $100,000 or more costs 0%, and $25,000 to $99,999.99 costs 0.5%. Compare that with the $9.95 journal fee and your spreads before choosing the gambit.

5. Use limit orders on both legs

A limit order caps the price you pay for DLR and sets the least you accept for DLR.U. On a conversion, the saving can sit in a few cents a unit, so a poor market fill can take a large share of it.

6. Read the implied rate before you trade

Divide the DLR ask by the DLR.U bid. If that rate is not clearly better than your broker's quoted conversion rate after commissions, convert directly and save yourself the wait.

7. Space out gambits in taxable accounts

In a non-registered account, buying DLR again within 30 days of selling DLR.U at a loss, and still holding it 30 days later, triggers the superficial loss rule. Leaving a gap between conversions avoids a denied loss.

8. Keep US dividends in US dollars

At Wealthsimple, US-dollar dividends reinvested in an account without a USD side pay the conversion fee. Holding them in US dollars, or reinvesting them inside a USD account, avoids a small conversion every time a dividend arrives.

9. Compare with a low-cost converter

If you convert often, a broker that converts at a very low published cost may suit you better than repeated gambits. Interactive Brokers charges 0.20 basis points with a US$2 minimum; see Questrade vs Interactive Brokers for the full trade-off.

Illustrative Example: Converting $20,000 in an RRSP

This example is hypothetical. Priya wants US dollars in her RRSP to buy US-listed ETFs. She has $20,000 of Canadian cash and compares two brokers: Questrade, with $0 commissions and a 1.5% conversion fee, and TD Direct Investing, with $9.99 trades and a 1.26% spread under $25,000. To show the effect of trading spreads, we assume the bid-ask spreads on her two trades together cost 0.10% of the amount. That is an assumption for this example only, not a quoted market figure.

  1. Direct conversion at Questrade: ($20,000 x 1.5% = $300.00).
  2. Gambit commissions at Questrade: ($0.00 + $0.00 = $0.00).
  3. Assumed spreads on the two trades: ($20,000 x 0.10% = $20.00).
  4. Saving at Questrade: ($300.00 - $0.00 - $20.00 = $280.00).
  5. Direct conversion at TD: ($20,000 x 1.26% = $252.00).
  6. Gambit commissions at TD: ($9.99 x 2 = $19.98).
  7. Saving at TD: ($252.00 - $19.98 - $20.00 = $212.02).

Because the gambit happens inside her RRSP, Priya has no capital gain or loss to report. Once she holds US-listed securities directly in the RRSP, US dividends on them are exempt from US withholding under the tax treaty. If the same $20,000 sat in a non-registered account, she would report the small gain or loss on the DLR.U sale and keep her confirmations for her ACB records.

Norbert's Gambit FAQ

What is Norbert's Gambit?

Norbert's Gambit is a way to convert Canadian dollars to US dollars, or back, using a security that trades in both currencies. You buy the Canadian-dollar line, usually DLR, ask your broker to journal the units to the US-dollar line, DLR.U, and sell them for US dollars. You pay two trade costs instead of your broker's conversion fee.

Is Norbert's Gambit worth it for small amounts?

It depends on your broker's commissions. At a broker with $0 commissions, the published fees for the two trades are $0, so only the bid-ask spreads and any journal fee count against the saving. At TD, with $9.99 trades and a 1.26% spread, the break-even on published fees is $1,585.71. Below that, a direct conversion costs less.

How much does Norbert's Gambit save at Questrade?

Questrade charges $0 commission on Canadian-listed ETF trades and 1.5% to convert currency, as of October 22, 2026. The gambit's published fees are $0, so the fee saving is $150.00 on $10,000 and $300.00 on $20,000. Subtract the bid-ask spreads on both trades, and any journal fee Questrade quotes, to get your real saving.

Can I do Norbert's Gambit at Wealthsimple?

Yes. Wealthsimple's Active Trading page lists Norbert's Gambit as available. Trades are $0 commission, the fee schedule lists a $9.95 journaling fee, and you need a USD account, which costs $10 plus tax a month on Core after a 30-day trial. Inside a USD account, conversions of $100,000 or more cost 0%, so the gambit only helps below that.

What are DLR and DLR.U?

DLR and DLR.U are two trading symbols for the same US Dollar Currency ETF on a Canadian exchange. DLR is priced in Canadian dollars and DLR.U in US dollars. Because they are the same units, a broker can journal them from one line to the other, which is what makes Norbert's Gambit work. We name them to explain the method, not as a recommendation.

How long does Norbert's Gambit take?

The two trades take moments, but the journal between them depends on your broker. Processing time varies, and most brokers do not publish it, so ask yours before you buy. Some may also want the purchase to settle before they journal. Plan for a wait, and do not sell DLR.U until the units appear on the US-dollar side.

Can I do Norbert's Gambit in a TFSA or RRSP?

Yes, if the account can hold US dollars. Questrade holds US dollars in registered accounts at no extra cost, Qtrade charges US$15 a quarter per RRSP, RRIF or TFSA unless you have Investor Plus, and Wealthsimple uses a USD account. Some FHSAs, including those at National Bank, Qtrade and TD Easy Trade, do not hold US dollars.

Do I pay tax on Norbert's Gambit?

Not inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA. In a non-registered account, selling DLR.U is a disposition: your gain or loss is the Canadian-dollar value of the proceeds minus your Canadian-dollar cost. It is usually small. A loss can be denied under the superficial loss rule if you or your spouse hold identical units 30 days after the sale.

Can Norbert's Gambit convert US dollars back to Canadian dollars?

Yes. Reverse the steps: buy DLR.U with US dollars, ask your broker to journal the units to DLR, then sell DLR for Canadian dollars. Your broker charges its conversion fee in both directions, so the same break-even arithmetic applies. Use limit orders on both trades and wait for the journal to finish before you sell.

Does Norbert's Gambit work at Interactive Brokers?

It can, but it rarely saves money there. Interactive Brokers converts at 0.20 basis points of the trade value with a US$2 minimum, as of October 22, 2026. The gambit's two trades cost at least CAD 1.00 each before spreads, and per-share commissions rise with the number of units, so a direct conversion is usually cheaper at IBKR.

What is the biggest risk with Norbert's Gambit?

The biggest practical risk is execution: a market order filling at a poor price, or a sale placed before the journal is done. The fund tracks the US dollar, so price risk while you wait is smaller than with a stock, but it is not zero. Use limit orders and confirm the US-dollar side exists before you buy.

Should I hold US dollars instead of converting each time?

If you invest in US-listed securities regularly, holding US dollars avoids paying a conversion on every trade and every dividend. Questrade holds US dollars in registered accounts at no extra cost, while Wealthsimple charges $10 plus tax a month for a USD account on Core. Converting once, by gambit or direct, and then staying in US dollars usually costs least.
