International Investing From Canada: World Markets

The international investing Canada-based investors do most often is buying US-listed stocks and funds, and three costs decide how much of the return you keep. The first is the currency conversion: as of October 22, 2026, converting $10,000 costs US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers and $150 at a 1.5% broker. The second is US withholding tax on dividends: 15% with a W-8BEN, recoverable in a taxable account, lost in a TFSA, and 0% in a documented RRSP. The third is account placement. This hub explains each one, covers markets beyond the US and currency risk, and links to our detailed guides, calculators and broker rankings.

1. International Investing From Canada Starts With US Stocks

Why the US comes first. For most Canadian investors, investing in US stocks from Canada is the first step outside the home market. Every broker we cover offers US-listed stocks and ETFs, US shares can be held in a TFSA, RRSP, FHSA or taxable account, and the Canada-US tax treaty sets clear rules for the tax on dividends.

What changes between brokers. Commissions on US trades are $0 online at seven brokers. The steps. You choose the account, pick a broker with US-dollar accounts, open the account and file a W-8BEN, fund it, get US dollars at the lowest cost and place your order. 3. US Withholding Tax by Account

The treaty rate. Article X(2)(b) of the Canada-US tax treaty caps US tax on dividends paid to a Canadian resident at 15%. The broker, as withholding agent, needs a valid Form W-8BEN to apply it. Without the form, most US-source income paid to a foreign person is taxed at 30%.

The retirement exemption. Article XXI(2) exempts dividends and interest earned by arrangements operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs qualify, so US-listed securities held directly in them have no US tax withheld on dividends once the broker has the account documented. TFSAs, FHSAs and RESPs are not retirement arrangements.

In a TFSA, the 15% is a permanent cost, but growth and gains are never taxed in Canada, so US holdings that pay little or no dividend still fit there well. 4. Getting US Dollars for Less

Three routes. You can let your broker convert at its published rate, use Norbert's Gambit, or bring in US dollars you already hold. For small and regular amounts, the broker's rate is the practical choice, so the rate itself matters most. For large one-time amounts, the gap between routes can be hundreds of dollars.

Norbert's Gambit in brief. Some securities trade on a Canadian exchange in both Canadian and US dollars. You buy the Canadian-dollar listing, ask your broker to journal the position to the US-dollar listing, then sell it for US dollars. You pay two commissions, the bid-ask spreads and the risk of a price change while the journal is processed, instead of the conversion rate. Wealthsimple lists the method as available; ask any other broker how it handles the journal before you start. Our Norbert's Gambit step-by-step guide covers the details.

US dollars you already have. US-dollar income, savings or a US-dollar bank account in Canada can fund the account's US-dollar side with no conversion. US dollars waiting to be invested can sit in a US-dollar savings account; compare rates on our USD savings accounts page. Eligible deposits in Canadian or foreign currency at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per category at each member institution, while stocks and ETFs are not CDIC-insured.

The return trip. Every conversion has a way back. If you will need Canadian dollars later, the same rate applies again. A US-dollar side lets you choose when to convert, and a round trip on $10,000 at 1.5% costs $300 ($150 x 2).

Registered room is counted in Canadian dollars. A US-dollar contribution to a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA uses room at its Canadian-dollar value on the day it goes in. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000 and the 2026 RRSP dollar limit is $33,810.

5. Foreign Stocks Beyond the US: What Canadian Investors Can Reach

What the published pricing shows. Every broker we cover publishes commissions for Canadian-listed and US-listed stocks and ETFs. Interactive Brokers, for example, offers fractional trading on TSX and Cboe Canada stocks and ETFs as well as US stocks. Direct access to stock exchanges outside North America, and the commission and currency rates for each of those markets, is not listed by broker in the pricing we checked on October 22, 2026, so this page does not name which brokers offer it. If you want to buy foreign stocks in Canada directly on an overseas exchange, please ask the broker for its list of markets and its rates for each currency before you open an account.

Depositary receipts. Many large companies based outside North America also trade in the United States as American depositary receipts (ADRs). An ADR is a US-listed certificate that represents shares of a foreign company, traded in US dollars like any other US-listed share. Buying one needs US dollars, so the usual conversion cost applies, and some brokers pass on a custody fee: Webull charges an ADR custody fee of US$0.01 to US$0.03 per share. The company's home country may also withhold tax on its dividends, at a rate set by that country's rules and its treaty with Canada, which this page does not cover.

Funds. The most common route to markets outside the US is a fund: a Canadian-listed or US-listed ETF or mutual fund that holds international stocks. One purchase gives exposure to many countries, and a Canadian-listed fund can be bought in Canadian dollars with no conversion at your broker. The structure of the fund decides how many layers of foreign withholding tax apply, as section 7 explains.

The same account rules apply. Listed shares and funds are generally qualified investments for registered accounts, but confirm that a foreign listing qualifies before you buy it in a TFSA or RRSP, since a non-qualified investment is taxed at 50% of its fair market value. Foreign dividends in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP lose whatever the foreign country withholds. In a taxable account, foreign tax can generally be claimed as a foreign tax credit, up to the Canadian tax on that income.

Watch the extra costs. Overseas markets can bring other currencies, other trading hours and other fees. Before you invest outside North America, write down the conversion cost, the commission, any custody fee and the foreign withholding rate, and compare the total with a fund that gives similar exposure.

6. Currency Risk Basics

What currency risk is. When you own a US or other foreign holding, its value in Canadian dollars depends on two things: its price in its own currency and the exchange rate. Even if the price does not move, a change in the exchange rate changes what the holding is worth to you in Canadian dollars.

This example is hypothetical.

You buy US$10,000 of shares when US$1 costs C$1.40, so they cost C$14,000 (US$10,000 x 1.40).

  1. The Canadian dollar strengthens. The share price stays at US$10,000 but US$1 now buys C$1.30. The holding is worth C$13,000 (US$10,000 x 1.30), a C$1,000 loss in Canadian dollars ($14,000 - $13,000).
  2. The Canadian dollar weakens. If US$1 instead buys C$1.50, the holding is worth C$15,000 (US$10,000 x 1.50), a C$1,000 gain ($15,000 - $14,000).
  3. Both move at once. If the shares rise 10% to US$11,000 and US$1 buys C$1.30, the holding is worth C$14,300 (US$11,000 x 1.30). A 10% gain in US dollars becomes a C$300 gain in Canadian dollars ($14,300 - $14,000).

Match currency to spending. If you will spend the money in Canada, currency moves affect your result. If you will spend US dollars, for example on time in the United States, holding US-dollar assets can match the spending and reduce the risk that matters to you.

Hedging. Some funds hedge their currency exposure so that the exchange rate has less effect on returns. Hedging has its own costs and does not remove every effect of currency moves. Read a fund's documents to see whether and how it hedges.

Tax in a taxable account. In a non-registered account, gains and losses are calculated in Canadian dollars, using the exchange rate on the purchase date for the cost and on the sale date for the proceeds. A currency move can therefore create a taxable gain or a deductible loss even when the price in US dollars is unchanged. Inside a TFSA or RRSP, there is no yearly tax effect.

7. Global Investing in Canada Through Funds: Layers of Withholding

Why structure matters. Two funds can hold the same companies and still lose different amounts to foreign tax. The difference is where each layer of withholding is taken and whether your account can recover it. This section describes the layers in general terms; it is not a view on any fund.

Foreign withholding layers on dividends, by fund structure, in general terms
StructureOn US companiesOn companies outside the US
US-listed fund held directly15% US tax at your account (0% in a documented RRSP)Foreign country's tax inside the fund, then 15% US tax on the fund's distributions (0% of the US layer in a documented RRSP)
Canadian-listed fund holding the stocks directly15% US tax inside the fund, in every accountForeign country's tax inside the fund
Canadian-listed fund holding a US-listed fund15% US tax inside the structure, in every accountForeign country's tax, then 15% US tax, both inside the structure

In a TFSA. For US companies, each structure loses one 15% layer, so the conversion cost and the fund's fees decide. For companies outside the US, a US-listed fund or a Canadian fund that holds one can lose two layers, while a Canadian fund holding the stocks directly loses one.

In an RRSP. A US-listed fund held directly avoids the 15% US layer under the treaty's retirement exemption, which is the main reason investors hold US-listed funds there. A Canadian-listed fund does not, because the exemption does not reach through a Canadian fund.

In a taxable account. Foreign tax reported to you, such as US tax on a US-listed holding or the foreign tax a Canadian fund reports on its slips, can generally be claimed as a foreign tax credit. Tax withheld deeper inside a structure may not be reported to you in a way that can be claimed.

The trade-offs. Fewer layers usually means less tax lost, but US-listed funds need US dollars and a conversion, and fund fees differ. Weigh all three before choosing a structure, and read each fund's tax and distribution documents. Our US stocks in a TFSA guide applies these layers to the TFSA in more detail.

8. Foreign Investments in a Taxable Account

Reporting foreign dividends. In a non-registered account, foreign dividends are reported in Canadian dollars at their gross amount, before the foreign tax withheld. They do not qualify for the Canadian dividend tax credit, so they are taxed at your full marginal rate, like interest.

The foreign tax credit. You then claim a credit for the foreign tax withheld. The credit is the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. The federal part goes on Form T2209 (line 40500) and, outside Quebec, the provincial part on Form T2036.

A worked figure. This example is hypothetical. An Ontario resident with $70,000 of taxable income in 2026 has a combined marginal rate of 29.65% (20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario). On a US dividend worth $1,000 in Canadian dollars, Canadian tax is $296.50 ($1,000 x 29.65%). The $150 of US tax is less than that Canadian tax, so the full $150 is credited. Net Canadian tax is $146.50 ($296.50 - $150), and total tax is $296.50.

Capital gains and losses. Gains on foreign shares are calculated in Canadian dollars at the exchange rates on the trade dates, as section 6 shows. A loss is denied under the superficial loss rule if you or an affiliated person, including your spouse or common-law partner, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after. Net capital losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely against taxable capital gains. The capital gains tax calculator estimates the tax on a gain.

Registered accounts. None of this yearly reporting applies inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA. RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are taxed as income when you take them, and the foreign tax lost inside a TFSA, FHSA or RESP cannot be claimed.

How to Choose Your International Investing Setup

  1. Decide what you want to own. US-listed shares, funds holding international stocks, or both. Funds are the simplest route to markets outside North America.
  2. Choose the accounts. US dividend payers keep the most in a documented RRSP; growth-focused US holdings suit a TFSA; a taxable account lets you claim foreign tax as a credit.
  3. Pick a broker. Compare the conversion rate, US dollars in your registered accounts and US commissions, using the ranked table above or our best online brokers list.
  4. Plan your US dollars. Use a US-dollar side to convert once, consider Norbert's Gambit for large sums, and use US dollars you already hold.
  5. File your W-8BEN. It takes US withholding from 30% to 15% outside retirement accounts.
  6. Decide how much currency risk you accept. Match foreign holdings to where you will spend the money, and read how any fund handles currency.
  7. Keep records in Canadian dollars. For a taxable account, note the exchange rate on every purchase, sale and dividend.

International Investing From Canada: FAQ

How can Canadians invest internationally?

Most start with US-listed stocks and ETFs, which every broker we cover offers. For markets outside North America, the common routes are funds that hold international stocks and US-listed depositary receipts of foreign companies. Direct access to overseas exchanges varies by broker and was not listed in the pricing we checked, so please ask a broker before opening an account for that purpose.

What is the cheapest way to invest in US stocks from Canada?

Look at the conversion cost as well as the commission. As of October 22, 2026, seven brokers charge $0 on online US stock trades, and converting $10,000 costs US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers against $150 at a 1.5% broker. A US-dollar account avoids repeat conversions, and Norbert's Gambit can cut the cost on large sums.

Do Canadians pay US withholding tax on US dividends?

Yes, in most accounts. The Canada-US tax treaty caps it at 15% with a W-8BEN on file, against 30% without one. US-listed holdings in a documented RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF are exempt. The 15% is lost in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP and can be claimed as a foreign tax credit in a taxable account.

Which account is best for US stocks?

In general terms, US dividend payers keep the most in a documented RRSP, where nothing is withheld on US-listed holdings held directly. Growth-focused US holdings suit a TFSA, where gains are never taxed in Canada and little is lost to withholding. A taxable account lets you claim foreign tax and use losses. Your own choice depends on your tax rates and room.

Can I buy foreign stocks in Canada on overseas exchanges?

Some brokers may offer it, but direct access to exchanges outside North America was not listed by broker in the pricing we checked on October 22, 2026. Many foreign companies trade in the US as depositary receipts, and funds hold international stocks. Ask a broker for its list of markets and its rates for each currency.

What is currency risk?

It is the effect of exchange rate changes on the Canadian-dollar value of a foreign holding. US$10,000 of shares bought at C$1.40 per US dollar cost C$14,000; if the rate falls to C$1.30 and the price is unchanged, they are worth C$13,000. A rise in the rate has the opposite effect.

Do I need a US dollar account to invest internationally?

Not to buy, but it usually lowers costs if you hold US-listed investments. Without one, every purchase, sale and dividend is converted. Questrade lists no separate fee for US dollars in registered accounts, Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core, and Qtrade US$15 a quarter per account unless you have Investor Plus.

How are foreign dividends taxed in a non-registered account?

You report the gross dividend in Canadian dollars as foreign income, taxed at your full marginal rate without the dividend tax credit. You then claim a foreign tax credit for the foreign tax withheld, the lesser of the tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income, on Form T2209 and, outside Quebec, Form T2036.
