1. Capital Gains Tax Calculator Canada: How to Use It

Proceeds of disposition. Enter what you sold the property for: the number of shares or units times the sale price, before commissions. For US-dollar sales, convert to Canadian dollars at the exchange rate on the day of the sale.

Adjusted cost base. Enter your total cost, including purchase commissions. If you bought the same security more than once, your ACB is the average cost of all units you held, not the price of the last purchase. Section 5 explains how to work it out.

Outlays and expenses. Enter selling costs such as the commission on the sale, or legal and real estate fees on a property. They reduce your gain.

Other taxable income. Enter your income for 2026 from all other sources: salary, self-employment, pensions, RRSP or RRIF withdrawals, interest and the grossed-up amount of dividends. Your marginal rate depends on where the taxable half of the gain lands on top of this income.

Province and losses. Choose your province or territory of residence on December 31, 2026. If you have net capital losses from earlier years, enter them: they reduce the taxable part of the gain.

Read the result. Treat the answer as a planning estimate. Your final tax can differ because of credits and deductions the calculator does not ask about, such as CPP and EI credits, medical expenses, donations or the pension income amount.

2. Capital Gains Inclusion Rate in 2026: The Rules Behind the Numbers

The formula. A capital gain is the proceeds of disposition minus your adjusted cost base and selling costs. The taxable capital gain is that gain times the inclusion rate.

How a capital gain is calculated (hypothetical figures)
StepRuleExample
1. Proceeds of dispositionSale price before costs$25,000
2. Less adjusted cost baseTotal cost, including purchase commissions$15,000
3. Less outlays and expensesSelling commission or fees$10
4. Capital gainStep 1 minus steps 2 and 3$9,990
5. Taxable capital gainCapital gain x 50%$4,995

One half in 2026. The capital gains inclusion rate was one half for 2001 through 2025, and it remains one half for 2026. The proposed increase to two-thirds did not take effect, so the same rate applies to individuals at every level of gain.

Taxed at your marginal rate. There is no separate capital gains tax. The taxable half is added to your income and taxed with it. In 2026 the federal brackets are 14% up to $58,523, 20.5% up to $117,045, 26% up to $181,440, 29% up to $258,482 and 33% above that, plus provincial tax.

What counts. Shares, ETF and mutual fund units, bonds sold before maturity, crypto-assets, rental and recreational property, and land can all produce capital gains. Gains on your principal residence are usually exempt.

Capital or business income. If you buy and sell with the frequency and know-how of a professional trader, the CRA can treat your profits as business income, which is fully taxable. Occasional sales of long-held investments are normally on capital account.

The lifetime exemption. Owners of qualified small business shares and farm or fishing property may shelter gains with the lifetime capital gains exemption. Budget 2025 listed the proposed increase of that exemption to $1.25 million of eligible gains, first announced in Budget 2024.

3. Worked Examples

This example is hypothetical. Each person has only the basic personal amount as a tax credit, and the figures use 2026 federal and provincial brackets.

  1. Priya, Ontario, $85,000 salary. She sells shares for $30,000 that cost $18,000 and pays a $10 commission. Her capital gain is $11,990 ($30,000 − $18,000 − $10), and the taxable half is $5,995 ($11,990 x 50%). Federal tax is $1,228.98 ($5,995 x 20.5%) and Ontario tax is $548.54 ($5,995 x 9.15%), with no Ontario surtax or extra health premium at her income. Total: $1,777.52, or 14.83% of the gain ($1,777.52 ÷ $11,990).
  2. Marc, Quebec, $60,000 income, with an old loss. He sells for a $10,000 gain, so the taxable half is $5,000. He has a $6,000 capital loss from 2024 that he never used, which is a $3,000 net capital loss ($6,000 x 50%). Deducting it leaves $2,000 taxable ($5,000 − $3,000). Federal tax is $410.00 ($2,000 x 20.5%), less the 16.5% Quebec abatement of $67.65, so $342.35. Quebec tax is $380.00 ($2,000 x 19%). Total: $722.35, compared with $1,805.88 without the loss, a saving of $1,083.53.
  3. Dana, Alberta, $120,000 income. She sells a non-registered ETF holding for a $40,000 gain. The taxable half is $20,000. Federal tax is $5,200 ($20,000 x 26%) and Alberta tax is $2,000 ($20,000 x 10%). Total: $7,200, or 18% of the gain ($7,200 ÷ $40,000). Splitting the sale across two tax years would not lower her tax, because both halves stay in the same brackets.

What the examples show. The same gain is taxed very differently depending on your other income and province. Marc's loss carry-forward cut his tax by more than half. Dana's case shows that spreading a sale only helps when it keeps part of the gain out of a higher bracket or below a benefit threshold such as the OAS clawback.

5. Adjusted Cost Base and Selling Costs

What goes into ACB. Your adjusted cost base is what you paid, plus purchase commissions and other acquisition costs. For real estate it includes legal fees and land transfer tax on purchase and the cost of capital improvements, but not repairs or upkeep.

Average cost for identical units. When you buy the same stock or fund more than once, your ACB per unit is the total cost of all units divided by the number of units. Selling some units does not change the average for the rest; buying more does.

Reinvested distributions. Distributions reinvested in more units, and reinvested capital gains distributions from ETFs and mutual funds, add to your ACB because you already paid tax on them. Missing them makes you pay tax twice. A return of capital does the opposite and lowers your ACB.

US and foreign securities. Convert the purchase cost at the exchange rate on the purchase date and the proceeds at the rate on the sale date. A currency move alone can create a gain or loss. Conversion fees also affect your real return: see the currency conversion fee calculator, how to buy US stocks in Canada and Norbert's Gambit.

Your records, your responsibility. Brokers report proceeds, but the CRA expects you to support your ACB. Please keep trade confirmations and year-end statements for every holding until several years after you sell it. If you move between brokers, the new firm may not know your original cost.

Selling costs. Commissions, real estate commissions and legal fees on the sale are outlays and expenses that reduce the gain. Choosing a lower-cost broker cuts these costs: compare the best online brokers in Canada or read our Questrade review.

6. Capital Losses: Carry-Back, Carry-Forward and the Superficial Loss Rule

Losses offset only gains. A capital loss reduces capital gains in the same year. If losses are larger than gains, the unused part becomes a net capital loss, which can only be used against taxable capital gains, never against salary or interest.

Carry back three years, forward indefinitely. You can carry a net capital loss back to any of the three previous years to recover tax already paid, by filing Form T1A with your return. Or carry it forward indefinitely and deduct it against future taxable capital gains.

The superficial loss rule. Your loss is denied if you, or an affiliated person such as your spouse or common-law partner, acquire identical property in the 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after the sale. The denied loss is usually added to the ACB of the property you bought back, so it is deferred rather than lost.

This example is hypothetical. Jordan in British Columbia buys 200 units at $50 ($10,000). In November he sells them at $40 ($8,000), a $2,000 loss. Ten days later he buys 200 units back at $41 ($8,200) and still holds them 30 days after the sale.

  1. The $2,000 loss is a superficial loss and cannot be claimed this year.
  2. It is added to the ACB of the new units: $8,200 + $2,000 = $10,200, or $51 per unit ($10,200 ÷ 200).
  3. When he later sells at $45 ($9,000), he reports a $1,200 capital loss ($9,000 − $10,200), which matches his real overall result ($10,000 paid, $8,000 + $9,000 received, $8,200 paid again).

Registered accounts and the rule. Buying the same security back in your TFSA or RRSP within 30 days also triggers the rule, and the loss is not added to any ACB because registered accounts do not track one. That loss is gone for good.

Year-end timing. A tax-loss sale must settle by the last business day of the year to count for that year, so place the trade a few days early.

7. Principal Residence Exemption Basics

What it does. The principal residence exemption can eliminate the capital gain on a home you, your spouse or your child ordinarily lived in. It covers a house, condo, cottage, cabin or mobile home, with land around it.

One per family per year. A family unit can designate only one property as its principal residence for each year. Couples who own a city home and a cottage must choose which property to designate for each year, often the one with the larger gain per year.

The formula. The exempt part of the gain is one plus the number of years designated, divided by the number of years owned, times the gain. The extra year protects people who buy one home and sell another in the same year.

You must report the sale. Even when the whole gain is exempt, you report the sale and the designation on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND) for the year you sell. Forgetting the designation can lead to a late-filing penalty, and the CRA can deny the exemption if the designation is never made.

When the exemption does not apply. Rental properties, homes bought to resell, and the rented part of a home you changed to income use can produce taxable gains. Homes sold after a short holding period can fall under the residential property flipping rule, which taxes the full profit as business income unless a listed life event, such as a death, separation or new job, applies.

TFSA. Gains in a TFSA are never taxed, which makes it the natural home for investments you expect to grow most. The 2026 limit is $7,000; check your room with the TFSA contribution limit guide. Frequent day trading inside a TFSA can be taxed as business income.

RRSP and RRIF. There is no capital gains treatment inside an RRSP. Every dollar withdrawn, whether it came from contributions, interest or gains, is taxed as ordinary income. FHSA and RESP. Gains in an FHSA come out tax-free on a qualifying home withdrawal; the FHSA calculator tracks your room. In an RESP, growth is paid out to the student as educational assistance payments and taxed in the student's hands, usually at a low rate.

Where to hold what. Investments you plan to trade or that may lose value can sit in a non-registered account, where losses are usable. Long-term growth fits a TFSA. Compare the best TFSA accounts and weigh the TFSA vs RRSP choice for your income.

10. Reporting: T5008, T3, Schedule 3 and Your Return

T5008 slips. Your broker issues a T5008 showing the proceeds of securities you sold. The cost box is often blank or wrong, especially after a transfer between brokers or for shares bought long ago. The CRA receives the proceeds figure, so report every sale even if the slip shows no cost.

T3 slips. Capital gains distributions from mutual funds and ETFs are reported on T3 slips (or Relevé 16 in Quebec). They are taxed as capital gains even if reinvested, and reinvested amounts raise your ACB.

Schedule 3. List each disposition on Schedule 3 with the date, proceeds, ACB and outlays. The total taxable capital gain flows to line 12700 of your return. Net capital losses of other years are deducted on line 25300, and a carry-back is requested on Form T1A.

Crypto-assets. Selling, trading one coin for another or spending crypto are dispositions. Each trade needs a cost and proceeds in Canadian dollars. Compare record-keeping tools on the best crypto platforms list.

When the tax is due. Tax on a gain is paid with your return for the year of the sale. A large gain can mean the CRA asks for tax instalments the following year, because no tax was withheld at source.

8 Ways to Reduce Capital Gains Tax

1. Fill your TFSA first

Gains in a TFSA are never taxed. Moving new savings there before a non-registered account shields future growth. Contribute cash and buy inside the account, because transferring a losing holding in does not let you claim the loss.

2. Harvest losses before year end

Selling a holding with an unrealized loss can offset gains from the same year. Respect the 30-day superficial loss rule, and avoid buying the same security back in your TFSA or RRSP.

3. Use losses carried forward

Net capital losses never expire. Check your notice of assessment for unused amounts before you sell a winner, as Marc did in the example above.

4. Carry back a new loss

A loss this year can recover tax paid on gains in any of the last three years. File Form T1A with your return to request it.

5. Time sales across tax years

Splitting a large sale between December and January can keep part of the gain out of a higher bracket or below the OAS clawback threshold. It only helps when your rate would actually change.

6. Track your ACB carefully

Missing reinvested distributions or purchase commissions overstates your gain. A simple spreadsheet updated with each trade can save real tax over decades.

7. Sell in a lower-income year

A sabbatical, parental leave or early retirement before CPP and OAS start can be a low-income year in which gains are taxed at a lower rate.

8. Designate your principal residence well

Families with a home and a cottage should work out which property to designate for each year before they sell, not after.

Capital Gains Tax Calculator Canada FAQ

How is capital gains taxed in Canada?

Only half of a capital gain is taxable in 2026. You subtract your adjusted cost base and selling costs from the sale price, take half of the result and add it to your income. That taxable half is taxed at your federal and provincial marginal rates, so there is no separate capital gains tax rate.

What is the capital gains inclusion rate for 2026?

The inclusion rate for 2026 is one half, or 50%. It was also one half for 2001 through 2025. The proposed increase to a two-thirds inclusion rate did not take effect, so individuals include half of every capital gain in income, whatever the size of the gain.

How much tax will I pay on a $10,000 capital gain?

It depends on your other income and province. With $100,000 of other income in 2026, a $10,000 gain costs $1,574.00 in Ontario, $1,529.62 in British Columbia, $1,525.00 in Alberta and $1,805.88 in Quebec. At $50,000 of other income, it costs $997.97 in Ontario. Use the calculator for your own figures.

What is the highest capital gains tax rate in Canada?

In 2026 the highest top combined rate on capital gains is 27.40% in Newfoundland and Labrador, on taxable income above $1,141,275. Other top rates include 27.00% in Nova Scotia, 26.76% in Ontario, 26.75% in British Columbia and 26.65% in Quebec. The lowest is 22.25% in Nunavut.

Can I use capital losses to reduce my taxes?

Yes, but only against capital gains. Capital losses first reduce gains in the same year. Any net capital loss left over can be carried back to the three previous years with Form T1A or carried forward indefinitely to reduce future taxable capital gains. Capital losses cannot reduce salary, pension or interest income.

What is the superficial loss rule?

Your capital loss is denied if you or an affiliated person, such as your spouse, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after the sale. The denied loss is usually added to the cost of the repurchased property. If the repurchase happens in a TFSA or RRSP, the loss is lost.

Do I pay capital gains tax when I sell my home?

Usually not, if it was your principal residence for every year you owned it. You must still report the sale and the designation on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND). Only one property per family can be designated for each year, and rental use or a quick resale can make part or all of the profit taxable.

Do I pay capital gains tax inside a TFSA or RRSP?

No. Gains inside a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, FHSA or RESP are not taxed as capital gains, and losses inside them cannot be claimed. TFSA withdrawals are tax-free. RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are taxed in full as ordinary income, whatever mix of contributions, interest or gains they came from.

What is a T5008 slip and is the cost on it correct?

A T5008 reports the proceeds of securities you sold during the year. The cost box is often blank or wrong, especially after a transfer between brokers. You are responsible for your own adjusted cost base, so check it against your trade records before you file and report every sale on Schedule 3.

When do I pay the tax on a capital gain?

You pay it with your tax return for the year you sold, because no tax is withheld when you sell investments. If a large gain leaves you with a big balance owing, the CRA may ask you to pay tax instalments during the following year. Setting aside part of the sale proceeds avoids a cash crunch.
