Best RESP Providers in Canada for 2026

The best RESP providers Canada families can choose in 2026, on our published score, are Questrade and Wealthsimple at 4.0 / 5, with Desjardins Online Brokerage third at 3.9 / 5. We ranked the 9 online brokers that offer RESPs and the 3 ranked robo-advisors that manage them. Four of the nine brokers charge no RESP account fee and $0 commissions on Canadian stocks and ETFs, though converting to US dollars still has a cost, such as 1.5% at Questrade. 2. Which RESP Provider Type Suits Which Family

Self-directed online brokers. You choose every investment, decide when to buy and rebalance yourself. Costs are trading commissions, any account fee and the fees inside the funds you pick. At the four brokers with $0 trades and no RESP account fee, a family that buys ETFs every month can pay $0 to the broker, though the funds themselves still carry their own costs. This route suits parents who are comfortable choosing and adjusting a portfolio, and who want full control over contribution timing.

Robo-advisors. You answer questions, and the robo-advisor builds, rebalances and reinvests a portfolio for you. You pay a yearly percentage of the balance plus fund costs: from 0.37% to 0.83% all-in on $50,000 among the three we rank. That cost grows with the plan, so a $40,000 RESP costs $148 to $332 a year. It suits parents who want the RESP to run itself and value automatic rebalancing over the lowest cost.

Bank and co-operative brokerages. The bank-owned dealers (BMO, CIBC, RBC, Scotia iTRADE and National Bank) and Desjardins, part of a co-operative group, all offer RESPs, and having the account next to your chequing account can make automatic contributions easy. The trade-off is cost: CIBC, RBC and Scotia iTRADE still charge $6.95 to $9.99 per trade outside their $0 ETF lists, and four of the six have a small-balance fee.

Group plan dealers. A group plan pools families' savings, sets the contribution schedule for you and chooses the investments. Section 6 explains how they work in general terms. They suit a narrow group: families who want no decisions at all and are sure they can keep up a fixed schedule for many years.

4. Family RESP Providers and Individual Plans

The family plan rule. A family plan can name more than one beneficiary, and it is the only kind of RESP that can. Every beneficiary must be related by blood or adoption to each living subscriber and must be under 21 when named. Each one needs a social insurance number and Canadian residency when designated. That makes a family plan the natural home for siblings, and for grandparents saving for several grandchildren.

The individual plan. Any RESP that is not a family plan has a single beneficiary. It is the route when the subscriber is not related to the child by blood or adoption, such as a godparent or a family friend. If a provider offers only a family plan, those savers cannot use it.

6. Group Plans Explained in General Terms

How they work. A group plan, sometimes called a scholarship plan, pools the savings of children born in the same year. You sign up for a contribution schedule, and the dealer invests the pooled money under the rules in its prospectus. When the children reach school age, payments to students depend on the plan's rules and on how many members stayed in until the end.

Fees. Group plans usually charge enrolment or sales fees, and these are often collected in the early years of the plan. The fee table in the prospectus shows what you pay and when. If you stop paying, change plans or close early, part of what you paid in fees may not come back to you.

Investment choice. You do not choose investments. The dealer invests the pool, and group plans commonly hold conservative fixed income. That removes decisions but also removes control over risk and cost.

Grants still apply. The CESG and other grants follow the same rules in a group plan as anywhere else, as long as the plan requests them. The $50,000 lifetime limit per child is also the same.

Where they do not fit. A group plan is a poor fit for a family whose income may change, who may move a child to a different program or who wants low, visible costs. Please read the whole prospectus, especially the sections on missed payments, leaving early and what happens if the child chooses a program the plan does not accept, before you sign.

How to compare. Put the total fees over the life of the plan next to the yearly cost of a self-directed or managed RESP at your expected balance. Section 3 gives the self-directed and robo figures for four balances.

7. US Investments and Currency Costs in an RESP

US withholding tax is not refunded inside an RESP. The Canada-US tax treaty's exemption for retirement accounts does not cover RESPs. US dividends paid into an RESP have 15% withheld when the broker has a Form W-8BEN on file, or 30% without one, and that tax cannot be claimed back as a foreign tax credit inside the plan. The same is true in a TFSA, as our guide to US stocks in a TFSA explains.

Holding US dollars. Only some providers state a way to keep US dollars in an RESP. Questrade says registered accounts can hold US dollars at no extra cost. Wealthsimple lets you add a US-dollar account to an RESP for $10 plus tax a month on Core, included on Premium and Generation. Scotia iTRADE holds its RESP in Canadian dollars and offers a U.S.-Friendly RESP for $30 a quarter, which is $120 a year. RBC says its investment accounts can hold both currencies but does not state the price for registered accounts. The other five brokers did not state a US-dollar option for RESPs.

What conversion costs. Without a US-dollar account, every US-listed purchase and sale is converted, so the cost applies twice: converting $5,000 at 1.5% costs $75 ($5,000 x 1.5%), and converting it back costs another $75. The table shows each broker's published rate.

8. Safety: CIPF, CDIC and CIRO for RESPs

CIPF coverage has its own RESP category. If a dealer that is a member of the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) fails, CIPF covers missing cash and securities. All RESPs where you are the subscriber are covered together up to $1,000,000, separate from your general accounts (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA, up to $1,000,000 combined) and your retirement accounts (RRSP, RRIF and LIF, up to $1,000,000 combined). CIPF does not cover a fall in market value or crypto assets.

CDIC covers deposits, not investments. If your RESP holds savings deposits or GICs at a CDIC member, those deposits are insured as their own category up to $100,000 per member institution. CDIC does not cover stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds or crypto.

CIRO oversees the dealers. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO) regulates every investment dealer in Canada, and CIPF member firms are CIRO members. For three of the nine brokers, CIRO membership follows from the CIPF listing rather than a statement on their own pages.

9. How to Choose the Best RESP Account for Your Family

Decide the structure before the brand. The best RESP account for one family can be the wrong one for another, because plan type, balance and how much work you want to do matter more than any single fee. Work through these steps in order.

  1. Decide who will run the portfolio. If you will choose and rebalance investments, start with the brokers. If you want it done for you, start with the robo-advisors and compare all-in cost at your balance.
  2. Pick the plan type. Siblings, or grandchildren with one grandparent, point to a family plan. A godparent or friend needs an individual plan. Rule out any provider that does not state the type you need.
  3. Estimate your balance and trades. Under $15,000, the Desjardins and Scotia iTRADE fees apply; under $20,000, National Bank's applies unless you qualify for a waiver; at $10,000 or less, CIBC's applies after year one.
  4. List the grants you need. Every family wants the CESG. Lower-income families need the CLB, B.C. families the BCTESG and Quebec families the QESI. Ask each shortlisted provider in writing.
  5. Decide on US dollars. If you want US-listed holdings, favour a provider with a stated US-dollar option and a low conversion cost.
  6. Check the exit. Note the transfer-out fee ($0 at Wealthsimple, $150 at most others) and any rebate for moving in.
  7. Open the plan and name each beneficiary. Have every child's social insurance number ready, set up automatic contributions and confirm the grant requests are switched on.

If two providers tie. Choose the one where you already hold other accounts, so statements, contributions and transfers sit in one place. 10. How to Switch RESP Providers

Transfer, never withdraw. Open the new RESP first, then ask the new provider to pull the money across with its transfer form. A transfer keeps contributions, grants and growth inside the RESP system. Taking the money out yourself is a withdrawal, which has its own rules and can affect the grants.

What it costs to leave and arrive. Most brokers charge $150 to transfer an account out, and several pay some or all of that back when you move in.

Opening separate plans for siblings when one would do. A family plan holds every child in one account. Separate individual plans mean separate statements, separate contributions and, at providers with small-balance fees, separate thresholds to clear.

10 Strategies to Get More From Your RESP Provider

1. Settle the plan type first

Write down every child you want to cover and your relationship to each. That one list tells you whether you need a family plan, an individual plan or both, and it rules out providers before you compare a single fee.

2. Put $2,500 per child in each year

The CESG pays 20% on the first $2,500 a year per child, so $2,500 earns the full $500. For two children in a family plan, that is $5,000 in and $1,000 of grant.

3. Catch up at $5,000 a year

Unused grant room carries forward, and a child can receive up to $1,000 of CESG in one year. A family that skipped a year can contribute $5,000 for one child to collect the missed $500.

4. Clear the fee threshold or avoid it

If your provider charges under $15,000 or $20,000, a transfer or larger first deposit may clear it from day one. Otherwise, choose one of the four brokers with no RESP fee and $0 stock and ETF trades.

5. Use the $0 ETF list at a bank broker

CIBC lists 180+ $0 ETFs, Scotia iTRADE 200+ and RBC 50+. Buying only from those lists turns a $119.40 commission bill into $0, leaving only any account fee.

6. Ask about every grant in writing

Before you open, ask the provider to confirm it requests the CESG, additional CESG and CLB, plus the BCTESG or QESI if you live in B.C. or Quebec. Keep the answer with your records.

7. Keep the RESP in Canadian dollars unless you have a reason

US dividends lose 15% inside an RESP that cannot be recovered, and every conversion has a cost, such as 1.5% each way at Questrade and Wealthsimple. A Canadian-dollar plan avoids both costs.

8. Let a rebate pay for the move

Questrade rebates up to $150 per account, RBC up to $200 on $15,000 or more, and Wealthsimple up to $150 per account on $25,000 or more. Match your transfer size to the rule before you start.

9. Price a managed RESP at your real balance

A robo-advisor's percentage fee grows with the plan. On $40,000, the three ranked robo-advisors cost from $148.00 to $332.00 a year. Run the robo-advisor fee calculator at the balance you expect in five years, not today's.

10. Check the plan once a year

Each January, confirm last year's grants arrived, check the balance against any fee threshold and adjust the investment mix as the first tuition bill gets closer.

Illustrative Example: A Two-Child Family Compares Providers

This example is hypothetical. Maya and Leo live in Ontario and have two children, aged 1 and 4. Their family income is above $117,045, so only the basic CESG applies. They hold a $20,000 family RESP at RBC Direct Investing, contribute $5,000 a year ($2,500 per child) and make 12 ETF purchases a year outside RBC's $0 ETF list.

  1. Grants. Each child earns 20% of $2,500 ($2,500 x 20% = $500), so the family plan collects $1,000 a year ($500 x 2). That is the same at every provider that requests the CESG.
  2. Cost today. RBC charges no account fee, so the cost is commissions only (12 x $9.95 = $119.40 a year).
  3. Moving to Questrade. Questrade charges $0 commission and no account fee, so the yearly cost falls to $0.00. RBC's $150 transfer-out fee is covered by Questrade's rebate of up to $150 per account ($150 - $150 = $0 net).
  4. Moving to Wealthsimple. The yearly cost is also $0.00. But $20,000 is under Wealthsimple's $25,000 reimbursement rule, so the $150 fee would not be refunded, and its RESP transfers in are still in beta.
  5. A managed option. On $20,000, Questwealth costs up to $74.00 a year ($20,000 x 0.25% = $50.00, plus $20,000 x 0.12% = $24.00). Wealthsimple Managed Investing on Core costs up to $130.00 ($100.00 + $30.00).
  6. Ten years of commissions. Staying at RBC with the same buying pattern costs $1,194.00 over 10 years ($119.40 x 10), before any growth on the money saved.
  7. What they would weigh. Both Questrade and Qtrade state family plans; Questrade's rebate fully covers the $150 exit fee on this transfer. If they would rather not choose investments, Questwealth is the lowest-cost managed route at their balance.

Open the new RESP first, then ask the new provider to transfer the plan with its transfer form. Do not withdraw the money yourself. Most firms charge $150 to transfer out, and Questrade, RBC, Qtrade and Wealthsimple refund some or all of it when you move in, each under its own conditions.

Is my RESP protected if the broker fails?

At a CIPF member dealer, all RESPs where you are the subscriber are covered together up to $1,000,000 for missing cash and securities, separate from your other accounts. CIPF does not cover market losses. Deposits and GICs inside an RESP at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per institution.

Does every RESP provider request the Canada Learning Bond?

Not necessarily, and providers do not publish it. Please ask in writing before you open a plan. The CLB pays $500 in the first year and $100 a year after, up to $2,000, with no contribution needed. From April 2028 the government will open an RESP automatically for eligible children born in 2024 or later.
