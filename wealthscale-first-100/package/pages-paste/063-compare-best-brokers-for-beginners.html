Three different minimums. A minimum to open is the amount you must deposit to start. "No minimum to open" says nothing about the other two.

Why small amounts favour $0 brokers. On a $100 purchase, a $9.99 commission is 9.99% of the amount invested, and a $1.49 minimum is 1.49%. At a $0 broker the commission is 0%. For investors starting with $50 or $100 a month, that difference matters more than any other feature on this page.

Whole shares and cash drag. Without fractional shares, a $100 monthly deposit can only buy whole shares, and whatever is left over waits as cash, which earns nothing at several brokers. Over a year, that leftover cash can be a noticeable part of a small account. Fractional shares, covered next, solve this.

3. Fractional Shares: Buying Part of a Share

What a fractional share is. A fractional share is part of one share. If a share costs $400 and you invest $100, you own one quarter of a share and receive one quarter of its dividends. Fractional shares let you invest an exact dollar amount, spread a small sum over several holdings and reinvest every dividend in full.

Who offers them. Five of the 13 brokers publish fractional shares. Wealthsimple sells thousands of Canadian and US stocks and ETFs from $1, in real time during market hours. Questrade offers eligible Canadian and US stocks and ETFs, commission-free and in real time; its minimum is not stated. Interactive Brokers offers TSX and Cboe Canada stocks and ETFs (since August 1, 2023) and US stocks, charged on its normal schedule, so the $1 minimum applies. CIBC advertises fractional shares, with details on a separate page. TD charges $1.99 for each partial-share order, and TD Easy Trade accepts dollar-amount orders from $1. moomoo states it does not offer them.

4. Recurring Buys and Automatic Investing

Automatic beats remembering. A recurring buy invests a set amount on a schedule without you placing each order. Investing a fixed amount on a schedule, often called dollar-cost averaging, means you buy more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high, and it removes the temptation to wait for a better moment.

Who publishes it. Wealthsimple offers recurring investments daily, weekly, every two weeks or monthly in stocks, ETFs and crypto. In an RESP, they are funded only from cash already in the account, and they are not available in joint non-registered accounts with USD enabled. moomoo states that auto-invest is available. The other 11 brokers do not describe automatic purchases on the pages we checked. TD's fee waiver for $100 or more a month in pre-authorized contributions shows that automatic deposits are possible there, but a deposit is not the same as a purchase: the cash still has to be invested.

Why fractional shares matter here. A recurring $100 purchase of a $40 ETF buys 2.5 units with fractional shares. Without them, it buys 2 units and leaves $20 as cash. Recurring buys work best at a broker that also sells fractions.

Cost per recurring buy. A weekly buy is 52 orders a year. At a $0 broker that costs nothing; at a $9.99 broker it costs $519.48 a year. Profile 2 in the next section prices this for all 13 brokers.

6. Which Account to Open First

The account matters more than the broker. The account type decides how your money is taxed. The TFSA, FHSA and RRSP are the main registered accounts for a first investor, and every broker on this page offers the TFSA and RRSP. The general rules below come from Canada Revenue Agency publications; which one suits you depends on your income, your plans and your timeline.

TFSA. Growth and withdrawals are tax-free. The 2026 limit is $7,000, and someone eligible since 2009 who has never contributed has $109,000 of room. Room starts in the year you turn 18 (you can open one at 19 where that is the age of majority), and a withdrawal is added back to your room on January 1 of the next year. Our TFSA guide and TFSA room calculator cover the details.

FHSA. If neither you nor your spouse or partner owned and lived in a home in the current year before opening or in the previous four calendar years, an FHSA gives a deduction going in and tax-free withdrawals for a qualifying first home. The limit is $8,000 a year and $40,000 for life, room starts only when you open your first FHSA, and up to $8,000 of unused room carries forward. Opening one early starts the room clock. See our FHSA guide.

RRSP. Contributions are deductible, and withdrawals are taxed as income. The 2026 dollar limit is $33,810, and your own limit is 18% of your prior-year earned income up to that amount, plus unused room. US dividends are exempt from US withholding tax in an RRSP. Our TFSA vs RRSP comparison shows when each wins.

You do not have to start with US stocks. A first portfolio can be built entirely from Canadian-listed ETFs, some of which hold US and international companies. Buying a Canadian-listed security in Canadian dollars involves no currency conversion. If you do want US-listed shares, it helps to know what they cost before your first order.

The conversion fee. Buying a US-listed share with Canadian dollars means converting at the broker's rate: 1.5% at Wealthsimple, Questrade and Webull, 1.6% at BMO, CIBC and RBC, about 1.70% at National Bank and 1.26% at TD, on amounts under $25,000 (US$25,000 at BMO and National Bank). 10. Safety and Regulation

Safety counts for 20% here. We give safety more weight in the beginner score than in the overall score, because a new investor is less likely to spot a problem with a firm. Every one of the 13 brokers is on the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) member list, and CIPF member firms are regulated by the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO).

What CIPF covers. If a member firm becomes insolvent, CIPF covers missing cash and securities up to $1,000,000 for all your general accounts combined (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA), a separate $1,000,000 for RRSPs, RRIFs and LIFs, and a separate $1,000,000 for RESPs. It does not cover a fall in market value, unsuitable advice or crypto assets. Wealthsimple and Webull both state that crypto held through them is not CIPF-protected.

13. Opening a Beginner Investing App and Placing Your First Trade

Opening an account online takes a few steps. The order below works at any broker on this page.

  1. Gather what you need. Your Social Insurance Number, government photo ID, home address, employer details and a Canadian bank account.
  2. Open the account. Choose the account type (for example, TFSA), then answer the questions about your income, goals and investing knowledge. Brokers ask these to meet their regulatory duties.
  3. Fund it. Link your bank account and make a deposit, or start a transfer from another institution. For a registered account at another institution, start the transfer at the new broker so it moves directly.
  4. Complete Form W-8BEN if you will buy US shares. It lets the broker apply the 15% treaty rate to US dividends instead of 30%.
  5. Place your first order. Search for the stock or ETF, enter the amount or number of shares, and choose the order type. A limit order sets the highest price you will pay; a market order fills at the current price. At Wealthsimple, orders outside regular hours must be limit orders.

  7. Check the confirmation and keep a record. Read the trade confirmation for any fees, and note every TFSA, FHSA and RRSP contribution, because your room is one combined limit across every institution where you hold that type of account.

14. Common Beginner Mistakes

Re-contributing a TFSA withdrawal too soon. A TFSA withdrawal is added back to your room on January 1 of the next year, not right away. Putting it back in the same year can create an excess, taxed at 1% a month. Please check your room before you re-contribute.

Trading too often in a TFSA. $0 commissions make frequent trading easy, but the Canada Revenue Agency can treat frequent trading inside a TFSA as carrying on a business, and that income can be taxed.

8 Strategies for Your First Year of Investing

1. Automate on payday

Schedule your contribution for the day your pay arrives, and a recurring buy for the day after if your broker offers one. Money that is invested automatically is less likely to be spent.

2. Open an FHSA early if a home is possible

FHSA room starts only when you open your first FHSA, at $8,000 a year. Opening one this year, even with a small deposit, starts the room you can carry forward.

3. Keep the number of accounts small

Fees such as Webull's $0.70 a month apply per account, and each extra account adds tracking work. Start with the one account you need most and add others when you have a reason.

4. Keep a contribution log

Record every TFSA, FHSA and RRSP contribution and withdrawal with its date. If you hold accounts at more than one institution, a log is the easiest way to see your combined total during the year.

5. Switch on DRIP

Dividend reinvestment puts each dividend back to work without a new order. Questrade's DRIP has no fee and reinvests fractionally once fractional shares are on.

6. Leave margin and options for later

Borrowing to invest and options carry risks a first-year investor may not see. Learn how they work before you apply, and keep your first year to buying and holding.

7. Put US dividend payers in an RRSP

Once you have an RRSP, US dividends inside it are exempt from the 15% US withholding tax. In a TFSA, that tax is lost. This is a general rule, not a reason to open an RRSP on its own.

8. Review once a year

Check your broker's fees and our rankings each year. If your balance grows past a fee threshold or your needs change, a move may save money, and many brokers reimburse the transfer fee.

Illustrative Example: $250 a Month in a TFSA

This example is hypothetical. It uses published prices as of October 22, 2026, and ignores investment returns.

  1. The plan. Maya, 26, invests $250 a month in a TFSA, buying one Canadian-listed ETF each month: $250 x 12 = $3,000 a year.
  2. TFSA room. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, so her $3,000 leaves $7,000 minus $3,000 = $4,000 of 2026 room, which carries forward to later years.
  3. At a $9.99 broker. 12 x $9.99 = $119.88 a year in commissions, which is $119.88 / $3,000 = 4.0% of what she invests.
  4. Over five years. $119.88 x 5 = $599.40 in commissions on $3,000 x 5 = $15,000 contributed.
  5. If she buys weekly instead. 52 x $9.99 = $519.48 a year, or $519.48 / $3,000 = 17.3% of what she invests.
  6. At a $0 broker. Monthly or weekly, her commissions are $0. At Interactive Brokers, 12 monthly trades cost 12 x $1.00 = $12.00, or 0.4% of $3,000.
  7. At TD Easy Trade. Her 12 or 52 trades fall within the 100 $0 trades a year, so commissions are $0.

For a small, regular investor, the commission is the largest controllable cost. The broker choice matters less once the commission is $0 and no account fee applies at her balance or age.
