7. US Withholding Tax by Account Type

The treaty rate. The United States taxes dividends that US companies pay to Canadian residents. Article X(2)(b) of the Canada-US tax treaty caps that tax at 15% for an ordinary investor, and the broker withholds it before the dividend reaches your account. To get the treaty rate, your broker needs a valid Form W-8BEN on file. Without one, the rate is 30%.

The retirement exemption. Article XXI(2) of the treaty exempts dividends and interest earned by an arrangement operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits that is generally tax-exempt in Canada. RRSPs, RRIFs and locked-in plans such as LIRAs and LIFs qualify. US-listed securities held directly in them have no US tax withheld on dividends once your broker has the account documented.

12. How to Switch Brokers and Move US Holdings

Transfer in kind. When you move to a new broker, ask for an in-kind transfer: your US stocks move as they are, without being sold. Please confirm that the receiving account can hold US dollars and US-listed securities before you start.

Registered accounts move directly. A TFSA or RRSP must move broker to broker as a direct transfer. A direct TFSA transfer does not affect your contribution room. 13. Common Mistakes When Buying US Stocks

Letting every trade convert. At Wealthsimple, a US trade from a Canadian-dollar account pays 1.5% each time, and so does the sale. If you trade US stocks often, converting once into a US-dollar account, or using a broker that holds US dollars at no cost, stops paying the fee twice.

Choosing on commission alone. A $0 commission broker that charges 1.5% to convert costs $150.00 on a $10,000 purchase. A broker that charges US$1 a trade and US$2 to convert costs US$3.00 for the same purchase. Always add both.

Putting high-dividend US holdings in a TFSA by default. The TFSA loses 15% of every US dividend for good. If you have RRSP room, US-listed holdings that pay large dividends keep the full dividend there. Low-dividend holdings lose little in a TFSA. This is a general placement rule, not advice on any security.

Converting in pieces at a tiered broker. At TD, $25,000 converted as two $12,500 orders pays 1.26% on each ($315.00 in total); one conversion of $25,000 pays 0.78% ($195.00). At Wealthsimple's USD account, $100,000 in one transaction costs 0%, but four conversions of $25,000 cost 0.5% each ($500.00 in total). Tiers apply per transaction.

Assuming every account can hold US dollars. Wealthsimple has no US-dollar RRIF or LIF. National Bank, Qtrade and TD Easy Trade keep the FHSA in Canadian dollars. If you buy a US stock in an account that cannot hold US dollars, the dividends and proceeds convert back automatically.

10 Strategies to Cut the Cost of US Stocks

1. Convert once, in size

Tiered brokers charge less on larger single conversions. TD drops from 1.26% to 0.78% at $25,000, and Wealthsimple's USD account falls to 0.5% at $25,000 and 0% at $100,000. One large conversion beats many small ones when you plan to invest the money anyway.

2. Keep US dollars where it costs nothing

Questrade, National Bank, Desjardins, moomoo and TD Easy Trade state no fee for holding US dollars in registered accounts. Holding US dollars means dividends and sale proceeds stay in US dollars until you choose to convert.

3. Use the RRSP for US dividends

US-listed securities held directly in a documented RRSP pay no US withholding on dividends. The same holding in a TFSA loses 15% for good. When you have room in both, the RRSP is the treaty-friendly home for US dividend income.

4. File the W-8BEN on day one

Without the form, the United States keeps 30% of dividends paid to a TFSA or non-registered account instead of 15%. Filing takes minutes and halves the tax from the first payment.

5. Use Norbert's Gambit at a $0 broker

At a broker with $0 Canadian ETF trades and a US-dollar side, the gambit replaces a 1.5% to 1.7% conversion with two trades and a journal. On $10,000 that saves up to $170.00 in published fees before spreads.

6. Do not reconvert dividends

A US dividend converted to Canadian dollars and later converted back pays the fee twice. Leaving dividends in US dollars, or reinvesting them in US dollars, avoids both conversions.

7. Match the pricing model to your orders

Per-share pricing at Interactive Brokers costs US$1 for up to 200 shares. Flat bank pricing costs $9.95 or $9.99 whatever the size. If you buy small amounts often, flat fees take a large share of each order.

8. Know Wealthsimple's thresholds

The USD account is included at $100,000 in assets (Premium) and a single conversion of $100,000 or more inside it costs 0%. Below Premium, the account costs $10 a month plus tax, so check whether your conversions save more than $120 a year.

9. Ask the questions brokers do not answer online

Where a broker does not state US-dollar registered accounts, journal fees or its spread, ask in writing before you move money. A published answer protects you if the price later changes.

10. Make the old broker's fee someone else's cost

Questrade rebates up to $150 per account, RBC up to $200 on $15,000 or more, and Wealthsimple covers the other firm's fee, up to $150 per account, on $25,000 or more. A $150 transfer-out fee need not be your cost if you time the move right.
