At a glance

Top pick. EQ Bank Personal Account scores 4.6 / 5. It has no monthly fee, unlimited transactions, no-fee unlimited Interac e-Transfers, ATM fees reimbursed at any ATM in Canada, and 2.75% interest when you direct deposit your pay (1.00% without). It does not offer cheque writing.

Runner-up. Wealthsimple Chequing scores 4.4 / 5. It pays 1.25% on the Core tier, 1.75% with a $2,000+ direct deposit in 30 days, and up to 2.25% on higher tiers. Its prepaid Visa card charges 0% on foreign currency, and ATM operator fees are reimbursed worldwide. Your cash is held in trust at CDIC member institutions, up to $1,000,000 combined.

Third and fourth. Manulife Bank Advantage Account and KOHO Essential both score 4.0 / 5, with Manulife ahead on the unrounded score. Manulife pays 1.50% and lets you write personal cheques, but charges per-transaction fees below a $1,000 balance. KOHO pays 2% after you opt in, and its Essential plan costs $0 only with direct deposit or $1,000 a month in deposits (otherwise $7 a month).

How we ranked. Each account gets a score out of 5 from five weighted factors: fees 35%, interest 20%, transactions 15%, ATM access 15% and protection and features 15%. The full rubric is in section 1. The score alone sets the order; no provider can pay for a position.

Big banks. The best-scoring big-bank accounts are National Bank The Connected and TD Unlimited Chequing at 3.0 / 5. Their monthly fees ($15.95 and $17.95) are waived if you keep $4,500 or $4,000 in the account, and none of the big-bank accounts in this list publishes a chequing interest rate above 0.000%.

9. Deposit Protection: CDIC Members and Trust Coverage

The basic rule. The Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation insures eligible deposits up to $100,000, including principal and interest, per insured category at each member institution. Categories are separate: deposits in one name, joint deposits, deposits in trust, and deposits inside an RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, RESP, RDSP or FHSA are each insured up to $100,000. CDIC covers deposits in Canadian or foreign currency, GICs and term deposits. It does not cover mutual funds, stocks, bonds, ETFs or crypto.

Members in their own right. Equitable Bank (EQ Bank), Manulife Bank of Canada, Tangerine Bank, President's Choice Bank and all six big banks are CDIC members. Simplii Financial is a trade name of CIBC, so Simplii deposits fall under CIBC's membership. If you hold both a CIBC account and a Simplii account in your own name, they share one $100,000 limit in that category.

Trust coverage at fintechs. Wealthsimple is not a CDIC member. It places chequing cash in trust at partner member institutions, which gives up to $1,000,000 combined coverage ($100,000 per beneficiary per member institution). It does not disclose the partner names for contractual reasons. KOHO places up to $100,000 at unnamed member institutions, but only after you opt in to earn interest. Neo says its chequing funds are eligible for CDIC insurance up to $100,000 through a member it does not name.

Watch for overlap. Because trust partners are not named, you cannot tell whether your own deposits at a bank overlap with a fintech's trust deposits at the same bank. If your total cash in one name approaches $100,000 at any one institution, spreading it across unrelated banks keeps each piece inside a separate limit.

11. How to Choose the Best Chequing Account in Canada

Start with your balance, not the bonus. The right chequing account Canada-wide depends on three numbers you already know: your usual balance, whether your pay can be direct deposited, and how often you take out cash. Work through these steps in order.

  1. Find your lowest daily balance. Look at the last three statements. If it never fell below $4,000, a big-bank package can be $0; if it did, those packages will charge you in those months.
  2. Check where your pay goes. EQ Bank pays 2.75% instead of 1.00% with pay direct deposit, Wealthsimple Core adds 0.50% with $2,000 in 30 days, and KOHO Essential is $0 only with direct deposit or $1,000 a month in deposits.
  3. Count your transactions. More than 12 a month rules out TD Minimum, BMO Practical and the RBC Day to Day debit limit; more than 18 rules out CIBC Everyday, Scotiabank Basic and The Minimalist.
  4. Map your cash habits. If you use ATMs often, choose reimbursement (EQ Bank in Canada, Wealthsimple worldwide) or a network near you.
  5. List what you cannot give up. Cheque writing, a branch, or a 0% foreign exchange card narrows the list fast.
  6. Check protection. Decide whether you want a CDIC member in its own right or are comfortable with trust coverage.
  7. Only then look at offers. Use a welcome offer to choose between two accounts that already fit, and note the end date.

Keep two accounts if it helps. Many people keep a big-bank account for cheques and branch needs and an online account for pay and interest. If you do, keep the big-bank account on its lowest-fee plan.

12. How to Switch Your Chequing Account

Open before you close. Open the new account first and confirm you can log in, deposit and send an e-Transfer. Keep the old account open until every payment has moved.

Move your pay. Give your employer the new account's institution, transit and account numbers, usually through a direct deposit form from the new bank. Your pay may land in the old account for one more cycle. Federal benefits paid by direct deposit need to be updated with the government separately.

Move pre-authorized payments. List every automatic debit from the last two statements: rent, insurance, phone, utilities, gym and loan payments. Update each one with the payee and confirm the change took effect before you close the old account.

Watch the fee month. If the old account has a balance waiver, moving your money out early can trigger a full monthly fee. Move the last of the balance right after the final payment clears, and close the account early in the month.

Time welcome offers. If you are chasing an offer, open the account before the deadline and start the qualifying direct deposits straight away. Simplii's offer, for example, needs 4 straight months of direct deposit.

Investment accounts are separate. Moving a TFSA or RRSP between institutions uses a transfer form, not a withdrawal. See how to transfer an investment account before you move registered money.

13. Common Chequing Account Mistakes

Paying a package fee by accident. One day below $4,000 at BMO, CIBC or Scotiabank costs the full month. Three such months at $16.95 add up to $50.85.

Leaving pay in a 0% account. A $5,000 balance earns $137.50 a year at 2.75% and nothing at TD's 0.000%.

Assuming "no fee" means no charges. No-fee accounts still charge for some things: Tangerine and PC Money charge $1.50 at other ATMs in Canada, PC Money charges $3.50 to cancel an e-Transfer, and Neo's new fees start January 1, 2027.

Missing the conditions on a rate. EQ Bank's 2.75% needs your pay. Wealthsimple's extra 0.50% needs $2,000 in direct deposits within 30 days. KOHO's 2% needs you to opt in.

Ignoring the protection structure. Fintech trust coverage is real but has its own rules, such as KOHO's opt-in. Keep amounts above $100,000 at one institution in mind.

Closing the old account too soon. A bounced pre-authorized payment can bring a non-sufficient-funds charge ($10 at TD, Scotiabank and PC Money) and the payee may add its own charge.

10 Strategies to Pay Less and Earn More on Chequing

1. Point your pay at the rate

Direct deposit is the switch that lifts EQ Bank from 1.00% to 2.75% and Wealthsimple Core from 1.25% to 1.75%. If your employer allows split deposits, send at least enough to meet the rule ($2,000 in 30 days at Wealthsimple) and leave the rest where it is.

2. Price the waiver balance

A $4,000 waiver balance that earns nothing has a cost: the interest it could earn elsewhere. At 2.75% that is $110 a year. Compare that with the $203.40 or $215.40 fee it saves before you decide which side wins for you.

3. Downgrade instead of paying

If you rarely meet a package's balance rule, a $3.95 or $4.00 account with 12 or 18 transactions costs $47.40 or $48.00 a year, against $203.40 or more for the package at full price.

4. Use age and status waivers

National Bank charges $0 for ages 0 to 24 on The Minimalist and The Connected. Scotiabank's Basic account is $0 for seniors, and TD waives its Minimum account for newcomers in their first year.

5. Keep chequing lean

Chequing is for money you will spend this month. A larger cushion can sit in a savings account or GIC paying more, with CDIC cover in its own category if it is inside a TFSA or RRSP. The compound interest calculator shows what the higher rate adds up to.

6. Pick ATMs on purpose

If you use cash, choose an account that reimburses fees or has machines where you live. Two $1.50 withdrawals a month add up to $36 a year.

7. Avoid paid plans unless the maths works

KOHO Everything beats Essential only above $11,800 when billed annually. Check the break-even before you pay for a higher rate.

8. Stack offers carefully

Open an account with a live offer only if it already fits your banking. Put the end date and every condition in your calendar on the day you apply.

9. Use a 0% FX card abroad

A card with no foreign exchange charge and reimbursed ATM fees, such as Wealthsimple's prepaid Visa, removes two travel costs at once.

10. Review once a year

Rates, fees and offers change. CIBC's Smart Account fee rises on January 1, 2027, and Neo's new fees start the same day. A yearly check catches both.

Illustrative Example: Moving a $6,000 Balance

This example is hypothetical. Daniel lives in Ontario and earns $80,000 a year. He keeps about $6,000 in a Scotiabank Preferred Package and usually stays above $4,000, so he pays no monthly fee. He wants to know what moving his pay and balance to the EQ Bank Personal Account would change.

  1. Current cost. In a year where his balance never dips below $4,000, he pays $0 in fees and earns $0 in interest, because Scotiabank pays no interest on the package.
  2. A year with three dips. If his balance falls below $4,000 for one day in three separate months, he pays the full fee each time (3 x $16.95 = $50.85).
  3. Interest at EQ Bank. With his pay direct deposited, EQ Bank pays 2.75% ($6,000 x 2.75% = $165.00 a year), with no monthly fee and no balance rule.
  4. The gap. In a clean year EQ Bank is ahead by $165.00 ($165.00 - $0). In a year with three dips it is ahead by $215.85 ($165.00 + $50.85).
  5. Tax on the interest. Interest is taxed as income. At his 2026 marginal rate of 29.65% (federal 20.5% plus Ontario 9.15% at $80,000), tax on $165.00 is $48.92 ($165.00 x 29.65%), leaving $116.08.
  6. What he gives up. EQ Bank does not offer cheque writing or branches. If Daniel still writes cheques, he could keep a low-fee account that offers them, such as TD Minimum Chequing at $3.95 a month ($47.40 a year), and still come out ahead in either year ($165.00 - $47.40 = $117.60 before tax in a clean year).
