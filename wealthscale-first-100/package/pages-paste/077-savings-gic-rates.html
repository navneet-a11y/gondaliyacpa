3. Cashable vs Non-Redeemable GICs

Non-redeemable GICs. A non-redeemable GIC locks your money until maturity, and every top rate on this page is for one. Manulife's 1- to 5-year GICs cannot be redeemed early except on death. Outlook's and Achieva's GICs cannot be redeemed before maturity, all of Saven's GICs are non-redeemable, all of Peoples Trust's are non-redeemable, Alterna's eTerm GICs are cashable only at maturity, and Simplii sells only non-redeemable GICs with a 1-year minimum term.

Cashable and redeemable GICs. A cashable or redeemable GIC lets you take money out before maturity, on terms that vary by provider, in return for a lower rate. Oaken's 1-year cashable GIC pays 2.25%, RBC's 1-year cashable pays 2.15%, Desjardins' 1-year redeemable pays 2.00%, and Scotiabank's 24-month Personal Redeemable GIC special pays 2.75%. CIBC, National Bank and Manulife list cashable or redeemable GICs without rates on the pages we checked.

The price of flexibility. On $10,000 for a year, a 3.85% non-redeemable GIC pays $385 and a 2.25% cashable GIC pays $225: the right to cash out early costs $160 ($10,000 x 1.60%). At RBC, the gap between its 3.00% non-redeemable and 2.15% cashable GIC is $85 on the same $10,000.

A savings account may do the job better. On October 22, 2026, every cashable GIC rate we confirmed was below the 2.85% everyday rate at the best no-fee savings account, which also lets you withdraw any day. The one possible exception is Hubert's 1-Year Quarterly Term, which pays a 3.75% average with early redemption options, but Hubert's page did not give the redemption terms. Read those terms before you rely on getting money out early.

5. How GIC Interest Is Calculated and Paid

Compound GICs. In a compound GIC, interest is added to the balance each year and paid with your principal at maturity. The value at maturity is the deposit x (1 + rate) to the power of the number of years. At 4.50% for five years, $10,000 earns $450.00 in year one, $470.25 in year two, $491.41 in year three, $513.52 in year four and $536.63 in year five, for $12,461.82 at maturity.

Interest-paying GICs. If you want income, you can take interest yearly, twice a year or monthly, usually at a slightly lower rate. Oaken pays 3.75% on a 1-year GIC with annual interest, 3.70% semi-annual and 3.65% monthly, and 4.50%, 4.45% and 4.40% on a 5-year GIC. Manulife pays 3.35%, 3.30% and 3.25% on a 1-year GIC, and needs $10,000 for monthly interest. Scotiabank needs $5,000 for monthly interest.

Reading the table. The compound option earns $261.82 more over five years than monthly payments ($2,461.82 minus $2,200.00), because interest earns interest and the rate is higher. Monthly payments make sense when you spend the income, for example in retirement. If you would only redeposit the payments into a savings account, the compound option usually wins.

Terms under a year. Short-term GICs pay simple interest for the days or months you hold them. A 4-month GIC at 3.20% on $10,000 pays $106.67 ($10,000 x 3.20% x 4 / 12).

The rate is fixed. A GIC rate is set the day you buy and does not change for the term, whatever the Bank of Canada does. That is the main difference from a savings account. To test different amounts and terms, use the GIC calculator above or our compound interest calculator.

6. CDIC and Provincial Deposit Insurance for GICs

What CDIC covers. CDIC covers GICs and term deposits at member banks and trust companies up to $100,000 per insured category at each member institution. The limit includes principal and interest. The categories are deposits in one name, joint deposits, deposits held in trust, RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs, RESPs, RDSPs and FHSAs, each insured separately.

Interest counts, so size the deposit. Because the limit includes interest, the largest deposit that stays fully covered falls as the term gets longer. At Oaken's rates, it is $96,385.54 for one year at 3.75% and $80,245.10 for five years at 4.50%.

TFSA. The 2026 TFSA dollar limit is $7,000, and GIC interest earned inside a TFSA is never taxed. When a TFSA GIC matures, you can buy a new GIC inside the same TFSA with no effect on your room. To move it to another institution, use a direct transfer, which also has no effect on your room. If you are deciding which plan to fill first, see TFSA vs RRSP, and for the rules, our TFSA guide and the TFSA contribution limit page.

RRSP. The 2026 RRSP dollar limit is $33,810. Your own deduction limit is the lesser of 18% of your 2025 earned income and that dollar limit, minus pension adjustments, plus unused room. Contributions for a year are due by the 60th day of the following year; the exact deadline for 2026 contributions has not been published yet. You can contribute to your own RRSP until December 31 of the year you turn 71. Our RRSP contribution limit page has the detail.

RRIF. Each year after the year you set up a RRIF, you must withdraw at least the January 1 value x the prescribed factor: 5.28% at 71, 5.40% at 72 and 6.82% at 80. A $100,000 RRIF at age 72 needs a minimum of $5,400 ($100,000 x 5.40%). A 5-year non-redeemable GIC cannot pay that minimum early, so line up maturities with your withdrawals. See the RRIF minimum withdrawal table and the RRIF calculator.

FHSA. The FHSA gives $8,000 of room a year and $40,000 over your lifetime, and the contribution deadline is December 31, with no 60-day period. The account must close by December 31 of the year of the earliest of its 15th anniversary, your turning 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. Choose a GIC term that matures before you expect to buy. Saven, Hubert and Outlook offer FHSA GICs at their regular rates; see our FHSA guide.

Where to hold them. If you also want to hold investments in the same plan, our rankings of the best TFSA accounts, best RRSP accounts, best RRIF accounts and best FHSA accounts compare the platforms. Each registered plan is also a separate CDIC category with its own $100,000 limit at each member.

8. How to Build a GIC Ladder

What a ladder is. A GIC ladder splits your money across terms, usually one to five years, so that one GIC matures every year. When the shortest one matures, you reinvest it in a new 5-year GIC. After four years, every rung is a 5-year GIC earning a 5-year rate, and one still matures each year.

Why it helps. A ladder gives you access to a fifth of the money every year without breaking a GIC early. It also spreads your reinvestment across five different dates, so no single rate decision sets the return on all of your money.

The arithmetic. First-year interest is $1,062.50 ($192.50 + $212.50 + $215.00 + $217.50 + $225.00), an average rate of 4.25%. Putting all $25,000 in a 1-year GIC at 3.85% would earn $962.50, which is $100 less, and the whole amount would need a new rate a year from now. When rung 1 matures at $5,192.50 ($5,000 x 1.0385), a new 5-year GIC at 4.50%, if that rate were still on offer, would earn $233.66 in its first year.

One provider or several. A ladder at one provider is simpler to manage. Using Oaken's rates for every rung (3.75%, 4.20%, 4.30%, 4.35% and 4.50%), the first-year interest on $25,000 is $1,055.00, an average of 4.22%, only $7.50 less than mixing providers. At a single CDIC member, keep each category's total, with interest, within $100,000.

Ladders inside registered plans. A ladder works the same way in a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF or FHSA. In a RRIF, set rungs to mature before each year's minimum withdrawal. In an FHSA, shorten the ladder so the last rung matures before your expected purchase.

9. Tax on GIC Interest

Interest is fully taxable outside registered plans. GIC interest is taxed as income at your marginal rate, with no lower rate like the ones for dividends or capital gains. At $80,000 of taxable income in Ontario in 2026, the combined marginal rate is 29.65%. Our tax on savings interest table shows the rate at that income in nine provinces.

You pay tax before you get the interest. For a compound GIC longer than a year, you report the interest that builds up each year, on each anniversary of the purchase, even though you receive it only at maturity. Your institution usually reports that accrued interest on a T5 slip each year. You need cash from somewhere else to pay that tax.

The TFSA removes the problem. The same GIC inside a TFSA owes nothing in any year, and you keep all $1,346.27. If you have TFSA room, the highest-rate GIC you buy is a natural fit for it, because GIC interest is taxed at your full marginal rate outside it.

RRSPs and RRIFs defer the tax. Interest inside an RRSP or RRIF is not taxed each year; the whole withdrawal is taxed as income when it comes out. Inside an FHSA, interest is not taxed while it stays in the account.

Whose interest it is. Interest is generally taxed in the hands of the person whose money bought the GIC. A joint GIC does not change that, and money one spouse gives the other to invest can have its interest attributed back to the giver. Your rate is locked once you buy. A rate change after you buy does not affect a GIC you already hold, up or down. That is the trade: if rates fall, your GIC keeps paying the higher rate; if they rise, you wait for maturity to reinvest. A ladder means only part of your money reinvests at any one time.

11. How to Choose and Buy a GIC

Start from the date you need the money. Pick the longest term that ends before you need it. Money for a home purchase in two years belongs in a 2-year or shorter GIC, not a 5-year one at a better rate.

Compare like with like. Compare rates for the same term and the same interest option (compound, annual, semi-annual or monthly). A 4.40% monthly-pay rate and a 4.50% compound rate from the same provider are both correct; they are different products.

Check the insurer, the limit and the minimum. CDIC for banks and trust companies, DICO for Saven and the Deposit Guarantee Corporation of Manitoba for Hubert, Outlook and Achieva. Minimums run from $500 at Alterna Bank, Scotiabank, CIBC and National Bank to $1,000 at Oaken, Saven, Outlook and Achieva and $2,500 at Manulife Bank.

Read the redemption rules. If there is any chance you need the money early, find out exactly what a cashable or redeemable GIC lets you do and what it pays if you redeem. If there is no chance, a non-redeemable GIC pays more.

Open, fund and set maturity instructions. At an online bank or credit-union division you open an account (and, at Saven, a membership), fund it from your chequing account, then buy the GIC. Ask what happens at maturity if you give no instructions, and set your choice when you buy: renew for a new term, move to savings or pay out to chequing.

How we sort this page. GIC lists on WealthScale are sorted by published rate, not by a score. Our rating methodology covers scored rankings, our editorial standards explain how we check figures, and how WealthScale makes money explains our business model.

12. Common GIC Mistakes

Accepting the posted rate without comparing. RBC's posted 5-year rate of 3.55% earns $556.24 less on $10,000 over five years than the best 4.50%. Fix: compare the table on this page with your bank's posted rate and its specials before you renew.

Locking emergency money in a non-redeemable GIC. If you need it early, you may not be able to get it out at all. Fix: keep your emergency fund in a savings account and put only money with a known date into GICs.

Going over the insurance limit by maturity. A $100,000 5-year GIC at 4.50% grows to $124,618.19, and CDIC covers $100,000 of it. Fix: size each deposit with the table in section 6, or split it across members or categories.

Comparing a stepped GIC by its top rate. Fix: compare its effective annual yield, which for TD's 5-Year Stepper GIC was 0.8800%.

Being surprised by tax on interest you have not received. Fix: hold compound GICs in a TFSA where you can, and set aside cash each year for the tax on accrued interest in a taxable account.

Letting a GIC renew without checking the new rate. Fix: put the maturity date in your calendar and compare rates two weeks before it.

Breaking TFSA rules when you move a matured GIC. Withdrawing TFSA money and redepositing it at another institution in the same year can create an over-contribution taxed at 1% a month. Fix: use a direct transfer; our guide to transferring a TFSA or RRSP explains the steps.

What you need

  • The amount you can set aside for the full term
  • The date you will need the money, to pick the term
  • An account at the issuer (and Saven membership, if you use Saven)
  • Your Social Insurance Number, for interest reporting
  • Government photo ID to verify your identity
  • A linked chequing account to fund the GIC and receive interest
  • Unused room, if you buy the GIC inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA
  • Your maturity instructions: renew, move to savings or pay out

What you get

  • A rate fixed for the whole term, up to 4.50% for five years on October 22, 2026
  • Your principal back at maturity, plus interest
  • CDIC cover of $100,000 per category at banks and trust companies, including interest
  • Provincial insurance at credit unions, as published by each one
  • A choice of compound interest or regular payments
  • A T5 slip each year for interest outside registered plans
  • The option to hold the same GIC in a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF or FHSA
  • No market risk to your principal, unlike stocks, bonds or funds

How It Works: Six Steps to Buying a GIC

  1. Set the date and the amount. Write down when you need the money and how much you can lock away until then.
  2. Choose the account type. Use your TFSA first if you have room; otherwise an RRSP, FHSA or non-registered account, depending on your goal.
  3. Compare rates for that term. Use the best-rate table and the full table above, and check the insurer, minimum and redemption rules in each row.
  4. Size the deposit for insurance. Keep each category at each member within $100,000 at maturity, interest included.
  5. Open the account and buy the GIC. Fund it from your chequing account, choose compound or regular interest, and set maturity instructions.
  6. Diarize the maturity date. Compare rates again two weeks before maturity and decide whether to renew, ladder or move the money.

10 GIC Strategies to Earn More With Less Risk

1. Match the term to the goal

Buy the term that ends just before you need the money. A higher 5-year rate does not help if you have to break the GIC in year two, and many GICs on this page cannot be broken at all.

2. Put your best GIC in your TFSA

At 29.65%, tax takes $399.18 of the $1,346.27 earned on $10,000 over three years at 4.30%. In a TFSA you keep it all, so your highest-rate GICs are the ones to shelter first.

3. Roll a ladder every year

Each year, reinvest the maturing rung in a new 5-year GIC. You earn 5-year rates on most of the money while still freeing a fifth of it every year.

4. Split across insurers above the limit

For $150,000 over three years at 4.30%, keep no more than $88,134.72 at one CDIC member in one category and put the rest with a second member or a credit union with higher published coverage.

5. Use your CDIC categories

A single-name GIC, a joint GIC, a TFSA GIC and an RRSP GIC at the same member are four separate $100,000 categories. You can keep a large total at one institution and stay fully covered.

6. Choose compound interest unless you need income

On Oaken's 5-year GIC, compound interest earns $2,461.82 on $10,000, against $2,200.00 from monthly payments. Take payments only if you will spend them.

7. Look at odd terms

Oaken's 18-month GIC at 3.80% gives most of the 2-year rate with six months less lock-in. Odd terms can fit a known date better than a round number of years.

8. Ask your bank to match

If you prefer to stay with a big bank, bring the best rate for your term to the conversation and ask for its current specials. Specials such as Scotiabank's 2-year GIC at 3.50% are published separately from posted rates.

9. Line up RRIF maturities with minimums

Set one GIC to mature before each year's minimum withdrawal. At age 72, a $100,000 RRIF needs $5,400 out, so a rung of at least that size should mature each year.

10. Keep a fixed and a flexible bucket

Hold your emergency fund in a savings account at a variable rate and money with a known date in GICs at fixed rates. Money you will not need for many years may belong in investments instead; see how to invest in Canada.

Illustrative Example: $150,000 Needed in Three Years

This example is hypothetical. Lena lives in Ontario, has $80,000 of taxable income each year, and has $150,000 she will need for a home renovation in three years. She wants every dollar insured and a fixed rate.

  1. Size the CDIC deposit. At 4.30% for three years, compounded annually, the largest deposit that stays within $100,000 at one CDIC member is $88,134.72 ($100,000 / 1.043^3, rounded down to the cent). She puts $88,000 in Oaken's 3-year GIC at 4.30%.
  2. Place the rest. She puts the other $62,000 in Saven's 3-year GIC, also at 4.30%, which Saven's page states is insured by DICO up to $250,000.
  3. Growth. The Oaken GIC grows to $99,847.13 ($88,000 x 1.043^3) and the Saven GIC to $70,346.85 ($62,000 x 1.043^3, with interest rounded to the cent each year), for $170,193.98 in total and $20,193.98 of interest.
  4. Tax each year. Interest accrues of $6,450.00 in year one, $6,727.35 in year two and $7,016.63 in year three. At 29.65%, the tax is $1,912.43, $1,994.66 and $2,080.43, a total of $5,987.52. Her rate stays at 29.65% because $80,000 plus each year's interest stays in the same federal and Ontario brackets.
  5. After tax. She keeps $14,206.46 of interest ($20,193.98 minus $5,987.52).
  6. Against a savings account. At 2.85% compounded annually, and only if that variable rate never changed, the $150,000 would earn $13,193.99 over three years, $6,999.99 less than the GICs before tax. The GICs fix her rate; the savings account would let her withdraw early.

Are GICs covered by CDIC?

Yes, at CDIC member banks and trust companies. CDIC covers GICs and term deposits up to $100,000 per insured category at each member, and the limit includes interest. At 4.50% for five years, $80,245.10 is the most you can deposit and stay within $100,000 at maturity. Credit union GICs are covered by provincial insurers instead, such as DICO for Saven.

Can I hold a GIC in a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF or FHSA?

Yes. GICs are qualified investments for registered plans, and Saven, Oaken, Hubert, Outlook and Manulife Bank pay the same rates in registered plans as outside them. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, the RRSP dollar limit is $33,810 and the FHSA gives $8,000 of room a year. Each registered plan is a separate CDIC category.

How is GIC interest taxed?

Outside registered plans, GIC interest is fully taxable at your marginal rate. For a compound GIC longer than a year, you report the interest that builds up each year on the anniversary of purchase, even though it is paid only at maturity. At 29.65%, a 3-year GIC of $10,000 at 4.30% owes $127.50, $132.98 and $138.70 in tax over its three years.

What is a GIC ladder?

A GIC ladder splits your money across terms, usually one to five years, so one GIC matures each year. Each maturing GIC is reinvested in a new 5-year term. A $25,000 ladder at the best rates on October 22, 2026 earns $1,062.50 in its first year, an average of 4.25%, against $962.50 if all of it went into a 1-year GIC at 3.85%.

Is a GIC better than a high-interest savings account?

A GIC pays more for locking your money away: 3.85% for one year against 2.85% at the best no-fee savings account on October 22, 2026, or $100 more on $10,000.
