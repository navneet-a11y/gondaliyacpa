2. What Commission-Free Trading Covers, and What It Does Not

The commission is one fee among several. A commission is the broker's charge for placing your order. "Commission-free" removes that charge on online orders for listed stocks and ETFs. It does not remove the cost of turning Canadian dollars into US dollars, the fees exchanges and regulators charge on US trades, the price of options contracts, or the fees for holding and moving an account.

5. Remaining Cost: ECN, Exchange and Regulatory Fees

Fees the broker passes on. Exchanges, electronic communication networks (ECNs) and US regulators charge small fees on some trades. A $0-commission broker can pass these on to you, and they appear on your trade confirmation. They are small on most trades, but they are not zero.

Questrade. Its direct-routing ECN fee of US$0.003 to US$0.004 a share comes to US$3 to US$4 on 1,000 shares. The SEC fee on US sales is 0.0000206 times the sale value, which is $0.206 on a $10,000 sale.

Wealthsimple. Pass-through exchange, ECN, TAF or ORF fees may apply to some US-listed orders and are shown on trade confirmations. The TAF is FINRA's trading activity fee, and the ORF is the options regulatory fee.

Webull. Regulatory and exchange fees may apply to Canadian trades. On US trades, Webull passes on an ADR custody fee of US$0.01 to US$0.03 a share (US$1 to US$3 on 100 ADR shares), FINRA's trading activity fee of $0.000195 a share (US$0.195 on 1,000 shares, capped at $9.79) and the SEC fee on sales. ADRs are US-listed shares of foreign companies, so the custody fee applies only if you hold them.

Qtrade, National Bank, BMO and Desjardins. Qtrade charges an SEC-based fee on US sales. National Bank, BMO and Desjardins do not list pass-through fees on the pricing pages we checked, so we cannot say whether or how they apply.

How to limit them. The SEC fee applies only to sales of US securities, so a buy-and-hold investor pays it rarely. Please read your first few trade confirmations to see which fees your trading attracts.

14. Common Mistakes With Free Stock Trading in Canada

Treating "free" as free. Free stock trading in Canada means $0 commissions, not $0 costs. On $10,000 of US purchases, a 1.5% conversion fee costs $150, more than 15 trades at a $9.99 broker.

Trading more because it is free. A $0 commission removes a brake on frequent trading. In a TFSA, the Canada Revenue Agency can treat frequent trading as carrying on a business, and that income may then be taxed. Please keep a TFSA for investing rather than day trading.

8 Strategies to Keep Commission-Free Trading Cheap

1. Price conversion first

If you buy US shares, compare conversion costs before commissions. A 0.2-point difference in the conversion rate on $20,000 of conversions is $40 a year ($20,000 x 0.2%), and it repeats every year you keep converting.

2. Hold USD where it costs nothing

Questrade holds USD in registered accounts at no cost, and National Bank and Desjardins list no USD fee. Converting once and keeping proceeds in USD avoids paying the fee on every round trip.

3. Convert in larger lots

Where tiers exist, one larger conversion costs less per dollar. At BMO, 0.9% from US$25,000 replaces 1.6%. Inside a Wealthsimple USD account, a single conversion of $25,000 or more costs 0.5%.

4. Avoid direct routing at Questrade

Questrade charges ECN fees on US orders only when they are direct-routed. Unless you have a reason to pick the venue, letting the order route normally avoids the fee.

5. Turn on DRIP where it is $0

Questrade reinvests dividends with no fee, fractionally when fractional shares are on. National Bank reinvests full shares on request with zero commission. Reinvesting keeps cash from sitting at 0%.

6. Keep fewer accounts at Webull

Webull charges $0.70 a month per account. If you do not need a margin or cash account alongside your TFSA, closing it saves $8.40 a year.

7. Mind options minimums

For one- or two-contract trades, a per-trade minimum matters more than the contract rate. Two contracts cost $6.25 at National Bank and $8.75 at Desjardins, against $1.50 at Qtrade.

8. Ask for the transfer reimbursement

Questrade rebates up to $150 per account, Wealthsimple covers the fee up to $150 per account on $25,000 or more, and Qtrade's offer covers up to $150 per client on $15,000 or more. Keep the statement that shows the fee.

Illustrative Example: The Cost of a $0 US Trade

This example is hypothetical. It uses published prices as of October 22, 2026, ignores exchange-rate movements and taxes, and uses Canadian-dollar amounts throughout.

  1. The trade. Jordan buys $10,000 of a US-listed ETF in a TFSA at a $0-commission broker that charges 1.5% to convert, and sells two years later for $11,000.
  2. Buying. Commission: $0. Conversion to USD: $10,000 x 1.5% = $150.00.
  3. Selling and converting back. Commission: $0. Conversion to CAD: $11,000 x 1.5% = $165.00.
  4. Total cost of the round trip. $150.00 + $165.00 = $315.00, or $315.00 / $10,000 = 3.15% of the amount invested, with $0 in commissions.
  5. Keeping the proceeds in USD. At a broker that holds USD in the TFSA at no cost, Jordan converts once ($150.00) and leaves the $11,000 in US dollars for the next purchase. The second $165.00 is paid only if and when Jordan converts back.
  6. At a per-share broker. At Interactive Brokers, a 200-share purchase and sale cost the US$1 minimum each (200 x US$0.005 = US$1.00), so $2.00 in commissions, plus two conversions at the US$2 minimum, $4.00, counting US-dollar fees at par. Total: $6.00.

The commission-free label saved Jordan nothing that mattered here: the $315.00 came entirely from conversion.
