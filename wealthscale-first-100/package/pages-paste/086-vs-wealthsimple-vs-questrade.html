1. Wealthsimple vs Questrade: Quick Verdict

The deciding difference is US dollars. On Canadian stocks and ETFs the two are identical in price: $0 to buy or sell. On US stocks both charge $0 commission and the same 1.5% to convert Canadian dollars when you trade from a Canadian-dollar account. The split comes from what happens after the conversion. Questrade lets a TFSA, RRSP or other registered account hold US dollars at no extra cost, so you convert once and stay in US dollars. Wealthsimple sells a USD account for $10 plus tax a month on its Core tier (included from $100,000 in assets) and charges less to convert large amounts inside it.

Pick Questrade if you buy US stocks or US-listed ETFs regularly in a registered account, trade Canadian options, or want an advanced trading platform. Holding US dollars in registered accounts with no fee is the main reason it ranks first of 13 for US stocks.

Pick Wealthsimple if you are starting out, invest small amounts, want fractional shares and automatic recurring buys, borrow on margin, or plan to convert $25,000 or more at a time. It also costs nothing to leave, which Questrade cannot match.

How the scores split. They tie on cost (4.44) and on account types (5.0). Questrade wins US investing (4.0 against 3.0) and platform features (4.5 against 4). Wealthsimple wins cash and margin (2.0 against 0.5) and safety (4.0 against 3.5). The weighted totals land 0.025 apart.

2. Wealthsimple Questrade Fees: Commissions Compared

Stocks and ETFs. Both brokers charge $0 commission to buy or sell Canadian-listed and US-listed stocks and ETFs online. That puts both among the seven brokers in our commission-free trading ranking, where Questrade ranks first and Wealthsimple second, both at 4.0 / 5. Neither is free of every cost: the 1.5% conversion fee on US trades is the main cost left at either one (see section 3).

Small fees on US orders. Questrade charges ECN fees only on orders you route directly to a specific market, for example US$0.003 to US$0.004 a share when your order removes liquidity. Fills in its overnight session cost US$0.003 a share. US sales carry the SEC fee of 0.0000206 times the trade value, so selling US$10,000 of stock costs about US$0.21 (US$10,000 x 0.0000206 = US$0.206). At Wealthsimple, pass-through exchange, ECN, TAF or ORF fees may apply to some US-listed orders and are shown on the trade confirmation.

Options. Both charge $0 base and $0 a contract on US equity and ETF options. Questrade also prices Canadian options at CA$0.99 a contract and US index options at US$0.99 a contract from September 28, 2026. Exercising costs $24.95 at Questrade, assignment has no fee, and Questrade charges $45 if it has to close your option on expiry day. Wealthsimple charges US$45 for an early exercise or a do-not-exercise instruction.

Questrade Plus. Questrade's optional Plus subscription costs $19.99 a month and gives CA$0.05 a contract back on US equity options. You would need about 400 contracts a month for the cashback to cover the fee ($19.99 ÷ $0.05 = 399.8 contracts).

3. Currency Conversion and USD Accounts

The same 1.5% on ordinary trades. When you buy a US stock from a Canadian-dollar account, both brokers convert your money at a 1.5% fee. At Wealthsimple the fee is the same on Core, Premium and Generation, and it applies on top of Wealthsimple's corporate exchange rate. Sell that stock in the same Canadian-dollar account and the proceeds are converted back, so a round trip pays the fee twice. In our lowest currency conversion fees list, Wealthsimple ranks third and Questrade fourth of the nine brokers that publish a rate, each at $150 on a $10,000 conversion.

Questrade: hold US dollars with no account fee. Questrade's registered accounts are dual-currency at no extra cost, with no forced conversions on US stocks and ETFs. You convert once at 1.5%, buy US stocks with the US dollars, and US dividends and sale proceeds stay in US dollars until you choose to convert them.

Wealthsimple: a USD account with tiered conversion. Wealthsimple's USD account costs Core clients $10 plus tax a month ($120 a year before tax) after a 30-day free trial, with no pro-rated refund if you cancel; Premium and Generation clients get it included. Inside the USD account, each conversion is priced by its own size: 1.5% under $10,000, 1.0% from $10,000 to $24,999.99, 0.5% from $25,000 to $99,999.99 and 0% from $100,000. Trading US-listed securities from the USD account carries no conversion fee.

8. Safety: CIRO and CIPF

Wealthsimple. Wealthsimple Investments Inc. is an investment dealer and a dealer member of the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO), and it is on the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) member list. Wealthsimple states its CIRO membership, and CIRO lists it among the dealers it regulates. That earns 4.0 on our safety factor.

Questrade. Questrade, Inc. is on CIPF's current member list. CIPF member firms are CIRO members, so its CIRO membership follows from that listing, but none of the Questrade pages we checked quoted a CIRO statement. Under our rubric that scores 3.5. Neither firm gets the extra point our rubric gives to a broker owned by a bank or a financial cooperative group.

What CIPF covers. CIPF protects cash and securities held at a member firm if the firm becomes insolvent. It does not cover market losses, unsuitable advice, an issuer's insolvency or crypto assets. Limits are per client, by category:

One possible split. Wealthsimple vs Questrade does not have to be either-or. Moving from Questrade to Wealthsimple. Questrade charges $150 for a full or partial transfer out. Wealthsimple covers the other firm's transfer-out fee, up to $150 per account, on transfers of $25,000 or more, and the account must stay funded for 90 days. Moving from Wealthsimple to Questrade. Wealthsimple charges $0 to transfer out. Questrade rebates the other firm's fee up to $150 per account, which matters only when you move from a broker that charges one. Sell any fractional shares first, because they cannot be transferred.

  1. Open the same account type at the new broker: a TFSA for a TFSA, an RRSP for an RRSP.
  2. Decide whether to move in kind or in cash, and sell fractional shares at Wealthsimple if you are leaving it.
  3. Request a direct transfer from the new broker; never withdraw from a TFSA or RRSP yourself.
  4. Check the value you are moving against the $25,000 threshold for Wealthsimple's fee coverage.
  5. Confirm the old firm's fee and whether the new one covers it.
  6. Keep the account funded for the required period so any rebate or bonus is not clawed back.

Why the direct transfer matters. A direct TFSA transfer does not affect your contribution room. If you withdraw and redeposit yourself, the redeposit uses new room and the withdrawal is only added back on January 1 of the next year, which can cause an over-contribution.
