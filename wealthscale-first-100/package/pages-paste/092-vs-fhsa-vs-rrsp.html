FHSA vs RRSP Home Buyers' Plan

FHSA vs RRSP comes down to one difference for a first home: both give you a tax deduction when you contribute, but money taken from an FHSA for a qualifying home is tax-free and never repaid, while money taken from an RRSP under the Home Buyers' Plan has to be paid back over 15 years. The FHSA takes $8,000 a year up to $40,000 over your lifetime; the HBP lets you withdraw up to $60,000 of RRSP savings. You can use both for the same home, and a couple who both qualify can put up to $200,000 toward it, plus FHSA growth. Here is how to choose and how to combine them.

At a glance

The short answer. For new savings toward a first home, the FHSA usually comes first: it gives the same kind of deduction as an RRSP and the home withdrawal never has to be repaid. The RRSP Home Buyers' Plan is the way to use RRSP savings you already have. Many buyers use both for the same home.

What they share. Both give a deduction for contributions, both let a first-time buyer take money out for a qualifying home without tax, and both need a written agreement to buy or build and a purchase completed before October 1 of the year after the withdrawal.

The big difference. FHSA money used for a qualifying home is gone for good, with nothing to repay. HBP money must go back into your RRSPs at no less than 1/15 a year for up to 15 years, and any shortfall is added to your income.

Limits. The FHSA gives $8,000 of room a year from the year you open it, up to $40,000 over your lifetime, with at most $16,000 in one year. The HBP limit is $60,000 per person. RRSP room for 2026 is 18% of 2025 earned income up to $33,810.

Couples. Two partners who both qualify can use $80,000 of FHSA contributions and $120,000 of HBP withdrawals for the same home: $200,000 in total, plus FHSA growth.

If you never buy. An FHSA can be moved to an RRSP or RRIF tax-deferred without using any RRSP room. RRSP money simply stays invested for retirement.

1. The Short Answer: FHSA or HBP?

For new savings, the FHSA usually wins. A dollar you put in an FHSA gets the same kind of deduction as a dollar you put in an RRSP. The difference shows up when you buy: a qualifying FHSA withdrawal is tax-free and final, while an HBP withdrawal is a loan from your RRSP that you repay over up to 15 years. Same deduction, no repayment, is a hard combination to beat.

For money already in an RRSP, the HBP is the tool. If you have RRSP savings today, the Home Buyers' Plan is how you use up to $60,000 of them for a first home without paying tax on the withdrawal. You cannot move that money into an FHSA faster than FHSA room allows, so for most buyers with existing RRSP savings, the HBP stays part of the plan.

The best answer is often both. The rules allow an HBP withdrawal and a qualifying FHSA withdrawal for the same home, as long as you meet the conditions of each at the time of each withdrawal. Section 10 covers how to combine them, and our Home Buyers' Plan guide covers the HBP in full detail.

When the RRSP route makes more sense. The RRSP can be the better place for extra savings once your FHSA room for the year is used, or when you need more than the FHSA can hold by your purchase date: room builds at only $8,000 a year from the year you open it. The RRSP is also the only one of the two you can keep contributing to for retirement if you are not a first-time buyer.

When neither fits. If you lived in a home that you or your spouse owned during the current year or the previous four calendar years, you fail the HBP's first-time buyer test and cannot open an FHSA. In that case a TFSA is the usual flexible alternative; our FHSA vs TFSA comparison and TFSA vs RRSP comparison explain the trade-offs.

2. How the FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan Work

The FHSA in brief. A First Home Savings Account can be opened by a Canadian resident aged 18 or older (19 where that is the age of majority) who is 71 or younger at December 31 of the opening year and is a first-time home buyer. Room starts only when you open your first FHSA: $8,000 a year, with up to $8,000 of unused room carried forward, and $40,000 over your lifetime. Contributions are deductible, and a qualifying withdrawal for a first home is tax-free. Our FHSA guide has every rule.

The RRSP in brief. Your RRSP deduction limit for 2026 is 18% of your 2025 earned income, up to $33,810, less any pension adjustment, plus unused room from earlier years. The 2027 dollar limit has been published at $35,390. Contributions for a tax year can be made until the 60th day of the next year. Withdrawals are normally added to income and have tax withheld at source. The RRSP guide explains the account, and the RRSP contribution limit page shows how room is calculated.

The Home Buyers' Plan in brief. The HBP lets a first-time buyer withdraw up to $60,000 from their RRSPs for a qualifying home without tax on the withdrawal. You need a written agreement to buy or build, must intend to live in the home within one year, must be resident in Canada at the withdrawal, and must acquire or build the home before October 1 of the year after the first withdrawal. You then repay at least 1/15 of the balance a year.

The structural difference. The FHSA is built as deduction in, tax-free out. The RRSP is deduction in, taxable out, and the HBP is a temporary exception that lets you borrow your own RRSP money tax-free as long as you put it back. Once repaid, that money is taxed like any other RRSP money when you finally withdraw it, usually in retirement.

The 89-day point. RRSP contributions made in the 89 days before an HBP withdrawal may not be deductible, so last-minute RRSP top-ups before a purchase can lose their refund. The FHSA has a different timing rule to remember: there is no 60-day period, so contributions made in January and February count for the current year only.

Where they split: tax later. The FHSA money that goes into your home is never taxed. The RRSP money you take out under the HBP is repaid into your RRSP, where it is taxed when you eventually withdraw it. So the FHSA deduction is a permanent saving if you buy, while the RRSP deduction is a deferral that is settled later at whatever your tax rate is then.

Timing the deduction. Both accounts let you carry an undeducted contribution forward and claim it in a later year. That matters if your income is about to rise. In Ontario, an $8,000 deduction saves $1,674 at $50,000 of taxable income but $2,372 at $90,000, so a buyer expecting a raise can contribute now and deduct later for $698 more ($2,372 - $1,674). The FHSA deduction can be carried forward even after the account is closed.

4. Repayment vs No Repayment

The FHSA asks for nothing back. Once you make a qualifying FHSA withdrawal, the money is yours for the home. The account must then be closed by December 31 of the year after your first qualifying withdrawal, and nothing is owed.

The HBP asks for 1/15 a year. Each year's minimum HBP repayment is your remaining balance divided by the years left, starting at 1/15. Repayment normally starts in the second year after the year of your first withdrawal. For first withdrawals made from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2025, it starts in the fifth year after, and the extension of that relief to first withdrawals made from January 1, 2026 to December 31, 2028 was announced and is confirmed in the Canada Revenue Agency's main Home Buyers' Plan guidance.

What a missed payment costs. If you repay less than the minimum, the shortfall is added to your income for that year on line 12900 and taxed at your marginal rate. For Jordan in the illustrative example below, with $88,000 of taxable income in Ontario, missing his $2,298.21 minimum entirely would add $681.42 of tax at 2026 rates ($2,298.21 x 29.65%).

The cash-flow squeeze. Repayments usually land in the early years of a mortgage, when budgets are tight. A $333.33 monthly HBP cost on a $60,000 withdrawal sits on top of mortgage, property tax and upkeep. The fifth-year start for first withdrawals made from 2022 to 2028 eases that, but only by delaying it.

The case for repayment. Repaying is not wasted money: it rebuilds your retirement savings, which then grow tax-deferred again. Think of the HBP as forced saving with a tax penalty for skipping. The FHSA simply gives you the home money without rebuilding anything, so your retirement savings start from where your RRSP was.

5. Flexibility and Withdrawals

FHSA money used for something else is taxed. You can withdraw from an FHSA for a purpose other than a qualifying home, but that withdrawal is added to your income for the year. The FHSA is flexible about what happens if you do not buy (section 8), not about spending the money on other things.

FHSA to RRSP or RRIF. A direct transfer from an FHSA to an RRSP or RRIF is tax-deferred and does not use any of your RRSP deduction room, provided you have no excess FHSA amount. Only direct transfers qualify, so the money should move institution to institution rather than through your bank account.

RRSP to FHSA. You can also transfer from an RRSP into an FHSA. The transfer counts toward your FHSA limits, including the $40,000 lifetime cap, and it is not deductible because the money was already deducted when it went into the RRSP. What it does is turn RRSP money you would otherwise take out under the HBP, and later repay, into FHSA money you never repay. The cost is FHSA room that you could have filled with new, deductible cash.

RRSP withdrawals outside the HBP. A regular RRSP withdrawal is added to your income and the issuer withholds tax: 10% on $5,000 or less, 20% on amounts over $5,000 up to $15,000, and 30% above that, outside Quebec. Quebec residents have federal tax withheld at 5%, 10% or 15% plus Quebec tax.

The HBP's withdrawal window. HBP withdrawals have to happen in the calendar year of your first withdrawal or in January of the next year, each with its own Form T1036. The FHSA has no such window, but your FHSA must close by December 31 of the year after your first qualifying withdrawal.

6. Effect on Benefits

Both deductions lower your net income. An FHSA or RRSP deduction reduces the net income on your return, and family benefits such as the Canada Child Benefit (CCB) are reduced as adjusted family net income rises. A deduction can therefore increase those benefits on top of the tax refund.

How much the CCB can rise. For July 2026 to June 2027, the CCB is reduced by a percentage of adjusted family net income above $38,237, and by a lower add-on percentage above $82,847. The groceries and essentials benefit. The GST/HST credit was replaced by the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit from the July 2026 payment. Withdrawals for the home do not add income. A qualifying FHSA withdrawal is tax-free and an HBP withdrawal is not taxed when you take it, so neither raises your income in the year you buy. The exception is a missed HBP repayment: the shortfall is added to income on line 12900, which can trim income-tested benefits in that year.

Who gains most. Families with children and incomes in the lower CCB band get the largest benefit boost per deduction dollar. For them, an $8,000 deduction can be worth the tax refund plus more than $1,000 a year in extra CCB with two or more children.

7. Limits and Room

FHSA room. FHSA participation room starts only in the year you open your first FHSA: $8,000 that year and $8,000 each year after, subject to the $40,000 lifetime limit. Unused room carries forward, but only up to $8,000, so the most you can contribute in a single year is $16,000. Contributions count for the calendar year they are made in, with a December 31 deadline.

The HBP limit is a withdrawal cap. The $60,000 HBP limit is not contribution room. It caps how much of your existing RRSP savings you can take out tax-free for a first home. Your RRSP can hold far more; anything above $60,000 stays invested.

Over-contributions. Excess FHSA amounts are taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess in each month until removed. RRSP excess contributions are taxed at 1% a month too, but only on the amount above your limit plus a $2,000 cushion, available if you were 18 or older at any time in the previous year.

8. If You Never Buy a Home

The FHSA has a clean exit. If you decide not to buy, you can transfer your FHSA to an RRSP or RRIF tax-deferred without using any RRSP room, or withdraw it as taxable income. Either has to happen before your maximum participation period ends. Anything still in the account after that loses its FHSA status, and its fair market value is added to your income for that year.

When the period ends. The maximum participation period ends on December 31 of the year of the earliest of three events: the 15th anniversary of opening your first FHSA, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. Someone who opens their first FHSA in 2026 at age 30 has until December 31, 2041 if they never buy.

What the transfer is worth. Say you contribute $8,000 a year for five years and the account grows to $43,747.28 at an assumed 3% return. Moving it to your RRSP keeps all five deductions you already claimed and leaves your RRSP room untouched. In effect, the FHSA gave you $40,000 of extra RRSP-style room. That is why an FHSA is a low-regret choice for anyone who qualifies, even if buying is uncertain.

Deductions survive. If you contributed but did not deduct, you can still claim the FHSA deduction in a later year, even after the account is closed.

The RRSP route has no exit to plan. RRSP money you saved for a home but never used simply stays invested for retirement. You give up nothing, but you also never got the FHSA's tax-free home withdrawal, and every dollar is taxed when it eventually comes out.

10. FHSA and RRSP Together: Using Both for One Home

The rule. You can make a qualifying FHSA withdrawal and an HBP withdrawal for the same qualifying home if you meet all the conditions of each at the time of each withdrawal. Using one does not reduce the limit of the other.

While you save. A common order is to put up to $8,000 a year into the FHSA first, then add any further home savings to an RRSP (which you can later tap through the HBP) or a TFSA. Opening the FHSA early matters, because its room only starts in the year you open it.

When you buy. Taking both withdrawals in the same calendar year keeps the two October 1 deadlines aligned. The FHSA side uses Form RC725 and must close by December 31 of the following year. The HBP side uses Form T1036 for each withdrawal and starts its own repayment clock.

Watch the RRSP dates. If you top up your RRSP before the purchase, leave at least 90 days between that contribution and the HBP withdrawal, because contributions made in the 89 days before may not be deductible. That is an RRSP rule; on the FHSA side, the timing point to remember is that contributions count only for the calendar year they are made in.

Short on cash, long on RRSP. If your savings are mostly in an RRSP and you have little new cash, a direct RRSP-to-FHSA transfer within your FHSA room can move part of that money to the side that is never repaid. You get no new deduction on the transfer, and it uses FHSA room, so it suits people who could not fill that room with cash anyway.

Why the FHSA usually leads. For someone who qualifies, the FHSA beats the RRSP on the home purchase (no repayment) and matches it on the deduction. If you never buy, it turns into RRSP savings without using RRSP room. That combination is why it tends to come first in any order built on the rules.

Where the RRSP keeps its place. The RRSP has far more room than the FHSA's $8,000 a year, so higher earners saving more than that for a home, or saving for retirement at the same time, keep using it. Its HBP feature then gives them access to up to $60,000 for the purchase.

Where the TFSA fits. The TFSA gives no deduction, but every withdrawal is tax-free for any purpose and the room comes back on January 1 of the next year. For money that might go to a home, a car or an emergency, it is the flexible layer on top of the FHSA.

12. Common Mistakes With the FHSA and the HBP

Waiting to open the FHSA. FHSA room only starts in the year you open your first account. Someone who opens in 2028 instead of 2026 loses two years of room build-up and cannot reach the $40,000 lifetime limit until 2032.

Expecting a 60-day FHSA deadline. FHSA contributions made in January or February count for the current year, not the previous one. Only the RRSP has the 60-day period.

Over-counting carried-forward room. Unused FHSA room carries forward only up to $8,000. A person who skipped two full years can still add only $16,000 in the third year, not $24,000, and any excess is taxed at 1% a month.

Using the HBP while FHSA room sits empty. Every new dollar that goes to the RRSP instead of unused FHSA room is a dollar you will have to repay if you use it for the home.

Topping up the RRSP right before the HBP withdrawal. Contributions made in the 89 days before the withdrawal may not be deductible, which can cost the refund on that money.

Letting the FHSA run past its end date. Property left in an FHSA after the maximum participation period is taxed at its fair market value. Please move it to an RRSP or RRIF, or withdraw it, before December 31 of the final year.

Taking a regular RRSP withdrawal instead of the HBP. Without Form T1036, an RRSP withdrawal for a home is taxable, with 10% to 30% withheld outside Quebec.

10 FHSA and HBP Strategies for First-Time Buyers

1. Open the FHSA as soon as you qualify

FHSA room does not build before you open your first account. Opening early starts the $8,000-a-year clock, so the full $40,000 is reachable sooner. Keep in mind that the 15-year maximum participation period also starts on opening.

2. Deduct in your highest-income year

FHSA and RRSP deductions can both be carried forward. If your income is set to rise, contributing now and claiming the deduction later can raise the refund. In Ontario, $8,000 saves $1,674 at $50,000 of income but $2,372 at $90,000.

3. Move RRSP money to the FHSA when cash is short

A direct RRSP-to-FHSA transfer gives no new deduction, but it shifts money from the HBP side, which you repay, to the FHSA side, which you do not. It fits buyers whose savings sit in an RRSP and who could not fill FHSA room with cash.

4. Keep the HBP for RRSP savings you already have

RRSP savings already earned their deduction. The HBP lets you use up to $60,000 of them for the home without tax, while new money goes to the FHSA.

5. Turn refunds into next year's FHSA contribution

The refund from this year's FHSA deduction can fund part of next year's contribution, which earns another deduction. Over five years this keeps the account growing with money the tax system handed back.

6. Make sure both partners open FHSAs

Each partner has their own $8,000 a year and $40,000 lifetime FHSA room and their own $60,000 HBP limit. A couple where only one partner opens an FHSA leaves up to $40,000 of tax-free, never-repaid room unused.

7. Know the 2028 deferral cut-off

Under current rules, a first HBP withdrawal made by December 31, 2028 starts repaying in the fifth year after, while a 2029 first withdrawal starts in the second year after. If closing falls near that date, the year of the first withdrawal matters.

8. Keep the FHSA even if buying is uncertain

An FHSA you never use for a home can move to an RRSP or RRIF tax-deferred without using RRSP room. The deductions you claimed stay claimed, so the account still did its job.

9. Use deductions to protect family benefits

FHSA and RRSP deductions lower net income, which can raise the Canada Child Benefit. With two children and family income between $38,237 and $82,847, an $8,000 deduction raises the yearly CCB by $1,080 for July 2026 to June 2027.

10. Budget the HBP repayment before you buy

A $60,000 HBP withdrawal means at least $4,000 a year, or $333.33 a month, on top of your mortgage. Building that into your budget before you choose a price range avoids a taxable shortfall later.

Illustrative Example: The Same Savings in an FHSA or an RRSP

This example is hypothetical. Tax figures use 2026 Ontario and federal rates for every year, and the 3% return is an assumption, not a forecast.

  1. The saver. Jordan lives in Ontario, has $88,000 of taxable income and has never owned a home. He can save $8,000 every January from 2026 to 2029 and plans to buy in 2030.
  2. Path A, FHSA. He contributes $8,000 a year to an FHSA, $32,000 in total. At 3%, the account holds $34,473.09 by January 2030 ($8,000 x (1.03^4 + 1.03^3 + 1.03^2 + 1.03)). Each deduction saves $2,372, or $9,488 over four years ($2,372 x 4). He withdraws $34,473.09 tax-free for the home and owes nothing back. The FHSA closes by December 31, 2031.
  3. Path B, RRSP and HBP. He puts the same $8,000 a year into an RRSP instead. The balance and the tax saved are identical: $34,473.09 and $9,488. He withdraws the $34,473.09 under the HBP in 2030. That first withdrawal falls after the extended deferral window, so under current rules repayment starts in 2032. His minimum is $2,298.21 a year ($34,473.09 / 15) through 2046.
  4. The difference. Both paths fund the same down payment and give the same refunds. Path B adds 15 years of repayments totalling $34,473.09, and if he skips the 2032 payment entirely, $2,298.21 is added to his income and costs $681.42 at a 29.65% marginal rate.
  5. Path C, both. If Jordan could save $16,000 a year, he could put $8,000 in each. His deductions of $16,000 a year would save $4,894 a year at 2026 rates, or $19,576 over four years ($4,894 x 4), and he would have $68,946.18 for the home ($34,473.09 x 2), with only the RRSP half to repay.

FHSA vs RRSP FAQ

Is an FHSA better than an RRSP for buying a first home?

For new savings, usually yes. Both give a deduction for contributions, but a qualifying FHSA withdrawal is tax-free and never repaid, while money taken from an RRSP under the Home Buyers' Plan must be repaid over up to 15 years. The RRSP is still useful for savings above the FHSA's $8,000 a year.

Can I use the FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan together?

Yes. You can make a qualifying FHSA withdrawal and an HBP withdrawal for the same qualifying home, as long as you meet the conditions of each program at the time of each withdrawal. Using one does not reduce the limit of the other.

Do I have to repay money I take out of an FHSA?

No. A qualifying FHSA withdrawal for a first home is tax-free and is never repaid. Your FHSA must then be closed by December 31 of the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. Only the Home Buyers' Plan requires repayment. Any FHSA money you do not need for the home can be moved to an RRSP or RRIF before the account closes.

How much do I repay each year under the Home Buyers' Plan?

At least your remaining HBP balance divided by the years left, starting at 1/15. A $60,000 withdrawal means $4,000 a year. Repayment normally starts in the second year after your first withdrawal, or the fifth year for first withdrawals made from 2022 to 2028.

Which gives a bigger tax refund, the FHSA or the RRSP?

Neither. A deduction of the same size saves the same tax in either account in the year you claim it. In Ontario, an $8,000 deduction saves $2,372 at $90,000 of taxable income in 2026. The difference comes later: FHSA money used for a home is never taxed.

What is the most a couple can use from FHSAs and the HBP?

If both partners qualify, a couple can use $80,000 of FHSA contributions ($40,000 each) and $120,000 of HBP withdrawals ($60,000 each) for the same home. That is $200,000, plus any growth inside the FHSAs. To reach it, each partner must pass the first-time buyer tests, hold at least $60,000 in RRSPs and make five years of full FHSA contributions.

What happens to my FHSA if I do not buy a home?

You can transfer it to an RRSP or RRIF tax-deferred without using any RRSP room, or withdraw it as taxable income. You must do this before your maximum participation period ends, or the remaining value is added to your income for that year.

Can I transfer money from my RRSP to my FHSA?

Yes. A direct RRSP-to-FHSA transfer is allowed. It counts toward your FHSA limits, including the $40,000 lifetime cap, and it is not deductible. It can move money you would otherwise take out under the HBP into the FHSA, where it never has to be repaid.

Does contributing to an FHSA reduce my RRSP room?

No. The FHSA has its own participation room of $8,000 a year and $40,000 over your lifetime, separate from your RRSP deduction limit. A later transfer from your FHSA to your RRSP also does not use RRSP room. A first-time buyer can contribute to both in the same year and deduct both.

Can I contribute to an FHSA in January or February for last year?

No. The FHSA has a December 31 deadline and no 60-day period. Contributions made in the first 60 days of a year count for that year only. The 60-day period applies to RRSP contributions. If you want an FHSA deduction for a tax year, please contribute by December 31 of that year.

When does HBP repayment start for a 2026 withdrawal?

In 2031. The repayment deferral has been extended to first withdrawals made from January 1, 2026 to December 31, 2028, so repayment starts in the fifth year after. A 2026 first withdrawal therefore has 2031 as its first repayment year and 2045 as its last.

Does an FHSA or RRSP deduction help with the Canada Child Benefit?

It can. Both deductions lower net income, and the CCB is reduced as adjusted family net income rises. For July 2026 to June 2027, an $8,000 deduction raises the yearly CCB by $560 for one child when family income stays between $38,237 and $82,847.
