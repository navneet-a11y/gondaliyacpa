9. Options Risks: You Can Lose the Whole Premium

Buying an option can lose 100% of what you paid. If you buy a call or a put and it expires worthless, the premium is gone, plus the commissions you paid to open it. If you pay $300 in premium for two contracts and they expire out of the money, you lose the $300 and the fees. This is not a rare edge case: an option has a fixed expiry, and the price of the shares has to move far enough, soon enough, for the option to be worth anything at expiry.

Selling an option can lose more than you received. When you write an option you collect a premium, but you take on an obligation. A covered call caps your gain on the shares at the strike price while you still carry the full drop if the shares fall. A secured put can force you to buy shares at the strike price when the market price is lower. An uncovered option can lose far more than the premium received, which is why Wealthsimple, for one, allows it only in a margin account.

Small moves, big swings. An option controls more shares than its price would buy outright, so its value can swing much more, in percentage terms, than the shares underneath it. That works both ways. It also means a few trades can use up a TFSA's contribution room, which does not come back when money is lost.

Assignment and margin calls. If you sell options, you can be assigned at a time you do not choose, and the resulting share purchase or sale can create a debit balance in a margin account. If the value of your account falls below the broker's requirement, it can ask for more cash or sell positions to cover the shortfall.

10. Safety and Regulation: CIRO and CIPF

Who regulates options brokers. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO) oversees all investment dealers in Canada and trading on Canada's debt and equity marketplaces. Every member of the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) is a CIRO member. All 13 brokers on this list are CIPF members or, for National Bank Direct Brokerage and TD Direct Investing, whose own pages do not name the legal entity, operate under a bank-owned dealer on CIPF's member list.

What CIPF covers. If a member firm becomes insolvent, CIPF covers missing cash and securities held for you, up to $1,000,000 for all general accounts combined (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA), a separate $1,000,000 for registered retirement accounts (RRSP, RRIF, LIF) and a separate $1,000,000 for RESPs. It does not cover market losses. An option that expires worthless is a market loss, so CIPF does nothing for it.

13. Common Options Cost Mistakes

Comparing only the per-contract fee. National Bank's $1.25 a contract looks close to Webull's US$0.99, but its $6.25 minimum means a one-contract trade costs $6.25. Always price a whole trade, not one contract.

Forgetting the base commission. At TD, RBC and Scotia, the base fee is most of the cost on small trades: $9.99 of the $11.24 for one contract at TD.

Ignoring currency. A $0 contract fee does not make US options free if each premium is converted at 1.5%. Hold US dollars where your broker allows it and convert in larger, less frequent amounts.

Letting positions drift into expiry. Questrade charges $45 if it has to close an option on expiry day, and Wealthsimple charges US$45 for a do-not-exercise instruction. Decide what to do with each position before the last day.

Assuming active-trader pricing. CIBC, TD, RBC and Scotia need 150 or more trades in a quarter for their lower price. Ten trades a month is 30 a quarter, so a trader at that pace pays the standard rate.

Assuming protection covers losses. CIPF protects property held at a failed member firm. It does not cover an option that expires worthless or a strategy that goes wrong.

10 Ways to Cut Options Trading Costs

These are cost-control habits, not trading strategies. None of them tells you what to buy or sell.

1. Match the pricing model to your trade size

One-contract traders pay least where there is no minimum: Qtrade at $0.75, BMO at $0.90 or a $0 broker. From two contracts up, Interactive Brokers' US$0.65 rate beats those flat fees. Base-commission brokers are the most expensive from two contracts up in our tables; on a single contract, Desjardins' $8.75 minimum costs more than CIBC's $8.20.

2. Price the minimum, not the headline

A $6.25 or $8.75 minimum turns a $1.25 contract fee into a much larger one on small trades. Divide the minimum by the per-contract fee to see how many contracts you must trade before the minimum stops costing you extra: five at National Bank, seven at Desjardins.

3. Add exercise and assignment fees

If you expect to exercise or be assigned, these fees can rival a year of commissions. Questrade charges $24.95 to exercise, National Bank $28.95 and Qtrade $30 for assignment. moomoo charges nothing, and Webull $1 per line item.

4. Close positions before expiry day

Questrade charges a $45 service fee if it closes an option for you on expiry day. Wealthsimple charges US$45 for a do-not-exercise instruction. Deciding a day or two early avoids both.

5. Hold US dollars for US options

Converting each premium at 1.5% adds $15 on every $1,000. Where your account can hold US dollars, convert once and keep premiums and proceeds in US dollars. Questrade's registered accounts hold US dollars at no extra cost.

6. Convert large sums the cheap way

Interactive Brokers converts with no markup and a US$2 minimum. At other brokers, Norbert's Gambit can replace a percentage fee with two stock commissions. Our currency conversion calculator shows the break-even amount.

7. Be honest about active-trader thresholds

The lower prices at CIBC, TD, RBC and Scotia need 150 or more trades in a quarter. Unless you trade at that pace, compare their standard prices, which run from $6.95 to $9.99 plus $1.25 a contract.

8. Keep margin balances small and short

On $10,000 for a year, margin interest is $367.50 at 3.675% and $870.00 at 8.70%. If you must borrow, the margin rate can matter more than the contract fee.

9. Ask the new broker to cover the exit fee

Most brokers charge $150 to transfer out. Questrade rebates up to $150 per account, RBC up to $200 on $15,000 or more, and Wealthsimple up to $150 per account on $25,000 or more.

10. Read your first confirmations

Exchange, clearing and regulatory fees appear on the trade confirmation, not in the headline price. Check the first few after you switch so the real cost matches what you expected.

Illustrative Example: One Active Options Trader at Three Brokers

This example is hypothetical. Mei trades options on US-listed stocks in a non-registered margin account. She makes 10 trades a month of 5 contracts each (120 trades a year), exercises an option 3 times a year, and converts $10,000 from Canadian to US dollars once a year. US-dollar fees are counted at par. Exchange and regulatory fees are left out.

  1. TD Direct Investing. Each trade costs $9.99 + (5 x $1.25) = $16.24. A year of trades costs $16.24 x 120 = $1,948.80. Converting $10,000 at TD's 1.26% illustrative spread costs $10,000 x 1.26% = $126.00. Total: $1,948.80 + $126.00 = $2,074.80, plus exercise fees, which TD does not publish.
  2. Questrade. US equity options cost $0 a contract, so commissions are $0. Three exercises cost 3 x $24.95 = $74.85. The conversion costs $10,000 x 1.5% = $150.00. Total: $74.85 + $150.00 = $224.85.
  3. Interactive Brokers. Each trade costs 5 x US$0.65 = US$3.25. A year costs US$3.25 x 120 = US$390.00. The conversion commission is 0.20 basis points of $10,000, or $0.20, so the US$2.00 minimum applies. Total: $390.00 + $2.00 = $392.00, plus exercise fees, which are not published.
  4. Moving from TD to Questrade. Saving = $2,074.80 - $224.85 = $1,849.95 a year at today's prices.
  5. Now add a margin balance. Suppose Mei also carries a $10,000 CAD margin balance all year. At Questrade's 8.70% that costs $10,000 x 8.70% = $870.00, for a total of $224.85 + $870.00 = $1,094.85. At Interactive Brokers' 3.675% it costs $10,000 x 3.675% = $367.50, for a total of $392.00 + $367.50 = $759.50.
  6. The result. Without borrowing, Questrade costs Mei least of the three. With a $10,000 balance, Interactive Brokers costs $1,094.85 - $759.50 = $335.35 less than Questrade.

Yes, if your broker allows it. Listed options are qualified TFSA investments under Canada Revenue Agency rules. Wealthsimple allows long calls, long puts, secured puts and covered calls in a TFSA. Yes, where the broker allows it. The Canada Revenue Agency says investments permitted in a TFSA are generally the same as those permitted in an RRSP, and listed options are on that list. What are exercise and assignment fees?

They are fees for turning an option into a share trade. Exercising uses your option to buy or sell the shares; assignment happens when an option you sold is exercised against you. Can I lose more than I invest with options?

When you buy a call or put, the most you can lose is the premium you paid plus fees, and you can lose all of it. CIPF covers missing cash and securities if a member broker becomes insolvent, up to $1,000,000 for general accounts combined and a separate $1,000,000 for registered retirement accounts.
