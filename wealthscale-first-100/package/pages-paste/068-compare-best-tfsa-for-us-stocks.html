At a glance

Top pick. Questrade scores 4.0 / 5 for US stocks. Its TFSA is dual-currency at no extra cost, so once you convert, dividends and sale proceeds can stay in US dollars. US stock and ETF trades cost $0, and converting costs 1.5%, or $105.00 on the 2026 TFSA limit of $7,000.

Also 4.0 / 5. Interactive Brokers converts with no markup for a US$2 minimum, so converting $7,000 costs US$2.00. US trades cost at least US$1. Its pages do not say whether its TFSA can hold US dollars, which matters if you want dividends to stay in US dollars.

No-fee USD TFSA accounts. Questrade, National Bank Direct Brokerage, Desjardins, moomoo and TD Easy Trade state that a TFSA can hold US dollars with no fee. Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on its Core tier, and Qtrade charges US$15 a quarter unless you have Investor Plus.

The cost no broker can remove. The United States withholds 15% of US dividends paid into a TFSA when a W-8BEN is on file, and 30% without one. The TFSA is not a retirement arrangement under the Canada-US tax treaty, and the tax cannot be recovered as a credit. On US$50,000 of US holdings yielding 2%, that is US$150 a year (hypothetical).

What you control. How you convert (once, in size, or by Norbert's Gambit), whether US dollars stay in US dollars, and which US holdings sit in the TFSA rather than an RRSP. Low-dividend US holdings lose little to withholding in a TFSA; high-dividend ones lose more.

2. The 15% US Withholding Tax in a TFSA

The rule. The Canada-US tax treaty caps US tax on dividends paid to a Canadian resident at 15% under Article X(2)(b), once your broker has a valid Form W-8BEN on file. The broker withholds the tax before the dividend reaches your TFSA, so a US$100 dividend arrives as US$85.

Why a TFSA pays it. The treaty's Article XXI(2) exempts dividends earned by arrangements operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs qualify. A TFSA does not: it is a general savings account, not a retirement plan, so the 15% is withheld. The same applies to an FHSA and an RESP.

Why it is a permanent cost. In a non-registered account you can claim a foreign tax credit for US tax paid, up to the Canadian tax on that income. Inside a TFSA, Canada does not tax the dividend at all, so there is no Canadian tax to claim the credit against. The 15% is lost for good, every year you hold the investment.

3. W-8BEN, the 30% Rate and Other Withholding Traps

The W-8BEN halves the tax. Form W-8BEN tells the US payer, through your broker as withholding agent, that you are a foreign beneficial owner entitled to the treaty rate. Without it, US-source dividends paid to a foreign person are withheld at 30%. In the table above, that doubles the loss on a 2% yield from US$150 to US$300 a year on US$50,000.

When to file. If your broker did not ask for the form when you opened an account that can trade US securities, file it before your first US dividend. It is a broker form, not a tax-return form, and you file it with the broker, not with the Canada Revenue Agency.

Check your first dividend. Your activity statement usually shows the gross dividend and the tax withheld on separate lines. In a TFSA, the withholding should be 15% of the gross amount. If it is 30%, the broker does not have a valid W-8BEN on file for you.

The same cost in an FHSA and RESP. A first home savings account and an RESP are not retirement arrangements either, so US dividends paid into them also lose 15% with no way to recover it. If you hold US stocks in several registered accounts, only the retirement accounts (an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF) let US-listed holdings avoid the tax.

Canadian-listed funds that hold US shares. When a Canadian-listed fund owns US shares, the fund is the shareholder, and any US tax on those dividends is taken before the fund pays you. Holding that fund in a TFSA does not recover it, and switching to a Canadian-listed fund does not avoid US withholding; it only moves it inside the fund. This is a general rule about structure, not a view on any fund.

What the TFSA still shelters. Capital gains on US holdings, and the 85% of each dividend that the United States does not keep, grow free of Canadian tax inside the TFSA, and withdrawals are not taxed in Canada. The withholding is a drag on income, not on growth.

6. US Dividends in a TFSA: Withholding Plus Conversion

Two cuts, in order. When a US company pays a dividend into your TFSA, the United States takes 15% first. If the TFSA cannot hold US dollars, the remaining 85% then converts to Canadian dollars at your broker's rate. If you later buy more US shares with that cash, it converts again.

A worked figure. This example is hypothetical. A TFSA receives US$600 of US dividends in a year. With a W-8BEN on file, US$90 is withheld (15%), leaving US$510. Counting US dollars at par, converting US$510 at 1.5% costs $7.65; at TD's 1.26% it costs $6.43; at about 1.6% it costs $8.16; and at about 1.70% it costs $8.67. Converting it back to reinvest in US shares at 1.5% costs another $7.65, so the round trip is $15.30 on top of the US$90 withheld.

DRIP in US dollars. A dividend reinvestment plan in a TFSA that holds US dollars buys more of the same US-dollar security, so no conversion happens. Questrade's DRIP has no fee, National Bank and TD reinvest in full shares, and Interactive Brokers charges its standard commission. Wealthsimple applies its conversion fee when US-dollar dividends are reinvested in a non-USD account, so its DRIP avoids conversion only inside a USD TFSA.

RBC's dividend rate. RBC publishes a separate rate for converting dividends and interest: 60 basis points as of March 9, 2026, lower than its 230 basis point spread on conversions under $25,000. No other broker on this list publishes a separate dividend rate.

What to take from this. The withholding is the larger cut and you cannot avoid it in a TFSA. The conversion cut is smaller and you can avoid it, by holding US dollars in a TFSA that allows it and leaving dividends there. 8. US Stocks in a TFSA in Canada: Rules That Still Apply

Who can contribute. You need to be a Canadian resident for tax purposes, 18 or older, with a valid social insurance number. Room starts building in the year you turn 18, or in the year you become a resident if you arrive later. A year in which you are a non-resident adds no new room, and contributions made while non-resident are taxed at 1% a month.

How much you can put in. The 2026 dollar limit is $7,000. Your room is this year's limit, plus unused room from past years, plus anything you withdrew in earlier years, minus what you contribute this year. Withdrawals are added back to your room on January 1 of the next year, not right away. An excess contribution is taxed at 1% a month for as long as it stays in the account.

What you can hold. A TFSA can hold cash, GICs, most securities listed on a designated stock exchange (shares, warrants, options, ETF and REIT units), mutual and segregated funds, and certain bonds. Permitted investments are generally the same as in an RRSP. A non-qualified investment is taxed at 50% of its fair market value, so please check anything unusual, such as shares of a private company, with your broker first.

Options on US stocks. Brokers limit options in registered accounts. Wealthsimple, for example, allows long calls, long puts, cash-secured puts and covered calls in a TFSA, and keeps every other strategy for margin accounts. Check your broker's rules before you plan an options strategy in a TFSA.

Frequent trading. The Canada Revenue Agency says that a TFSA holder who invests with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have the account's income taxed as business income. It gives no trade-count test. Active US trading inside a TFSA, at any broker, carries that risk.

Moving and closing. A direct transfer between institutions does not affect your room. Withdrawing and redepositing yourself counts as a new contribution, and the withdrawn amount comes back as room only on January 1 of the next year. Withdrawals are not taxed in Canada, whether the TFSA held Canadian or US investments. For the full rules, see our guide on TFSA withdrawal rules.

12. Common Mistakes With US Stocks in a TFSA

Expecting the TFSA to shelter US dividends. It shelters them from Canadian tax only. The United States still keeps 15%, and there is no credit to claim.

Leaving the W-8BEN unfiled. Without it, 30% is withheld. On US$1,000 of dividends, that is US$300 instead of US$150.

Converting every dividend back. In a TFSA without a US-dollar side, each dividend converts to Canadian dollars at the broker's rate, and buying US shares again converts it back. Holding US dollars avoids both.

Paying for a USD TFSA you do not need. A $10-a-month USD account on Wealthsimple Core costs $120 a year before tax. A buy-and-hold investor who adds $7,000 a year and never sells may save less than that.

Withdrawing and redepositing to switch brokers. That counts as a new contribution. If your room is used up, the redeposit is an over-contribution taxed at 1% a month until the withdrawn amount comes back as room on January 1. Use a direct transfer.

Trading like a professional. Very frequent trading in a TFSA can lead the Canada Revenue Agency to tax the account's income as business income.

Putting the highest-dividend US holdings in the TFSA by default. If you have RRSP room, those holdings lose nothing to US withholding there. The TFSA is usually the better home for low-dividend holdings.

1. Hold US dollars inside the TFSA

A no-fee US-dollar TFSA at Questrade, National Bank, Desjardins, moomoo or TD Easy Trade lets dividends and sale proceeds stay in US dollars, so you pay to convert only once.

5. Convert once a year, not every month

At a broker with a fixed minimum, fewer conversions mean fewer minimum fees. At a tiered broker, a larger single conversion can fall into a lower band.

Where Canadian ETF trades cost $0 and the TFSA holds US dollars, the gambit replaces a $105.00 to $119.00 conversion on $7,000 with two trades and their spreads.

7. Reinvest dividends in US dollars

A DRIP in a US-dollar TFSA buys more shares without converting. In a Canadian-dollar TFSA, Wealthsimple applies its conversion fee to reinvested US dividends.

Wealthsimple's $120-a-year Core fee pays for itself once you sell and rebuy more than $4,000 of US holdings in a year. Buy-and-hold investors often do better with a no-fee broker.

A direct transfer protects your room, and an in-kind transfer moves US shares without selling and converting them. Ask for the transfer-fee rebate at the new broker.

Frequent, professional-style trading can make TFSA income taxable as business income. Long-term holding keeps the TFSA's tax shelter intact.

Yes, if the TFSA can hold US dollars and your broker processes journal requests. There is no capital gain or loss to report inside a TFSA. At a broker with $0 Canadian ETF trades, the published cost is $0 plus bid-ask spreads. Wealthsimple lists the gambit as available and a $9.95 journaling fee.

Can frequent US trading in a TFSA cause tax problems?

It can. The Canada Revenue Agency says that a TFSA holder who invests with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have the account's income taxed as business income. It gives no fixed number of trades. Occasional buying and selling of US stocks is not the concern; very active trading is.
