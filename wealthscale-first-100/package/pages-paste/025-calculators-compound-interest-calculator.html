1. How to Use the Compound Interest Calculator

Starting amount. Enter what you already have set aside for this goal: a TFSA balance, a GIC, or cash in a savings account. Enter 0 if you are starting from scratch. The calculator treats the starting amount as invested on day one, so it earns a full month of interest in month one.

Monthly contribution. This amount is added at the end of every month, beginning with month one. $500 a month is $6,000 a year. If you save once a year instead, for example a lump sum into your TFSA each January, divide the yearly amount by 12. The result will be a little lower than a January 1 deposit would give, because each monthly slice has less time to grow.

Annual rate. For a savings account or GIC, use the posted annual rate. For investments, use an assumed average return, and please try more than one, because no investment return is guaranteed. The field accepts negative rates so you can test a bad stretch.

Years and compounding. Enter whole years. Then choose how often interest is added to the balance. For a deposit product, match the frequency in its terms. Stocks, bonds and funds have no stated compounding; their gains arrive unevenly, so monthly is a sensible way to model them.

Reading the result. You get the value at the end of the period, the total you contributed, the interest and growth (value minus contributions) and the growth share of the total. The bar chart and the table show the balance at the end of each year, which is useful for checking a milestone such as a down payment date. Change one input at a time to see which one moves the answer most. It is usually time.

2. The Compound Interest Formula, Explained

Simple versus compound. Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. $10,000 at 5% simple interest earns $500 a year, or $5,000 over 10 years, for a total of $15,000. Compound interest adds each payment to the balance, so the next payment is larger. With yearly compounding the same $10,000 reaches $16,288.95, which is $1,288.95 more than simple interest.

The lump-sum formula. A = P x (1 + r/n)^(n x t), where P is the starting amount, r the annual rate as a decimal, n the number of compounding periods a year and t the number of years. For $10,000 at 5% compounded monthly for 10 years: 1 + 0.05/12 = 1.0041667, raised to the power of 120 gives 1.6470095, and $10,000 x 1.6470095 = $16,470.09.

The formula for monthly savings. FV = PMT x [((1 + i)^N - 1) / i], where PMT is the monthly deposit, i the monthly rate and N the number of months. For $500 a month at 5% compounded monthly for 20 years, the bracket works out to 411.0337, so the deposits grow to $205,517 ($500 x 411.0337). Add the $10,000 starting amount, which grows to $27,126 over the same 20 years, and the total is $232,643 ($27,126 + $205,517), the calculator's default result.

The effective monthly rate. The calculator works month by month. It converts your annual rate with the formula (1 + r/n)^(n/12) - 1. At 5%, that gives 0.407412% a month for yearly compounding, 0.416667% for monthly and 0.417507% for daily. Using one monthly engine for every choice keeps monthly contributions consistent while still honouring the compounding frequency you picked.

The effective annual rate. The formula (1 + r/n)^n - 1 shows what a quoted rate really earns over a year. A 5% rate compounded monthly is an effective 5.1162% a year. Use the effective rate to compare products that quote different frequencies.

Contributions at month end. Deposits are added at the end of each month, which is called an ordinary annuity. If you deposit at the start of each month instead, every deposit earns one extra month of interest, so your real result would be slightly higher than the calculator shows.

  1. The calculator converts your annual rate to an effective monthly rate for the compounding you chose.
  2. It grows the starting amount by one month of interest.
  3. It adds your monthly contribution at the end of that month.
  4. It repeats the two steps above for every month, 240 times for a 20-year plan.
  5. It records the balance and the running total of contributions at the end of each year.
  6. It reports the final value, total contributed, and growth as value minus contributions.

What the table shows. More frequent compounding pays interest on interest sooner, so the balance is higher. Moving from yearly to monthly compounding adds $181.14 over 10 years ($16,470.09 - $16,288.95). Moving from monthly to daily adds only $16.56 ($16,486.65 - $16,470.09). The rate matters more than the frequency. A rate just 0.25 percentage points higher, 5.25% compounded only once a year, grows $10,000 to $16,680.96 in 10 years. How to read a product's terms. A deposit agreement usually states two things: how often interest is calculated and how often it is paid or added to your balance. Interest compounds only once it is added to the balance. A GIC that pays interest out to your chequing account each year does not compound at all, because the interest leaves the GIC. A compound GIC keeps the interest inside and grows on it.

Daily compounding in the calculator. The daily option uses 365 compounding periods in every year. At 5%, that works out to 0.417507% a month, only slightly above the 0.416667% of monthly compounding, which is why the monthly and daily rows in the table are so close.

Comparing quotes. When two posted rates are close, frequency can decide it. 4.40% compounded monthly is an effective 4.4898% a year, which beats 4.45% compounded yearly. Convert both quotes to effective annual rates with the formula in section 2 before you decide. 4. Worked Examples: Three Hypothetical Savers

Each example below uses the calculator's method: an effective monthly rate and deposits at the end of each month. The people are invented to show the arithmetic.

This example is hypothetical. Amira, 30, has $10,000 in her TFSA. She adds $500 a month and assumes a 5% annual return, compounded monthly, for 20 years.

  1. Her own money: $10,000 + ($500 x 240 months) = $10,000 + $120,000 = $130,000.
  2. The starting $10,000 grows to $27,126 ($10,000 x 1.0041667 to the power of 240).
  3. The monthly deposits grow to $205,517 ($500 x 411.0337).
  4. Total value: $232,643.24, shown by the calculator as $232,643 ($27,126 + $205,517).
  5. Growth: $232,643.24 - $130,000 = $102,643.24, or 44.1% of the final balance.
  6. Her deposits total $6,000 a year ($500 x 12), which fits under the 2026 TFSA limit of $7,000, so if she has the room, none of that growth is taxed. Our TFSA guide explains how room builds.

This example is hypothetical. Ben starts saving $300 a month at 25. Chloe starts the same $300 a month at 35. Both assume 6% a year compounded monthly and stop at 65.

  1. Ben saves for 40 years: $300 x 480 months = $144,000 of deposits. His balance at 65 is $597,447.22.
  2. Chloe saves for 30 years: $300 x 360 months = $108,000 of deposits. Her balance at 65 is $301,354.51.
  3. Ben put in $36,000 more ($144,000 - $108,000) and ends with $296,092.71 more ($597,447.22 - $301,354.51).
  4. To match Ben's $597,447.22 in 30 years, Chloe would need to save $594.76 a month, almost double his amount.

This example is hypothetical. Dev wants $50,000 for a down payment in five years. He has $5,000 saved and expects 3% a year in a savings account. He uses the savings goal calculator in section 9, which compounds the rate yearly and works month by month (0.246627% a month).

  1. His $5,000 grows by itself to $5,796.37 in 60 months.
  2. The gap his deposits must fill: $50,000 - $5,796.37 = $44,203.63.
  3. The monthly deposit that fills it: $684.47.
  4. His own money: ($684.47 x 60) + $5,000 = $41,068.20 + $5,000 = $46,068.20.
  5. Interest supplies the rest: $50,000 - $46,068.20 = $3,931.80.
  6. His yearly saving is $8,213.64 ($684.47 x 12). As a first-time buyer he could put $8,000 of it each year into a First Home Savings Account, the annual FHSA limit, and the other $213.64 into a TFSA.

What the three examples teach. Amira shows that steady deposits do most of the early work and growth takes over later. Ben and Chloe show that time is the input you cannot buy back. Dev shows that for a short goal at a low rate, almost all of the target comes from your own deposits, so the saving rate matters far more than the interest rate. Our FHSA calculator handles Dev's case with room and deductions included.

6. The Rule of 72

The shortcut. Divide 72 by the annual rate of return to estimate how many years it takes for money to double. At 6%, 72 / 6 = 12 years. The exact answer for yearly compounding is the natural log of 2 divided by the natural log of (1 + rate), which gives 11.90 years at 6%.

Where it is accurate. The estimate is closest between about 6% and 10%. At 8% it is almost exact (9.00 years against 9.01). At very low rates it runs long: at 1% it says 72 years when the true figure is 69.66. At 12% and above it runs a little short.

Using it for fees and inflation. The rule works for anything that compounds. If prices rise 3% a year, money left in cash loses half its buying power in about 24 years (72 / 3). A fee works the same way in reverse: it lowers the rate you double at, so a fund earning 7% before a 2% fee doubles in about 14.4 years (72 / 5) instead of about 10.3 years (72 / 7).

Using it for debt. An unpaid balance growing at 20% a year doubles in about 3.6 years (72 / 20); the exact figure with yearly compounding is 3.80 years. That is why paying off high-interest debt usually comes before investing for growth.

8. Compound Interest in Canada: Tax, TFSAs and RRSPs

Interest outside a registered account. Interest earned in a regular (non-registered) account is added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate every year, whether you spend it or not. Tax paid each year leaves the account, so the balance compounds at the after-tax rate, not the posted rate.

This example is hypothetical. Maya lives in Ontario and has $75,000 of taxable income in 2026. Her combined marginal rate on interest is 29.65% (20.5% federal + 9.15% Ontario). She earns 5% a year, compounded yearly, on $10,000 for 10 years.

  1. In a TFSA, nothing is taxed: $10,000 grows to $16,288.95.
  2. In a non-registered account, her after-tax rate is 5% x (1 - 0.2965) = 3.5175%, and $10,000 grows to $14,129.86.
  3. The TFSA leaves her $2,159.09 more ($16,288.95 - $14,129.86).
  4. In an RRSP, her $10,000 contribution is deductible, grows to $16,288.95 and is taxed when withdrawn. If her rate is still 29.65% then, she keeps $11,459.27 ($16,288.95 x 0.7035).
  5. That matches the TFSA route for the same pre-tax $10,000: $7,035 after tax ($10,000 x 0.7035) grows to $11,459.27 in a TFSA, but only to $9,940.35 in a non-registered account.

Why the RRSP and TFSA tie in that example. When your tax rate is the same when you contribute and when you withdraw, an RRSP and a TFSA give the same after-tax result. An RRSP wins if your rate is lower at withdrawal, and a TFSA wins if it is higher. Our TFSA vs RRSP comparison works through the cases, and the RRSP tax refund calculator shows the deduction for your province.

2026 room that shelters growth. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and someone who has been 18 or older and resident in Canada every year since 2009 and never contributed has $109,000 of room. RRSP room is 18% of last year's earned income, up to $33,810 for 2026, less any pension adjustment. The First Home Savings Account gives $8,000 a year up to $40,000 for life. See the TFSA contribution limit by year and the RRSP contribution limit for the details, or check your own room with the TFSA room calculator.

Dividends and capital gains. Outside a registered account, dividends from Canadian companies are grossed up and then reduced by a dividend tax credit (eligible dividends are grossed up by 38%, with a federal credit of 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount). Only part of a capital gain is taxed, and only when you sell, so growth investments compound with less tax drag than interest. The capital gains tax calculator works out the tax on a sale.

US dividends in a TFSA. The United States withholds 15% of dividends paid to a Canadian resident who has filed a W-8BEN, or 30% without one. In a TFSA that tax cannot be recovered, so it lowers the compounding rate for good. An RRSP is exempt under the Canada-US tax treaty when you hold US-listed securities directly. Read more about US stocks in a TFSA.

10. What Rate to Use, and Where Rates Stand

Savings rates as of October 22, 2026. The Bank of Canada policy rate is 2.25%, unchanged since the cut that took effect on October 30, 2025. Among the accounts in our high-interest savings account table, everyday rates without a monthly plan fee reach 2.85%, and the best non-registered GIC rates in our GIC rate table are 3.85% for one year and 4.50% for five years. Promotional savings rates are higher for a few months and then drop, so model a promo with the regular rate unless you plan to move the money when it ends.

Investment returns. Stocks, bonds and funds have no posted rate. Their returns vary from year to year and can be negative. Run the compound interest calculator with a cautious, a middle and a hopeful assumption, for example 4%, 6% and 8%, and plan around the cautious one. The calculator assumes a steady return every month, which real markets never deliver.

Inflation. The calculator shows future dollars. To see a result in current buying power, subtract an assumed inflation rate from your return. With a 6% return and 2% inflation, run the tool at 4%: $10,000 over 25 years shows $27,137.65 in current dollars instead of $44,649.70 in future dollars. This is an approximation, and both rates here are assumptions.

A reality check. Treat every result from a compound interest calculator in Canada as a planning range, not a promise. Rates change, contributions slip and fees creep in. Re-run your numbers once a year with your actual balance. 10 Ways to Get More From Compound Interest

Start now, even small

Ben and Chloe saved the same $300 a month. Ben's 10-year head start produced $296,092.71 more at 65. Time multiplies every dollar, so a small amount started early can beat a large amount started late. Begin with what you can, then raise it.

Automate the monthly deposit

Set a transfer for the day after payday. The calculator assumes a deposit every month without fail, and automation is the only reliable way to match that assumption. Missed months cost both the deposit and all the growth it would have earned.

Fill registered room first

Growth in a TFSA is never taxed, and growth in an RRSP or FHSA is taxed only when withdrawn, if at all. In our Ontario example the TFSA beat a taxable account by $2,159.09 on $10,000 over 10 years.

Reinvest every payment

Compounding only works if interest, dividends and distributions stay invested. Choose compound GICs over ones that pay interest out, and set fund distributions to reinvest. Cash sitting idle in an investment account earns little or nothing.

Cut your total fee

On $10,000 over 25 years at 6% before fees, a 1% fee costs $9,836.80 and a 2% fee costs $17,512.05. Compare the full cost, including fund expense ratios, before you choose a platform.

Compare effective annual rates

Convert quotes to effective annual rates before choosing. 5% compounded monthly is 5.1162% a year. When posted rates are far apart, the higher one wins. When they are close, compounding can flip the result: 4.40% monthly (4.4898% effective) beats 4.45% yearly.

Collect matching and grants

An employer that matches group RRSP contributions adds to your balance at once. In an RESP, the basic Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% of the first $2,500 you contribute each year, up to $500, before any growth.

Do not break the chain

Withdrawing from a TFSA and putting the money back in the same year uses new room, because withdrawals are added back only on January 1 of the next year. An unplanned redeposit can trigger the 1% a month over-contribution tax.

Raise deposits with your pay

Each time your income goes up, raise the monthly deposit. Moving from $300 to $400 a month for the last 20 years of a 40-year plan adds a full extra stream of compounding without changing your lifestyle much.

Clear high-interest debt first

Compounding works against you on debt. A balance at 20% doubles in about 3.6 years by the rule of 72. Paying it off earns a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate, which few investments can match.

Compound Interest Calculator FAQ

How does this compound interest calculator work?

It converts your annual rate into an effective monthly rate for the compounding you choose, grows your starting amount one month at a time and adds your monthly contribution at the end of each month. It then reports the final value, the total you contributed and the growth. It does not include tax or fees, so use the fee calculator on this page to see what costs take, and a TFSA or RRSP to shelter the growth.

What is the formula for compound interest?

For a lump sum, A = P x (1 + r/n)^(n x t), where P is the starting amount, r the annual rate as a decimal, n the compounding periods a year and t the number of years. For $10,000 at 5% compounded monthly for 10 years, the result is $16,470.09. Monthly deposits use FV = PMT x [((1 + i)^N - 1) / i], where i is the monthly rate and N the number of months.

How much will $10,000 grow to in 10 years at 5%?

$10,000 at 5% grows to $16,288.95 with yearly compounding, $16,386.16 semi-annually, $16,436.19 quarterly, $16,470.09 monthly and $16,486.65 daily. The interest earned is between $6,288.95 and $6,486.65 depending on the compounding. These figures assume no further deposits, no tax and no fees.

Does daily compounding make a big difference?

Not much. On $10,000 at 5% for 10 years, daily compounding beats monthly compounding by $16.56 and yearly compounding by $197.70. A slightly higher rate matters far more: 5.25% compounded yearly grows the same $10,000 to $16,680.96, which is $194.31 more than 5% compounded daily.

What is the rule of 72?

The rule of 72 estimates how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual rate. At 6% that gives 12 years, close to the exact 11.90 years with yearly compounding. It is most accurate between about 6% and 10%. It also works for inflation and debt: a balance growing at 20% a year doubles in about 3.6 years.

Does the calculator include tax?

No. It shows growth before tax. In a TFSA, that is what you keep. In a non-registered account, interest is taxed every year at your marginal rate. For an Ontario resident with $75,000 of taxable income in 2026, that rate is 29.65%, which turns a 5% return into 3.5175% after tax. Please run the calculator at the after-tax rate to model a taxable account.

Is compound interest taxed in a TFSA or RRSP?

Interest and growth inside a TFSA are not taxed, and withdrawals are tax-free. Growth inside an RRSP is not taxed while it stays in the plan, but withdrawals are taxed as income. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and the 2026 RRSP limit is 18% of your prior-year earned income up to $33,810, less any pension adjustment.

How much do fees reduce compound growth?

More than most people expect, because a fee is charged on the whole balance every year. On $10,000 invested for 25 years at a 6% return before fees, a 1% yearly fee costs $9,836.80 and a 2% fee costs $17,512.05 compared with no fee. What rate of return should I use in the calculator?

For a savings account or GIC, use the posted rate. As of October 22, 2026, the best non-registered GIC rates in our table are 3.85% for one year and 4.50% for five years. Investments have no fixed rate, so run a cautious, a middle and a hopeful assumption, such as 4%, 6% and 8%, and plan around the cautious one.
