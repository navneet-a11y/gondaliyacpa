How to buy US stocks in Canada comes down to six steps: choose the account that will hold them, pick a broker with US-dollar accounts, open the account and file a W-8BEN, fund it, get US dollars at the lowest cost, and place your order. The step that costs the most is usually the currency conversion. As of October 22, 2026, converting $10,000 costs $150 at a 1.5% broker and US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers, and Norbert's Gambit can cut the cost on large amounts. This guide walks through every step with real costs, the US withholding rules, and a broker table ranked by our US-stocks score.

1. How to Buy US Stocks in Canada, Step 1: Choose the Account

Why the account comes first. The same US share costs you different amounts in tax depending on where it sits. The United States withholds tax on dividends paid to Canadians, and whether you lose that tax, recover it or avoid it depends on the account. Decide this before you open anything, because it decides which broker features you need.

RRSP, RRIF, LIRA and LIF. Article XXI(2) of the Canada-US tax treaty exempts arrangements operated exclusively to provide retirement benefits. US-listed securities held directly in these accounts have no US tax withheld on dividends once the broker has the account documented. The trade-off is that RRSP withdrawals are taxed as income. The 2026 RRSP dollar limit is $33,810, or 18% of your 2025 earned income if less, adjusted for pension adjustments and unused room.

TFSA, FHSA and RESP. These are not retirement arrangements, so 15% of each US dividend is withheld (30% without a W-8BEN) and it cannot be recovered. Growth and capital gains are still sheltered from Canadian tax. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and FHSA room is $8,000 a year to a $40,000 lifetime limit. Our US stocks in a TFSA guide puts the TFSA cost in dollars.

Non-registered cash or margin account. The 15% is withheld, but you claim it back as a foreign tax credit on your Canadian return. Dividends, interest and realized gains are taxable each year, and capital losses can be used against gains.

3. Step 3: Open the Account and File Your W-8BEN

What the broker will ask for. Expect to give your social insurance number, government photo ID, your address, employment details and a Canadian bank account for deposits. For a registered account, you also confirm your eligibility, such as residency and age for a TFSA, and name a beneficiary or successor holder where the account allows it.

Minimums. Interactive Brokers and Qtrade say there is no minimum to open an account. National Bank Direct Brokerage, RBC Direct Investing and Wealthsimple publish no minimum, and Wealthsimple's Core tier starts at $1 in assets. TD Easy Trade lets you start with $1.

The W-8BEN. Form W-8BEN is the US Internal Revenue Service form a foreign beneficial owner gives the withholding agent, which is your broker, to claim the reduced treaty rate. With it on file, US dividends have 15% withheld instead of the 30% that applies to most US-source income paid to a foreign person. You give the form to your broker, not to the IRS. Brokers that offer US trading ask clients for it, often as part of the application. Please confirm it is on file before you buy your first US dividend payer.

Check the regulator status. Every broker in the table above is a member of the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) or belongs to a bank group whose dealer is on CIPF's member list. CIPF covers property missing when a member firm fails, up to $1,000,000 for all general accounts combined (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA), with separate $1,000,000 limits for retirement accounts and for RESPs. It does not cover market losses. All CIPF members are regulated by the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO).

Turn on the features you need. If the broker requires it, ask for a USD side on each account that will hold US stocks, enable US trading, and switch on fractional shares or a dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) if you plan to use them. A USD side added later may carry a fee: Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core, after a 30-day trial, and Qtrade US$15 a quarter per registered account unless you qualify for Investor Plus.

4. Step 4: Fund the Account

From your bank. Most investors fund in Canadian dollars by linking a bank account and sending a transfer. The money arrives in the Canadian-dollar side of the account. For a registered account, the deposit counts against your room: $7,000 of new TFSA room for 2026, $8,000 of FHSA room a year, and your personal RRSP deduction limit.

By transferring an account in. Moving an existing account is usually done as a direct transfer from the old institution. For a TFSA, a direct transfer has no effect on your room, while withdrawing and redepositing counts as a new contribution and can create an excess taxed at 1% a month. Investments can often move in kind, which avoids selling. Our guide on how to transfer a TFSA, RRSP or investment account covers the forms.

5. Step 5: Choose How to Get US Dollars

Three routes. There are three ways to turn Canadian dollars into US dollars for investing. You can let the broker convert at its rate, which is simple and immediate. You can use Norbert's Gambit, which swaps an interlisted security from its Canadian-dollar listing to its US-dollar listing. Or you can bring in US dollars you already hold. The best way to buy US stocks in Canada at the lowest cost usually combines a broker with a USD side and the cheapest of these routes for your amount.

7. Option B: Norbert's Gambit in General Terms

The idea. Some securities are listed on a Canadian exchange in both Canadian dollars and US dollars. Norbert's Gambit uses that. You buy the Canadian-dollar listing, ask your broker to journal (move) the position to the US-dollar listing in your account, then sell it for US dollars. You pay trading costs instead of the broker's conversion rate. It works in reverse to turn US dollars into Canadian dollars.

The usual mechanism. The interlisted US Dollar Currency ETF, which trades as DLR in Canadian dollars and DLR.U in US dollars, is the security most often used, because its value tracks the US dollar. We name it only to explain how the method works. This is not a recommendation to buy it or any other security.

  1. Buy the Canadian-dollar listing (DLR) with the Canadian dollars in your account.
  2. Ask your broker to journal the units to the US-dollar listing (DLR.U). Some brokers take this request online, others by message or phone.
  3. Wait for the journal. It is not instant. The units are still yours, and their value still moves with the market while you wait.
  4. Sell the US-dollar listing. The proceeds land in your US-dollar balance, ready to invest.

What it costs. Two commissions at your broker's rates (one to buy, one to sell), the bid-ask spread on both trades, and any price change during the wait. There is no published conversion fee. At a broker charging $0 on Canadian-listed ETF trades, the spreads are most of the cost. At a broker charging $9.95 a trade with a 1.6% conversion rate, the two commissions ($19.90) equal the conversion fee at $1,243.75 ($19.90 / 1.6%); spreads and price movement push the break-even point higher.

Where it works. You need an account with both a Canadian-dollar and a US-dollar side, and a broker willing to journal the position. Wealthsimple lists Norbert's Gambit as available. For other brokers, ask how they handle the journal request before you start. Our Norbert's Gambit step-by-step guide covers timing, record-keeping and what to do if the journal is delayed.

8. Option C: Bring In US Dollars You Already Have

The cheapest conversion is none. If you already earn or hold US dollars, for example from US employment, US rental income or a US-dollar bank account in Canada, sending those dollars straight to your account's US-dollar side means no conversion at all. Before you send money, please ask your broker which methods it accepts for US-dollar deposits and into which accounts.

Registered accounts still count in Canadian dollars. A US-dollar deposit into a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA uses room at its Canadian-dollar value on the day it goes in. A US$5,000 TFSA contribution uses more or less of your $7,000 2026 limit depending on the exchange rate that day, so check the value before you deposit.

Parking US dollars. US dollars waiting to be invested can sit in a US-dollar savings account rather than earning little in a brokerage account. Compare rates on our USD savings accounts page. Eligible deposits in Canadian or foreign currency at a CDIC member are covered up to $100,000 per insured category at each member institution, while stocks and ETFs are not covered by CDIC.

Moving US shares in kind. US shares already held at another Canadian broker can usually be transferred in kind, which avoids selling, converting to Canadian dollars and converting back. Check the transfer-out fee at the old broker and whether your new broker covers it, as section 4 sets out.

When this route fits. Cross-border workers, people with US pensions or rental income, and anyone who keeps a US-dollar balance for travel often have a steady source of US dollars. For them, the broker's conversion rate may barely matter, and a broker with low US commissions and a USD side in every account matters more.

9. Step 6: Place Your Order

Find the right listing. Search the company name or ticker and check that you have the US listing, priced in US dollars, if that is what you want. Some companies also trade in Canada, and some brokers show both. Confirm which account the order is coming from and whether it will draw on US dollars or trigger a conversion.

Market or limit order. A market order buys at the best available price right away, which may be higher than the last price you saw. A limit order sets the highest price you will pay and fills only at that price or better. Limit orders give you control over price, and they are required for extended-hours trading at Wealthsimple.

Trading hours. The regular US session runs from 9:30 am to 4:00 pm Eastern time. Wealthsimple also offers pre-market trading from 4:00 am, after-hours trading until 8:00 pm and an overnight session from 8:00 pm to 4:00 am, from Sunday 8 pm to Friday 8 pm Eastern time. Prices outside regular hours can be less stable, and fewer shares may trade.

Fractional shares. A fractional order buys part of a share. Wealthsimple offers it on thousands of Canadian and US stocks and ETFs from $1, during market hours only. Questrade and Interactive Brokers offer fractional trading, CIBC advertises it, and TD charges $1.99 per partial-share trade. moomoo does not support fractional shares.

10. After You Buy: Dividends and US Withholding Tax

What happens on payment day. The US tax is taken before the dividend reaches your account. The rate is 15% under Article X(2)(b) of the Canada-US tax treaty with a W-8BEN on file, 30% without one, and 0% on US-listed securities held directly in a documented RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF.

11. After You Buy: Taxes on US Stocks in a Non-Registered Account

US dividends. Report the gross dividend, converted to Canadian dollars, as foreign income. US dividends do not get the Canadian dividend tax credit, so they are taxed at your full marginal rate, like interest. You then claim a foreign tax credit for the US tax withheld: the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. The federal part goes on Form T2209 (line 40500) and, outside Quebec, the provincial part on Form T2036.

A worked figure. This example is hypothetical. An Ontario resident with $70,000 of taxable income in 2026 has a combined marginal rate of 29.65% (20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario). On a dividend worth $1,000 in Canadian dollars, Canadian tax is $296.50 ($1,000 x 29.65%). The $150 of US tax is less than that Canadian tax, so all $150 is credited. Net Canadian tax is $146.50 ($296.50 - $150) and total tax stays at $296.50.

Capital gains are calculated in Canadian dollars. For Canadian tax, your cost is converted at the exchange rate when you bought, and your proceeds at the rate when you sold. A currency move can create a gain or loss even if the US price did not change. Suppose you bought US$10,000 of shares when US$1 cost C$1.40 (C$14,000) and sold them for US$10,000 when US$1 cost C$1.30 (C$13,000): you have a C$1,000 capital loss ($14,000 - $13,000). Use our capital gains tax calculator for the tax on a gain.

The superficial loss rule. A loss is denied if you or an affiliated person, which includes your spouse or common-law partner, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after. The denied loss can usually be added to the cost of the replacement shares, so it is deferred rather than lost.

Using losses. Net capital losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, and they offset only taxable capital gains. Losses inside a TFSA or RRSP cannot be claimed at all.

Registered accounts. None of the yearly reporting above applies to US holdings inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA. RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are taxed as income when you take them out.

How to Buy US Stocks in Canada: The Steps in Brief

  1. Choose the account. RRSP for US dividend payers if you have room, TFSA for growth-focused US holdings, taxable account for the rest.
  2. Pick a broker. Look for a low conversion rate, a USD side in the accounts you will use, and low US commissions.
  3. Open the account and file a W-8BEN. Confirm the form is on file and ask for a USD side on each account that will hold US stocks.
  4. Fund it. Deposit Canadian dollars, transfer an account in directly, or send US dollars you already hold.
  5. Get US dollars. Use the broker's conversion for small sums and consider Norbert's Gambit or a lower-cost broker for large ones.
  6. Place your order. Use a limit order outside regular hours, and check commissions and pass-through fees.
  7. Check the first dividend. 15% withheld in a TFSA or taxable account, 0% in a documented RRSP.

Mistakes to Avoid When You Buy US Stocks From Canada

Converting on every trade. In a CAD-only account, each purchase, sale and dividend is converted. A round trip on $10,000 at 1.5% costs $300. Ask for a USD side before you start trading.

Skipping the W-8BEN. Without it, 30% of each US dividend can be withheld instead of 15%.

Assuming a Canadian-listed fund gets the RRSP exemption. Only US-listed securities held directly in a documented RRSP avoid US withholding. A Canadian-listed fund holding US shares pays 15% inside the fund.

Market orders outside regular hours. Fewer shares trade in extended sessions, so prices can move more. A limit order caps what you pay.

Tracking cost in US dollars only. In a taxable account, your gain or loss for Canadian tax is in Canadian dollars at the exchange rates on each trade date. Keep the rate with every purchase and sale.

Buying back within 30 days after a loss. Repurchasing identical shares within 30 days before or after a sale at a loss, yourself or through your spouse, triggers the superficial loss rule.

Moving a TFSA by withdrawing. A withdrawal followed by a deposit counts as a new contribution and can create an excess taxed at 1% a month. Use a direct transfer.

10 Ways to Cut FX Fees and Tax on US Stocks

1. Choose a USD side for every account

A US-dollar side in your TFSA and RRSP lets you convert once and keep dividends and sale proceeds in US dollars. Questrade, National Bank, Desjardins, moomoo and TD Easy Trade list no separate fee for it; Wealthsimple (on Core) and Qtrade charge a monthly or quarterly fee, and others do not publish their terms, so compare before you open.

2. Convert in fewer, larger amounts

Rates fall on larger conversions at the bank-owned brokers and inside a Wealthsimple USD account. At TD, one $50,000 conversion costs $390 while five $10,000 conversions cost $630. At a flat 1.5% broker, timing does not change the rate.

3. Use Norbert's Gambit for lump sums

On a large amount, two trades and a journal can replace a 1.5% conversion. At $0 commissions, the spreads and the price risk while you wait are the main costs. Our Norbert's Gambit guide covers the steps.

4. Spend US dollars you already earn

US employment, pension or rental income paid in US dollars can go straight into a USD side. That avoids two conversions: into Canadian dollars at the bank and back into US dollars at the broker.

5. File the W-8BEN before the first dividend

The form takes US withholding from 30% to 15% in a TFSA, FHSA, RESP or taxable account. On US$1,000 of dividends, that keeps US$150 a year in your account.

6. Put US dividend payers in the RRSP

A documented RRSP pays no US withholding on US-listed holdings held directly. US$1,000 of dividends keeps all US$1,000 there, against US$850 in a TFSA. Growth-focused US holdings lose little in a TFSA instead.

7. Keep dividends in US dollars

If the account has no USD side, each dividend is converted at the broker's rate. Collecting dividends in US dollars and reinvesting them in US holdings avoids converting out and back in.

8. Use limit orders

A limit order sets the price you will pay and avoids surprises when prices move fast or few shares trade. At Wealthsimple, extended-hours orders must be limit orders.

9. Match the commission type to your order size

Per-share pricing is low on small orders and rises with share count: 40 shares cost US$1.00 at Interactive Brokers and 1,000 shares cost US$5.00. Flat $0 commissions do not change with size, but the conversion rate matters more.

10. Remove account fees

Small accounts can pay $25 a quarter at TD Direct Investing or $100 a year at CIBC or National Bank unless a waiver applies. Check the waiver conditions against your balance and age once a year.

Illustrative Example: Moving $25,000 Into US Shares

This example is hypothetical.

Sam has $25,000 in cash in his RRSP at a broker whose registered accounts have a US-dollar side. The broker charges $0 on Canadian-listed ETF trades and 1.5% to convert. He wants US-listed shares that would pay about US$500 a year in dividends.

  1. Broker conversion. Converting $25,000 at 1.5% costs $375.00 ($25,000 x 1.5%).
  2. Norbert's Gambit. Two trades at $0 commission. Assume, for this example only, that the bid-ask spreads on the two trades cost 0.05% of the amount: $12.50 ($25,000 x 0.05%). That is $362.50 less than the broker conversion ($375.00 - $12.50), before any price change during the journal.
  3. If commissions applied. At $9.95 a trade, the cost would be $32.40 ($9.95 x 2 + $12.50), still $342.60 less than converting ($375.00 - $32.40).
  4. Dividends in the RRSP. With the account documented, nothing is withheld on the US$500, so the full US$500 is credited.
  5. The same shares in a TFSA. US$75 would be withheld each year (US$500 x 15%), or US$750 over 10 years (US$75 x 10) at the same dividend, with no way to recover it.
  6. Selling later. Sale proceeds stay in US dollars in the RRSP's US-dollar side. Sam converts back only when he needs Canadian dollars, and pays the conversion then.

Sam's biggest saving came from the conversion method, and his second biggest from choosing the RRSP for a dividend-paying US holding.

How to Buy US Stocks in Canada: FAQ

How do I buy US stocks in Canada?

Open an account at a broker that offers US trading, such as a TFSA, RRSP or taxable account. File a W-8BEN with the broker, deposit money, and convert Canadian dollars to US dollars or let the broker convert when you trade. Then search the ticker, choose a market or limit order, and buy. Check the first dividend to confirm the US withholding rate.

Do I need a US dollar account to buy US stocks?

No, but it usually costs less. Without a USD side, the broker converts on every purchase, sale and dividend. With one, you convert once and keep US dollars until you choose to convert back. Some brokers charge for the USD side: Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core, and Qtrade US$15 a quarter per registered account unless you have Investor Plus.

What is the cheapest way to convert Canadian dollars to US dollars for investing?

Among published broker rates as of October 22, 2026, Interactive Brokers charges the least: no markup and 0.20 basis points, with a US$2.00 minimum, which is US$2.00 on $10,000. Norbert's Gambit replaces the conversion rate with trading costs and a short wait. Using US dollars you already hold avoids conversion altogether. This is general information, not a recommendation.

What is Norbert's Gambit?

A way to convert currency by buying an interlisted security in Canadian dollars, having your broker journal it to its US-dollar listing, and selling it for US dollars. The US Dollar Currency ETF, listed as DLR and DLR.U, is the usual mechanism; naming it is not a recommendation. You pay two commissions and the bid-ask spreads instead of the conversion rate.

Do Canadians pay US tax on US stock dividends?

Yes, in most accounts. Under the Canada-US tax treaty, 15% is withheld from US dividends when a W-8BEN is on file, and 30% without one. US-listed securities held directly in a documented RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF are exempt. In a taxable account you recover the 15% as a foreign tax credit; in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP you cannot.

What is a W-8BEN and do I need one?

Form W-8BEN is a US Internal Revenue Service form that a foreign beneficial owner gives the withholding agent, usually the broker, to claim the treaty rate. Without it, most US-source income paid to a foreign person is taxed at 30%. With it, US dividends are taxed at 15%. You give it to your broker, not to the IRS.

Which account should hold my US stocks?

In general terms, US dividend payers keep the most in an RRSP, where nothing is withheld on US-listed holdings held directly. Growth-focused US holdings lose little in a TFSA, where gains are never taxed in Canada. A taxable account lets you claim the US tax as a credit and use capital losses. Your own order depends on your tax rates now and later.

Can I buy US stocks with no commission in Canada?

Yes. As of October 22, 2026, Questrade, Wealthsimple, Webull, Qtrade, National Bank Direct Brokerage, BMO InvestorLine and Desjardins Online Brokerage charge $0 commission on online US stock trades. Costs remain: a currency conversion, often 1.5% or more, plus small pass-through fees such as the SEC fee on US sales and, at some brokers, ECN or exchange fees.

Can I buy fractional US shares in Canada?

Yes, at some brokers. Wealthsimple offers fractional trading in thousands of Canadian and US stocks and ETFs from $1 during market hours. Questrade and Interactive Brokers offer fractional shares, CIBC advertises them, and TD charges $1.99 per partial-share trade. moomoo does not support fractional shares.

When can I trade US stocks from Canada?

The regular US session runs from 9:30 am to 4:00 pm Eastern time on trading days. Some brokers offer extended hours. Wealthsimple offers US-listed stocks and ETFs from Sunday 8 pm to Friday 8 pm Eastern time, including pre-market, after-hours and overnight sessions, with extended-hours orders placed as limit orders.

How are US stocks taxed in a non-registered account in Canada?

US dividends are reported in Canadian dollars as foreign income, taxed at your full marginal rate, with a foreign tax credit for the US tax withheld, claimed on Form T2209 and, outside Quebec, Form T2036. Capital gains and losses are calculated in Canadian dollars using the exchange rate on each trade date, and the superficial loss rule applies to repurchases within 30 days.

Should I buy the US-listed or Canadian-listed version of a holding?

It depends on the account. In an RRSP, a US-listed holding avoids the 15% US withholding that a Canadian-listed fund of US shares pays inside the fund. In a TFSA, both lose one 15% layer, so the conversion cost and fund fees decide. In a taxable account, the US tax is generally creditable either way. This is general information, not a recommendation.
