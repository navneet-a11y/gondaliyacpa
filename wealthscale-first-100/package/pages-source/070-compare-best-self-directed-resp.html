2. What Running Your Own RESP Involves

The jobs you take on. A self-directed RESP gives you every decision a managed plan would make for you. You choose the investments, buy them when contributions arrive, decide when to rebalance, and move toward safer holdings as the first tuition bill gets closer. You also keep track of how much each child has received in grants and when each grant deadline falls.

Where it pays off. The cost gap widens as the plan grows. A robo-advisor's fee is a percentage of the balance, so on $40,000 the three ranked robo-advisors cost $148.00 to $332.00 a year, while a $0-trade broker with no RESP fee still costs $0.00. Over the many years an RESP stays open, that difference adds up.

Where it does not. If you are unlikely to rebalance, or would leave contributions in cash for months, a managed plan's automatic investing may be worth its fee. A self-directed plan only works if you actually run it. Our RESP provider rankings compare both routes side by side, and our robo-advisor comparison covers managed fees in full.

A middle path. Some families pick a broker with fractional shares and DRIP, buy one or two broad funds and rebalance once a year. That keeps the work small while keeping costs at the broker level.

4. Family RESP at an Online Broker: How It Works

Who can be named. A family plan can hold several beneficiaries, and it is the only RESP that can. Each one must be related by blood or adoption to every living subscriber and must be under 21 when named. Each needs a social insurance number and Canadian residency at the time of naming. A newborn sibling can be added later, as long as the same tests are met. Among the nine brokers, Questrade, Wealthsimple and Qtrade state that they offer a family RESP online.

Each contribution belongs to a child. Grants are worked out per beneficiary, so every contribution is credited to one named child. A family that puts $5,000 into a two-child plan and splits it $2,500 each collects $1,000 of CESG ($500 per child). Putting all $5,000 against one child collects only $500 of basic CESG in a normal year, because the grant applies to the first $2,500 per child, unless that child has unused grant room to catch up.

Limits are per child. Each beneficiary has a $50,000 lifetime contribution limit across all RESPs and a $7,200 lifetime CESG maximum. The Canada Learning Bond tops out at $2,000, the BCTESG at $1,200 and the QESI at $3,600 per child. If grandparents run their own plan for the same child, their contributions count against that child's $50,000 too.

5. Grant Administration: What Each Broker Publishes

The broker files the requests. You do not apply for the CESG, the Canada Learning Bond, the BCTESG or the QESI with the government yourself. The RESP provider requests each grant with your contributions and the child's details, and the money is paid into the plan. A broker that does not offer a program cannot request it for you.

What we could verify. None of the nine brokers' pages we checked names the grant programs it requests. Wealthsimple's RESP page, for example, does not mention the CESG, the CLB, the BCTESG or the QESI. We therefore make no claim about which broker administers which grant. What the brokers do state about plan types is in the master comparison table above.

The questions to send before you open. Please ask, in writing: Do you request the basic and additional CESG? Do you request the Canada Learning Bond? If we live in B.C., do you request the BCTESG, and if we live in Quebec, the QESI? What do you need from us for the income-tested grants? Keep the reply with your records.

Deadlines that sit with you. The BCTESG can be requested from the 6th birthday until the day before the 9th. The QESI needs the child to be under 18 and a Quebec resident on December 31. The CLB must be requested before the beneficiary turns 21, and the CESG stops after the year the child turns 17. CESG is paid at 16 and 17 only if at least $2,000 went in by the end of the year the child turned 15, or at least $100 a year in any four years before then, with nothing withdrawn. Our guide to RESP grants sets out each program's rules, and the RESP grant calculator projects each child's grants to age 17.

8. Self-Directed RESP in Canada: US Holdings and Currency

The 15% that does not come back. US dividends paid into an RESP have 15% withheld by the US when the broker holds your Form W-8BEN, or 30% without it. The treaty exemption for retirement accounts does not reach RESPs, and the tax cannot be recovered as a foreign tax credit inside the plan. That is a lasting drag on any US dividend-paying holding in an RESP.

Paying to hold US dollars, or paying to convert. Two of the nine brokers publish a priced US-dollar option for RESPs, and they cost the same: Wealthsimple's US-dollar account is $10 plus tax a month on Core ($120 a year before tax), and Scotia iTRADE's U.S.-Friendly RESP is $30 a quarter ($120 a year). Questrade says its registered accounts hold US dollars at no extra cost. Without a US-dollar option, every US trade is converted: 1.5% at Questrade and Wealthsimple, and about 1.6% to 1.70% on smaller amounts at the bank-owned brokers that publish a rate.

The break-even. A US-dollar account lets later sales and dividends stay in US dollars, but getting into US dollars still costs a conversion: inside a Wealthsimple USD account, a conversion under $10,000 costs 1.5%. At 1.5%, the $120 yearly fee equals the cost of converting $8,000 ($120 / 1.5% = $8,000), so a family that rarely trades US holdings may pay less by converting each time. At Questrade, holding US dollars costs nothing extra, so the question does not arise.

9. Paying for School and Closing the Plan

Educational assistance payments. Grants and investment growth reach the student as educational assistance payments (EAPs). In the first 13 consecutive weeks of a full-time program (at least 10 hours a week), EAPs are capped at $8,000. In a part-time program (at least 12 hours a month), the cap is $4,000 for each 13-week period. At a broker, you sell enough holdings to raise the cash before you request each payment, which is why the final years are often held in cash or GICs.

If no child goes on to school. Investment income can be paid to the subscriber as an accumulated income payment (AIP) only if three conditions line up: there is one subscriber who is a Canadian resident, and either the plan is past the year of its 9th anniversary with every beneficiary 21 or older and not eligible for EAPs, or it is the plan's 35th-anniversary year, or every beneficiary has died. An AIP is taxed as income plus an extra 20% (12% in Quebec).

The RRSP route. Up to $50,000 of AIPs over a lifetime can go into your RRSP instead, within your available RRSP room, in the year received or the first 60 days after. Form T1171 waives the withholding. Our RRSP guide explains how contribution room works.

10. Moving an Existing RESP to a Self-Directed Broker

The new broker does the work. Open the self-directed RESP with the same plan type and the same beneficiaries, then sign the new broker's transfer form. The money moves directly between the two plans and stays inside the RESP system. Please do not withdraw and re-contribute: a withdrawal is not a transfer.

Fees on the way out and in. Most brokers charge $150 to move a plan out. Wealthsimple charges $0. On the way in, Questrade rebates up to $150 per account, RBC up to $200 on $15,000 or more, Qtrade up to $150 per client on $15,000 or more under its Fall Offer 2026, and Wealthsimple up to $150 per account on $25,000 or more, although its self-directed RESP transfers in are still in beta. The transfer fee calculator below shows which brokers would cover your old firm's fee for the amount you enter.

Partial transfers. If you move only some holdings, Desjardins charges $25 per security on a partial transfer rather than the $150 full-transfer fee, which can make a partial move cheaper when only one or two holdings are involved.

Moving from a group plan. Ask the group plan dealer for the exact amount that would transfer and the fees that will not be returned before you start. Read the prospectus sections on transfers and early exit.

Timing. Avoid moving a plan in the months before a BCTESG deadline or just before an EAP request, and keep December contributions out of the transfer window so they count in the right year. Our guide on how to transfer an investment account covers the forms step by step.

10 Strategies for Running a Self-Directed RESP

1. Credit $2,500 to each child every year

The full $500 CESG per child comes from exactly $2,500 per child in a calendar year. In a family plan, set the split on every contribution so no child misses out.

2. Use catch-up years on purpose

A child can receive up to $1,000 of CESG in a year when unused room exists. Contributing $5,000 for that child in one year collects $500 of missed grant.

3. Stop at $36,000 per child for grant purposes

The $7,200 CESG maximum is reached on $36,000 of contributions ($36,000 x 20%). Fourteen years at $2,500 ($35,000) plus $1,000 more gets there. Anything beyond grows tax-deferred but earns no grant.

4. Buy less often at per-trade brokers

Four quarterly purchases at $9.95 cost $39.80 a year, against $119.40 for twelve. At $0-trade brokers, buy as often as you like.

5. Turn on DRIP

Reinvested dividends stay working. Where DRIP buys only full shares, check the cash balance a few times a year and invest it by hand.

6. Clear the fee threshold early

If you transfer in an existing plan, a balance above $15,000 or $20,000 from day one can avoid Desjardins, Scotia iTRADE or National Bank fees entirely.

7. Rebalance with contributions

Direct each new contribution to the part of the plan that is below target. You rebalance without selling and without extra trades.

8. Protect the first year's money

Some families move the money for the first year of school into cash or GICs a few years ahead, so a market fall just before school cannot shrink it. CDIC covers eligible RESP deposits up to $100,000 at each member institution.

9. Request provincial grants in time

Diary the 6th birthday for the BCTESG and confirm the broker requests it. In Quebec, confirm the QESI is switched on from the first contribution.

10. Keep a one-page record

List each child's contributions, CESG received and remaining grant room. It makes catch-up years, the age 16 and 17 rule and the $50,000 limit easy to track.

Illustrative Example: A Family Plan for Two Children in B.C.

This example is hypothetical. Priya and Marc live in British Columbia with two children, aged 6 and 3. With family income above $117,045, they qualify for the basic CESG only. They hold a $30,000 family RESP at a self-directed broker and contribute $5,000 a year in four quarterly deposits of $1,250, credited $625 to each child. Each deposit buys one fund, and they make two rebalancing trades a year, for six trades in total.

  1. Yearly CESG. Each child is credited $2,500 ($625 x 4), earning $500 ($2,500 x 20%). The plan collects $1,000 a year ($500 x 2).
  2. The BCTESG. Their 6-year-old is in the request window, which runs until the day before the 9th birthday. If their broker requests it, the plan receives $1,200 once, with no contribution needed. This year's grants could total $2,200 ($1,000 + $1,200).
  3. Trading cost. Six trades cost $0.00 at Questrade, Wealthsimple, Desjardins, National Bank, Qtrade or BMO. At CIBC they cost $41.70 (6 x $6.95), at RBC $59.70 (6 x $9.95) and at Scotia iTRADE $59.94 (6 x $9.99), unless they buy only from those brokers' $0 ETF lists.
  4. Account fees. At $30,000 the plan is above every small-balance threshold on this page, so no broker charges an RESP fee.
  5. The younger child. The 3-year-old's BCTESG window opens at the 6th birthday, three years from now. Priya and Marc note the date and ask their broker, in writing, whether it requests the BCTESG.

Best Self-Directed RESP Account FAQ

What is the best self-directed RESP account in Canada?

On our published score, Questrade is the best self-directed RESP account at 4.0 / 5. It states individual and family RESPs, charges $0 on Canadian and US-listed stock and ETF trades and has no account fee. Wealthsimple is a close second, also at 4.0 / 5, and Desjardins Online Brokerage is third at 3.9 / 5.

What is a self-directed RESP?

A self-directed RESP is an RESP at an online broker where you choose and manage the investments yourself. You decide what to buy, when to buy it and when to rebalance. The grants are the same as in any RESP; the difference is control and cost, which can be $0 a year at several brokers.

Can I open a family RESP at an online broker?

Yes. Questrade, Wealthsimple and Qtrade state that they offer a family RESP online. The other six brokers we ranked list RESPs without naming the plan type on the pages we checked, so please confirm with them. Every child named in a family plan must be related to you by blood or adoption.

Do online brokers apply for RESP grants for me?

The RESP provider requests the grants, not you. None of the brokers' pages we checked lists which programs it requests, so please ask in writing whether it handles the CESG, additional CESG, Canada Learning Bond and, where you live, the BCTESG or QESI before you open the plan.

How much does a self-directed RESP cost?

At Questrade, Wealthsimple, Qtrade and BMO, a self-directed RESP can cost $0 a year in account fees and stock or ETF commissions. Other brokers add a small-balance fee: $50 at Desjardins and $25 at Scotia iTRADE under $15,000, $100 at National Bank under $20,000 and $100 at CIBC at $10,000 or less.

Which self-directed RESP is best for a small balance?

A broker with no RESP account fee and $0 trades charges nothing in account fees or stock and ETF commissions at any size: Questrade, Wealthsimple, Qtrade and BMO InvestorLine. Wealthsimple's fractional shares from $1 also let small contributions be invested in full. Avoid brokers whose fee applies below your balance, or check whether you qualify for a waiver.

Can I buy fractional shares in an RESP?

At some brokers. Wealthsimple offers fractional shares from $1, and Questrade and CIBC also offer them; Questrade's DRIP reinvests in fractions once fractional shares are switched on. The other brokers we ranked did not mention fractional shares on the pages we checked.

How should I invest a self-directed RESP?

WealthScale does not recommend specific investments. Families commonly hold more growth investments when school is far away and move the money needed for the first years into cash or GICs as school gets closer. Eligible deposits in an RESP at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 in their own category.

Can I hold US stocks in a self-directed RESP?

Yes, but US dividends lose 15% to US withholding with a W-8BEN on file, and that tax cannot be recovered inside an RESP. Questrade lets registered accounts hold US dollars at no extra cost; Wealthsimple's US-dollar account on Core and Scotia iTRADE's U.S.-Friendly RESP each cost $120 a year.

How do I transfer my RESP to a self-directed broker?

Open the new RESP with the same plan type and beneficiaries, then sign the new broker's transfer form. Do not withdraw the money. Most brokers charge $150 to transfer out; Questrade, RBC, Qtrade and Wealthsimple refund some or all of it under their own conditions. Wealthsimple's self-directed RESP transfers in are in beta.

What happens if my child does not go to school?

Investment income can be paid to you as an accumulated income payment once the plan meets the conditions, taxed as income plus an extra 20% (12% in Quebec). You can move up to $50,000 of it into your RRSP instead, within your RRSP room, using Form T1171.

Who can I name in a family RESP?

Every beneficiary must be related by blood or adoption to each living subscriber, be under 21 when named and have a social insurance number and Canadian residency at that time. A parent's children and a grandparent's grandchildren meet the relationship test. A godparent or friend needs an individual plan instead.
