Investment Accounts in Canada: TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, RESP and More

The main types of investment accounts Canada offers are the TFSA, the RRSP and spousal RRSP, the FHSA, the RESP, the RRIF, the locked-in LIRA and LIF, and the ordinary non-registered account. Each one is a tax wrapper, not an investment: you choose what goes inside. For 2026, the TFSA limit is $7,000, the RRSP limit is 18% of last year's earned income up to $33,810, and the FHSA gives $8,000 a year toward a first home. This page compares every account on limits and on tax going in, while invested and coming out, and shows which goal each one is built for.

At a glance

Registered accounts. The TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, RESP, RRIF, LIRA and LIF are registered with the Canada Revenue Agency, which gives them tax advantages in return for limits and rules. A non-registered account has no limits and no tax shelter.

2026 limits. TFSA: $7,000, with $109,000 of total room for someone 18 or older and resident every year since 2009. RRSP: 18% of 2025 earned income up to $33,810. FHSA: $8,000 a year and $40,000 for life. RESP: $50,000 lifetime per child, with grants of up to $7,200.

Tax in one line each. TFSA: no deduction, tax-free out. RRSP and FHSA: deductible going in; RRSP withdrawals are taxed, while FHSA withdrawals for a qualifying first home are tax-free. RESP: no deduction, but government grants are added, and grants and growth are taxed to the student when paid out. Non-registered: taxed every year.

US dividends. The US withholds 15% on dividends paid to a Canadian resident with a W-8BEN on file. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs holding US-listed securities directly are exempt; in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP the 15% cannot be recovered.

Protection. CDIC insures eligible deposits up to $100,000 per category at each member institution, and a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, FHSA and RESP are each a separate category. CIPF covers up to $1,000,000 in each of three account groups if a member investment dealer fails. Neither covers market losses.

1. TFSA: Tax-Free Growth for Any Goal

Who can open one. Any resident of Canada who is 18 or older and has a valid social insurance number. Where the age of majority is 19, you open it at 19 but keep the room from the year you turned 18. Banks, credit unions, trust companies and insurance companies can all issue TFSAs, and you can hold more than one, with one combined room limit.

2026 room. The 2026 limit is $7,000. Unused room carries forward with no end date, so someone who has been 18 or older and resident every year since 2009 and never contributed has $109,000 of room. Newcomers build room only from the year they become resident, and they get that full year's limit. No new room builds in a year you are a non-resident.

Tax in, during and out. Contributions are not deductible. Interest, dividends and capital gains earned inside are not taxed, and withdrawals are tax-free at any time and for any reason. Withdrawals are not income, so they do not raise the net income used to work out income-tested benefits. Two exceptions: US dividends lose 15% to US withholding, and someone who trades with the frequency and skill of a professional can have the income taxed as business income.

The rules that cost people money. A withdrawal is added back to your room only on January 1 of the next year. Withdraw and redeposit in the same year and you may over-contribute, which is taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess, with no grace amount. Moving money between TFSAs by direct transfer avoids that. A non-qualified investment is taxed at 50% of its value.

2. RRSP and Spousal RRSP: A Deduction Now, Tax Later

Room. Your RRSP deduction limit is 18% of your previous year's earned income, up to $33,810 for 2026 ($35,390 for 2027), minus any pension adjustment from a workplace pension, plus unused room from earlier years. Please check your latest notice of assessment for your exact figure before you contribute. You can be over by up to $2,000 without penalty if you were 18 or older in the previous year; above that the excess is taxed at 1% a month.

Tax in, during and out. Contributions are deductible, which lowers your taxable income, so the tax saved depends on your marginal rate. Growth is not taxed while it stays in the plan. Every withdrawal is taxable income, and the institution withholds tax up front: 10% up to $5,000, 20% over $5,000 up to $15,000 and 30% over $15,000 outside Quebec. In Quebec the federal part is 5%, 10% or 15%, with provincial tax withheld on top.

Deadlines and age 71. Contributions made by the 60th day of a year can be deducted for the year before. For the 2025 tax year the deadline was March 2, 2026; the 2026 date has not been published yet. December 31 of the year you turn 71 is the last day to contribute to your own RRSP. By then you must withdraw it, transfer it to a RRIF or buy an annuity.

Tax-free borrowing. The Home Buyers' Plan lets a first-time buyer withdraw up to $60,000 without tax and repay it over 15 years. The Lifelong Learning Plan allows $10,000 a year, up to $20,000, for full-time education for you or your spouse, repaid over 10 years. A missed repayment is added to your income.

Spousal RRSP. You contribute and claim the deduction, using your own room, but your spouse or common-law partner owns the plan. In retirement, withdrawals are taxed in their hands, which can even out two incomes. If your spouse withdraws money you contributed in that year or either of the two previous years, the withdrawal is generally taxed to you instead. You can keep contributing to a spousal RRSP until the end of the year your spouse turns 71, even after you pass 71.

3. FHSA: Tax-Free Savings for a First Home

Who can open one. A resident of Canada who is at least 18 (19 where that is the age of majority), no older than 71 at December 31 of the year the account is opened, and a first-time home buyer. That means neither you nor your current spouse or common-law partner owned and lived in a home in the current year before opening or in the previous four calendar years.

Room. Room starts only when you open your first FHSA: $8,000 that year and $8,000 each year after, up to $40,000 for life. Up to $8,000 of unused room carries forward, so the most you can add in one year is $16,000. Unlike an RRSP, the contribution deadline is December 31, with no 60-day period. Room builds from the year you open the account, so opening early matters even if you cannot contribute yet.

Tax in, during and out. Contributions are deductible like an RRSP, and you can carry the deduction forward to a higher-income year, even after the account closes. Growth is not taxed. A qualifying withdrawal for a first home is tax-free, which makes the FHSA the only account that is deductible going in and tax-free coming out. You need a written agreement to buy or build with completion before October 1 of the year after the withdrawal, and you must plan to live in the home within one year.

If you never buy. You can transfer the balance to an RRSP or RRIF without tax and without using RRSP room, or withdraw it as taxable income. The account must close by December 31 of the year of the earliest of its 15th anniversary, your 71st birthday or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. Over-contributions are taxed at 1% a month. You can use the FHSA and the Home Buyers' Plan for the same home.

4. RESP: Grants for a Child's Education

Limits. You can contribute up to $50,000 over a child's lifetime across all RESPs for that child. There has been no annual limit since 2007. Over-contributions are taxed at 1% a month. Contributions can continue until the year of the plan's 31st anniversary, and the plan must close by the end of the year of its 35th anniversary.

Grants. The Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year, or $500, up to $7,200 per child. Unused grant room carries forward, so up to $1,000 can be paid in one year. Families with adjusted family net income below $58,523 get an extra $100 a year on the first $500, and those from $58,523 up to $117,045 an extra $50. The Canada Learning Bond adds up to $2,000 for lower-income families with no contribution needed. British Columbia adds a one-time $1,200, and Quebec adds 10% of contributions, up to $250 a year or $500 with unused room, plus up to $50 for lower incomes. Grants stop after the year the child turns 17.

Tax in, during and out. Contributions are not deductible. Growth and grants are not taxed inside the plan. When the child studies, your own contributions can be returned to you without tax, and the grants and growth are paid to the student as educational assistance payments, which the student reports as income. Educational assistance payments are capped at $8,000 in the first 13 consecutive weeks of full-time study.

If the child does not study. Grants must be returned. Once conditions on the plan's age and the beneficiaries are met, growth can be paid to the subscriber as an accumulated income payment, taxed at your normal rate plus an extra 20% (12% in Quebec). Up to $50,000 of it can go into your RRSP instead if you have the room, which avoids the extra tax. A family plan can name several children related to you by blood or adoption, so savings can follow whichever child studies.

5. RRIF: Turning an RRSP Into Retirement Income

When you need one. An RRSP must be withdrawn, turned into an annuity or transferred to a registered retirement income fund by December 31 of the year you turn 71. Many people convert earlier to start income. A direct transfer from an RRSP to a RRIF has no tax withheld.

The minimum withdrawal. Each year after the year you open it, you must take at least the January 1 value multiplied by a prescribed factor. At 70 or younger the factor is 1 divided by (90 minus your age), which is 4.00% at 65 and 5.00% at 70. From 71 the factor comes from a fixed chart: 5.28% at 71, 5.82% at 75, 6.82% at 80, 8.51% at 85, 11.92% at 90 and 20.00% at 95 and over. You can elect to use a younger spouse's age when you set up the RRIF, which lowers the minimum.

Tax. Growth stays tax-deferred, and every payment is taxable income. Tax is withheld only on the part of a payment above the minimum, at the same 10%, 20% and 30% rates as an RRSP lump sum outside Quebec. RRIF income can qualify for the federal pension income amount of up to $2,000 at 65 or older, and up to 50% of it can be split with a spouse when the transferring spouse is 65 or older.

No maximum. A RRIF has no withdrawal cap, so you can take more in a low-income year. Higher withdrawals can push net income above the 2026 OAS recovery threshold of $95,323, above which 15% of the excess is clawed back from your OAS.

6. LIRA and LIF: Locked-In Pension Money

What a LIRA is. A locked-in retirement account holds pension money moved out of a registered pension plan, usually when you leave an employer. Pension law, not just tax law, governs it, so the money is locked in: you generally cannot withdraw it as cash before retirement, and you cannot add new contributions. Some pension laws allow limited early access in set situations; the rules depend on which pension law applies to your plan.

What a LIF is. A life income fund is the payout stage, much as a RRIF is for an RRSP. Under federal pension rules, a LIF must pay at least the same minimum as a RRIF, using the same factors, and before age 90 it also has a maximum set by a formula. From the year you turn 90 there is no maximum. Provincially regulated LIFs follow each province's pension law, which sets its own maximums.

Tax. A LIRA and a LIF are taxed like an RRSP and a RRIF: growth is tax-deferred and payments are taxable income. LIF payments qualify for pension income splitting when the transferring spouse is 65 or older. Because both are retirement arrangements, US-listed shares held directly in them are exempt from US dividend withholding under the Canada-US tax treaty.

Protection. At a CIPF member dealer, an RRSP, RRIF and LIF fall within one combined $1,000,000 retirement-account coverage group, separate from the $1,000,000 for general accounts and the $1,000,000 for RESPs. Our listing of LIRA accounts shows where you can hold one.

7. Non-Registered Accounts: No Limits, Taxed Every Year

What it is. A non-registered account is an ordinary investment account at a broker, bank or robo-advisor, held in one name or jointly. A cash account holds only what you deposit; a margin account lets you borrow against your investments. There is no contribution limit, no deadline and no restriction on withdrawals, which makes it the place for money after your registered room is used.

Interest. Interest is added to your income every year at your full marginal rate, whether or not you withdraw it. For an Ontario resident with $75,000 of taxable income in 2026, that rate is 29.65% (20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario), so a 5% interest rate keeps 3.5175% after tax.

Dividends. Dividends from Canadian companies are grossed up and then reduced by a dividend tax credit. Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and earn a federal credit of 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount; other than eligible dividends are grossed up by 15% with a 9.0301% federal credit. Provinces add their own credits.

Capital gains and losses. Only part of a capital gain is taxed, and only when you sell, so tax on growth can be deferred for years. A net capital loss can be carried back three years or forward with no time limit, against capital gains only. The superficial loss rule denies a loss if you or an affiliated person buy the same property within 30 days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days later.

US dividends. The 15% US withholding is not lost here. You report the full dividend and claim a foreign tax credit, which is the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. The capital gains tax calculator estimates the tax on a sale.

Registered vs non-registered in one sentence. Registered accounts trade limits and rules for tax savings; a non-registered account trades tax savings for freedom. Most investors use both: registered room first, then a non-registered account for the rest.

TFSA vs RRSP vs FHSA. All three shelter growth. The FHSA is deductible going in and tax-free coming out for a first home, so a first-time buyer usually gets the most from it per dollar. The RRSP is deductible but taxed on the way out, so it works best when your tax rate is higher now than in retirement. The TFSA is never deductible and never taxed, which makes it the most flexible. When your tax rate is the same now and later, an RRSP and a TFSA give the same after-tax result.

A common order, not a rule. Many Canadians work through their accounts in roughly the order below. It is general information. Your workplace plan, debts, benefits and tax rate can change the order, and the account chooser at the top of this page shows how your goal and income shift it.

  1. Build an emergency fund in a savings account or a TFSA holding cash or GICs.
  2. Pay down high-interest debt, which costs more than most investments earn.
  3. Collect any employer matching in a group RRSP or pension plan.
  4. If you are a first-time buyer, open an FHSA and contribute up to $8,000 a year.
  5. Split new savings between a TFSA and an RRSP based on your tax rate now and later; the account chooser treats a marginal rate of 30% or more as the point where the RRSP deduction becomes more valuable.
  6. If you have children, contribute at least $2,500 a year per child to an RESP to collect the full basic grant.
  7. Once registered room is used, invest the rest in a non-registered account.

This example is hypothetical. Leah, 30, lives in Ontario, earned $75,000 in 2025 and $75,000 in 2026, has no workplace pension and has never owned a home.

  1. Her new 2026 room: TFSA $7,000, RRSP $13,500 (18% x $75,000) and FHSA $8,000, for $28,500 in total ($7,000 + $13,500 + $8,000).
  2. Deducting $8,000 of FHSA contributions cuts her 2026 tax by $2,522.00: $2,372.00 at her 29.65% combined rate ($8,000 x 29.65%) plus $150 of Ontario Health Premium.
  3. Deducting $13,500 of RRSP contributions on top cuts it by a further $3,660.22, because part of that deduction falls into lower brackets.
  4. Her total tax saving is $6,182.22 ($2,522.00 + $3,660.22), or 28.75% of the $21,500 deducted ($6,182.22 / $21,500).
  5. The $7,000 she can put in her TFSA earns no deduction, but it can come out tax-free at any time.

11. Where to Open Each Account

Online brokers. A self-directed account at an online broker lets you buy shares, exchange-traded funds, bonds and GICs yourself. All 13 online brokers we track offer the TFSA and RRSP. Two of them do not offer the FHSA, and several do not list RESPs, LIRAs or US dollar registered accounts. Costs to compare are trading commissions, currency conversion and any account fees. Our online broker comparison lists each one's published pricing and account types.

Robo-advisors. A robo-advisor builds and rebalances a portfolio for you for a yearly management fee on top of the funds' own costs. It suits investors who want the account handled for them. See the robo-advisor comparison for fees at different balances.

Banks and credit unions. Banks, credit unions and trust companies offer registered savings accounts and GICs, and branch advisors sell mutual funds. Eligible deposits and GICs at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per category, and each registered account type is its own category. Credit unions use provincial deposit insurance instead.

Safety checks. Investment dealers in Canada are overseen by CIRO, the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization, and CIPF members are CIRO members. CIPF protects up to $1,000,000 of missing property in general accounts (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA combined), a separate $1,000,000 for retirement accounts and another for RESPs, if a member firm fails. It does not cover market losses or crypto assets.

Types of Investment Accounts in Canada: FAQ

What are the main types of investment accounts in Canada?

The main registered accounts are the TFSA, the RRSP and spousal RRSP, the FHSA for a first home, the RESP for education, the RRIF for retirement income, and the locked-in LIRA and LIF for pension money. The non-registered account has no limits and no tax shelter. Each is a wrapper: you choose the savings, GICs, funds or shares held inside.

What is the difference between registered and non-registered accounts?

Registered accounts are registered with the Canada Revenue Agency and shelter growth from tax, in return for contribution limits, deadlines and withdrawal rules. A non-registered account has no limits, but interest and dividends are taxed every year and capital gains are taxed when you sell. Most people fill registered room first and use a non-registered account for the rest.

Should I use a TFSA or an RRSP first?

It depends on your tax rate now compared with in retirement. An RRSP deduction is worth more when your rate is high now and expected to be lower later. A TFSA suits lower incomes and anyone who wants flexible, tax-free withdrawals. When the two rates are equal, both give the same after-tax result. Many people use both.

Can I have a TFSA, RRSP and FHSA at the same time?

Yes. Each has its own room, so in 2026 you can contribute up to $7,000 to a TFSA, up to 18% of your 2025 earned income (maximum $33,810) to an RRSP, and $8,000 to an FHSA if you are a first-time buyer. You can also use the FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan for the same home.

What are the 2026 contribution limits?

For 2026: TFSA $7,000, with $109,000 of total room since 2009 for someone eligible every year; RRSP 18% of 2025 earned income up to $33,810; FHSA $8,000 a year and $40,000 for life; RESP $50,000 per child for life, with no annual limit. The 2027 RRSP dollar limit is $35,390; the 2027 TFSA limit has not been published yet.

Which accounts avoid US withholding tax on dividends?

RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs are exempt from US withholding on dividends from US-listed securities held directly, under the Canada-US tax treaty. A TFSA, FHSA or RESP has 15% withheld with a W-8BEN, and it cannot be recovered. In a non-registered account the 15% is withheld but can be claimed as a foreign tax credit.

Are my accounts protected if my provider fails?

Eligible deposits and GICs at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per category, and each registered account type is a separate category. At a CIPF member investment dealer, missing property is covered up to $1,000,000 for general accounts, $1,000,000 for retirement accounts and $1,000,000 for RESPs. Neither covers drops in market value.

What happens to my RRSP when I turn 71?

December 31 of the year you turn 71 is the last day to contribute to your own RRSP and the deadline to withdraw it, transfer it to a RRIF or buy an annuity. A direct transfer to a RRIF has no tax withheld. From the next year, the RRIF must pay out a minimum each year, starting at 5.28% of its January 1 value if you are 71 on that date.

Can I hold stocks and ETFs in a TFSA or RRSP?

Yes. Qualified investments include cash, GICs, most shares, options, ETF and REIT units listed on a designated stock exchange, mutual and segregated funds, government savings bonds and investment-grade debt. The TFSA and RRSP follow generally the same list. A non-qualified or prohibited investment in a TFSA is taxed at 50% of its value.

How do I move an account to another provider?

Ask the new provider to request a direct transfer from the old one. For a TFSA, a direct transfer does not use any contribution room, while withdrawing and redepositing yourself counts as a new contribution and can cause an over-contribution taxed at 1% a month. Transfer-out fees vary by provider, and some new providers cover them.
