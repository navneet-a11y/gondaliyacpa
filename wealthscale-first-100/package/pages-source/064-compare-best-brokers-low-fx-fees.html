2. How Brokers Charge for Currency Conversion

Spread. Most brokers do not send a bill for converting. They give you a worse exchange rate than the one they get, and keep the difference. That difference is the spread. TD, CIBC, RBC, BMO and National Bank publish their spreads in tables that fall as the amount rises.

Markup on a reference rate. Questrade, Wealthsimple and Webull charge a flat 1.5%. Wealthsimple applies 1.5% on top of its own corporate exchange rate; Webull adds a 1.5% markup to the exchange rate. CIBC describes its spread as being over the Bank of Canada rate.

Commission with no markup. Interactive Brokers converts at the market quote and adds no markup. Instead it charges a commission of 0.20 basis points of the amount, with a US$2 minimum per order. Its cost is a separate commission, not a change to the exchange rate.

Basis points. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Bank brokers quote their spread in basis points of the exchange rate, then give an approximate percentage of the amount converted. CIBC's 225 basis points, for example, is shown as about 1.6%, and National Bank's 230 basis points as about 1.70%. We price conversions with the percentage each broker gives, which is why their costs are labelled "about".

Principal and preferred rates. Desjardins says it acts as principal, meaning it trades the currency with you from its own position, and earns a margin that depends on the amount. moomoo charges a 0% fee but converts at its Preferred Rate, which includes a spread of undisclosed size. In both cases, the cost is inside the rate and is not published as one figure.

Rates on dividends. A US dividend paid into a Canadian-dollar account is converted too. RBC publishes a separate rate for dividends and interest, 60 basis points as of March 9, 2026. Wealthsimple applies its currency fee when US-dollar dividends are reinvested in a non-USD account. The other brokers do not publish a separate dividend rate.

How it works. Norbert's Gambit converts currency through a security that trades in Canadian and US dollars. The common tool is the US Dollar Currency ETF, DLR in Canadian dollars and DLR.U in US dollars. We name the pair only to explain the mechanism; it is not a recommendation. 9. How to Check a Rate a Broker Does Not Publish

Why it matters. Four brokers on this page do not publish a rate we can price: moomoo (a 0% fee with a spread of undisclosed size), Qtrade (no rate published), Scotia iTRADE (no rate on its pricing page) and Desjardins (a margin that depends on the amount, up to 1.90%). You can still find out what you paid after a conversion, and ask before the next one.

Step 1: read the confirmation. Every conversion shows the amount you gave and the amount you received. Divide one by the other to get the rate you were given.

Step 2: compare with a reference quote taken at the same moment. The cost is the gap between the rate you got and a reference rate for the same time, as a share of the reference amount. This example is hypothetical: if a reference quote would have turned $10,000 into US$7,000 and you received US$6,895, you paid US$105, or 1.5% of US$7,000 (105 divided by 7,000 = 0.015).

Step 3: compare with the gambit's implied rate. Dividing the US-dollar price of DLR.U by the Canadian-dollar price of DLR at the same moment gives the exchange rate built into the interlisted pair. It is a convenient check because both lines are quoted during market hours.

Step 4: ask in writing. Please ask the broker to state its conversion spread or margin for your amount in writing before you convert a large sum. A written answer lets you compare it with the published rates on this page and gives you something to point to if the next conversion costs more.

Where to read more. The table of brokers without a single published rate, near the top of this page, quotes what each of the four does publish. Our moomoo review and Qtrade review cover the rest of their pricing.

11. How to Choose a Low-Cost Way to Convert

The lowest FX fee broker Canada investors can pick on paper is Interactive Brokers, but the lowest cost for you depends on your amounts, your accounts and how often money comes back. These steps put the tables above to work.

  1. Write down your pattern. How much will you convert in a year, in how many pieces, and will dividends or sale proceeds need to come back to Canadian dollars?
  2. Price one conversion at your size. Use the calculator at the top of this page or the four-amount table in section 3. Note which band your amount falls into at each broker.
  3. Add the round trip if money returns. If you cannot keep US dollars, double the one-way cost.
  4. Check US-dollar accounts and their cost. A no-fee US-dollar TFSA or RRSP removes most repeat conversions. A paid one has to save more than its fee.
  5. Compare with Norbert's Gambit. At a broker with $0 ETF trades, the gambit's published cost is $0 plus spreads. Use the gambit calculator above with your own spread estimate.
  6. Weigh everything else. Conversion is one cost among several. Check commissions, account fees and transfer costs in the US-stocks ranking before you move.

Three quick rules. Small and frequent: a low fixed minimum beats a percentage. Large and rare: compare bands, and Wealthsimple's USD account at $100,000 or more. Long-term US holdings: hold US dollars where it costs nothing and convert as little as possible. 12. Common Currency Conversion Mistakes

Judging a broker by its commission. A $0 trade that converts at 1.5% costs $150.00 on $10,000. A US$1 trade with a US$2 conversion costs US$3.00. The currency line is usually the larger cost.

Converting in small pieces at a tiered broker. Bands apply per transaction, so splitting one large conversion can push every piece into a higher band. Section 3 shows what that costs at TD and in Wealthsimple's USD account.

Paying the fee twice. Selling US shares in a Canadian-dollar account and buying again later pays the fee in both directions. The round-trip table in section 4 shows the cost at each broker.

Missing the US-dollar band rule. BMO and National Bank set bands in US dollars, so a Canadian-dollar amount that looks like $25,000 is under US$25,000 and still pays their top rate. Section 3 works through the figures.

Treating 0% as free. moomoo's 0% fee still converts at a rate that includes a spread of undisclosed size. A 0% fee and a 0% cost are not the same thing.

Running Norbert's Gambit below break-even. At brokers that charge commission, the gambit loses money on small amounts, and at Wealthsimple it adds a $9.95 journaling fee to a USD-account conversion that is already 0% at $100,000. Section 8 lists each break-even.

Forgetting the USD account fee. A $10-a-month USD account on Wealthsimple Core that you use for a single $5,000 conversion a year costs $120 before tax and saves $0 on the conversion itself, since both routes charge 1.5% under $10,000.

10 Strategies to Pay Less to Convert Currency

1. Convert in one piece

Bands apply per transaction. If you will invest $25,000 in US stocks this year anyway, one conversion lands in a lower band at TD, CIBC, RBC and Wealthsimple's USD account than several smaller ones. At BMO and National Bank the lower bands start at US$25,000.

2. Hold US dollars once converted

A US-dollar account stops dividends and sale proceeds from converting back. At 1.5%, every $10,000 that does not make the return trip saves $150.00.

3. Use a fixed minimum for small amounts

At US$2 per conversion, Interactive Brokers costs 0.2% on $1,000. A 1.5% broker charges $15.00 on the same amount. Small, regular conversions favour a low fixed fee.

4. Use the gambit where trades cost $0

At Questrade, National Bank, BMO and Webull, Canadian ETF trades cost $0, so Norbert's Gambit replaces the full published rate with bid-ask spreads. Confirm the US-dollar side first.

5. Watch band edges

At Wealthsimple, $25,000 inside a USD account costs $125.00 while $24,000 costs $240.00. At TD, $25,000 costs $195.00 while $24,999 costs about $314.99. A little more in one go can cost less.

6. Check the currency of the bands

BMO and National Bank set bands in US dollars. A Canadian-dollar amount near US$25,000 may fall in the higher band. Convert enough to clear the line if you are close.

7. Let dividends stay in US dollars

A DRIP or a US-dollar cash balance keeps dividends from converting. If dividends must convert, RBC publishes a lower 60 basis point rate for dividends and interest.

8. Pay for a USD account only if it earns its keep

Wealthsimple's $120-a-year Core fee pays for itself on a single conversion of $24,000 or on avoided round trips. If you convert $5,000 once a year and never sell, it costs more than it saves.

9. Measure what you actually paid

Use your confirmation and a reference quote to work out your real rate, as in section 9. It takes two minutes and tells you whether your broker's unpublished rate is competitive.

10. Revisit after big balance changes

Reaching $100,000 in Wealthsimple assets makes its USD account included and its 0% band reachable. Large balances also reach lower bands at bank brokers. Recheck your options when your balance changes a lot.

A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Bank brokers quote their spread in basis points of the exchange rate and give an approximate percentage of the amount. CIBC's 225 basis points works out to about 1.6% of the amount, and National Bank's 230 basis points to about 1.70%.

Does Wealthsimple charge 1.5% on every US trade?

Yes, when the trade is placed from a Canadian-dollar account, on every tier. Inside a USD account, conversions are tiered by transaction size: 1.5% under $10,000, 1.0% from $10,000, 0.5% from $25,000 and 0% from $100,000, and trades from the USD account carry no conversion fee. The USD account costs $10 a month plus tax on Core.

Does moomoo really charge nothing to convert?

moomoo charges a 0% currency exchange fee, waived until further notice. But it converts at its Preferred Rate, which includes a spread of undisclosed size, so a conversion still has a cost built into the rate. Because the spread is not published, we cannot price it or rank moomoo by conversion cost.

Why are Desjardins, Qtrade and Scotia iTRADE not ranked?

None publishes a single rate we can price. Desjardins publishes a margin that depends on the amount, with a lowest figure of 0.15% and a highest of 1.90%. Qtrade states only that it may earn revenue on conversions. Scotia iTRADE shows no spread on its pricing page. They appear in a separate table with their exact wording.

Do I pay the conversion fee again when I sell?

Yes, if the money comes back to Canadian dollars. Selling a US stock in a Canadian-dollar account, or receiving a US dividend there, converts at the broker's rate. A round trip on $10,000 costs $300.00 at 1.5%. Holding US dollars in an account that allows it avoids the second conversion until you choose to make it.

Does Norbert's Gambit beat the lowest FX fee broker?

Usually not at Interactive Brokers, where a direct conversion costs US$2 up to US$100,000 and the gambit adds two commissions of at least CAD 1.00 each plus bid-ask spreads. At brokers that charge 1.5% or more and $0 for Canadian ETF trades, the gambit replaces the whole published fee with bid-ask spreads: $150.00 to $170.00 of published-fee saving on $10,000.

Is Interactive Brokers' US$2 minimum per conversion or per trade?

Per conversion order. Interactive Brokers charges 0.20 basis points of the trade value on each currency conversion, with a minimum of US$2.00 per order. Stock trades are charged separately: on its fixed plan, US$0.005 a share with a US$1 minimum on US stocks and CAD 0.01 a share with a CAD 1 minimum on Canadian stocks.
