1. Start With the Right Account: TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, RESP and RRIF

The account comes first. A TFSA, RRSP or FHSA is a tax wrapper, not an investment. You pick the account for its tax rules, then a platform to hold it, then the investments to put inside. Choosing the platform first can leave you in the wrong account. Our guide to the types of investment accounts in Canada compares every account side by side.

TFSA. Any resident of Canada who is 18 or older (19 where that is the age of majority) and has a social insurance number can open one. The 2026 limit is $7,000, and unused room carries forward with no end date, so someone who has been 18 or older and resident every year since 2009 and has never contributed has $109,000 of room. Withdrawals are tax-free and come back as new room on January 1 of the next year. An over-contribution is taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess.

RRSP. Your 2026 deduction limit is 18% of your 2025 earned income, up to $33,810, minus any pension adjustment, plus unused room from earlier years. The 2027 dollar limit is already set at $35,390. Contributions lower your taxable income, and withdrawals are taxed as income. The Home Buyers' Plan lets a first-time buyer take out up to $60,000 without tax and repay it over 15 years. Please check your latest notice of assessment for your own limit before you contribute.

FHSA. A first-time home buyer can contribute $8,000 a year, up to $40,000 for life, and room starts only once the first FHSA is opened. Contributions are deductible like an RRSP, and a qualifying withdrawal for a first home is tax-free. The contribution deadline is December 31, with no 60-day period. Whether the FHSA beats the TFSA for a down payment depends mostly on your tax rate.

RESP and RRIF. An RESP can hold up to $50,000 per child over its life. The Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year, up to $500 a year and $7,200 per child. At the other end of life, by the end of the year you turn 71 you must withdraw your RRSP, transfer it to a RRIF or use it to buy an annuity, and a RRIF must pay out a minimum each year: 5.28% of its January 1 value at age 71.

9. Learn to Invest: A Beginner's Path

Order matters more than picks. Doing sensible things in the wrong order can be costly: investing money you need next month, or carrying high-interest debt while earning less on savings. The steps below are the order our investing for beginners in Canada hub follows. They are general information, not a plan for your situation.

  1. Keep an emergency fund in a high-interest savings account you can reach any day.
  2. Pay off high-interest debt, which can cost more than investments earn.
  3. Choose the account for your goal: a TFSA for flexibility, an RRSP for retirement, an FHSA for a first home or an RESP for a child.
  4. Choose how you want to invest: pick investments yourself at an online broker, or let a robo-advisor build a portfolio for a yearly fee.
  5. Compare the total yearly cost, including currency conversion and account fees, not only commissions.
  6. Choose investments that match when you need the money and how large a fall you can live with; funds that hold many companies spread the risk.
  7. Contribute on a schedule, and keep your slips and statements for tax time.

Fees compound too. On $50,000, Questwealth Portfolios costs $185 a year and CI Direct Investing $415, a difference of $230 a year ($415 minus $185) before any growth on the money saved. The gap grows with your balance, which is why every comparison on WealthScale shows the yearly cost in dollars.

Moving an account. Move registered money by a direct transfer between institutions, never by withdrawing it and depositing it again. A direct TFSA transfer does not use any room, while a withdrawal and redeposit in the same year counts as a new contribution and can trigger the 1% a month over-contribution tax. Many brokers charge $150 to transfer a full account out, and some cover the fee your old broker charges on larger transfers: Wealthsimple, for example, reimburses up to $150 per account on transfers of $25,000 or more.
