TFSA Guide: How a Tax-Free Savings Account Works

A TFSA (tax-free savings account) lets a Canadian resident aged 18 or older invest after-tax money, let it grow, and take it out without paying tax on the growth. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and someone who has been 18 or older and resident in Canada every year since 2009 has $109,000 of total room if they have never contributed. Room you do not use carries forward with no expiry, and anything you withdraw is added back to your room on January 1 of the next year. This guide covers every rule: who can open one, how room builds, what you can hold, US stocks, over-contributions and what happens at death.

1. What a TFSA Is and Who Can Open One

The basic idea. A tax-free savings account is a registered account with its own set of tax rules. You put in money that has already been taxed, and you get no deduction for it. In return, the interest, dividends and capital gains earned inside the account are not taxed, and you can take money out at any time without tax. Despite the name, a TFSA is not only a savings account. It can hold cash, GICs, stocks, ETFs, bonds and mutual funds.

Three conditions to open one. You must be a resident of Canada for tax purposes, be 18 or older, and have a valid social insurance number. All three have to be true when you open the account. Banks, insurance companies, credit unions and trust companies can issue TFSAs, and most online brokers and robo-advisors offer them as well.

The age of majority. A TFSA is a contract, and in some provinces and territories you must be 19 to sign one. If you live in one of those places, you open your TFSA at 19, and the room from the year you turned 18 is still yours. Nothing is lost by waiting the extra year.

Room belongs to you alone. Your contribution room is personal. You can open several TFSAs at different institutions, but they all draw on one combined room figure. Your spouse or partner has separate room of their own.

No strings on how you spend it. A TFSA is not tied to retirement, a first home or a child's education. There are no conditions on what you do with a withdrawal. That freedom is the main difference from the RRSP, the FHSA and the RESP, which section 12 compares side by side. 2. TFSA Limit 2026 and Every Annual Limit Since 2009

The 2026 limit. The TFSA dollar limit for 2026 is $7,000. It has been $7,000 since 2024. Every resident of Canada who is 18 or older at some point in 2026 gets this $7,000 of new room.

How the number is set. The limit is indexed to inflation and then rounded to the nearest $500. Because of the rounding, the limit moves only when inflation pushes the indexed amount past the next $500 step. That is why it held at $5,500 from 2016 to 2018 and at $6,000 from 2019 to 2022, then rose to $6,500 in 2023 and $7,000 in 2024.

The 2015 exception. The limit was $10,000 for 2015 only, and it went back to $5,500 in 2016. Anyone who was 18 or older and resident in 2015 received the full $10,000 of room, whether or not they used it that year.

The 2027 limit. The 2027 TFSA limit has not been published yet. As of October 22, 2026, the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) limits table lists TFSA dollar limits only up to 2026. Any 2027 figure you see before CRA publishes it is an estimate. We will add the official number to this page when it is released.

3. TFSA Rules for Contribution Room: How It Builds and Carries Forward

The room formula. Your room for a year equals that year's dollar limit, plus any unused room from earlier years, plus everything you withdrew in the previous calendar year, minus what you contribute this year. Every TFSA rule about room comes back to this one line. Withdrawals made this year do not change this year's room.

When your room starts. Room starts in the year you turn 18, and the full dollar limit for that year counts, even if your birthday is on December 31. Someone born in 2008 turned 18 in 2026 and has $7,000 of room for 2026. No room exists for any year before 2009, when TFSAs began.

Unused room never expires. Room you do not use carries forward indefinitely. A person who opens a first TFSA at 50 gets every annual limit since they became eligible, not only the current year's amount.

Newcomers to Canada. If you arrive at 18 or older, your room starts on the day you become a resident, and only years of residency count. You get the full annual limit for the year you arrive, with no proration. CRA's own example: a person who became resident in 2024 had $7,000 of room for 2024, not the cumulative limits since 2009. Someone who becomes resident in 2026 has $7,000 for 2026, even if they landed in November.

Years outside Canada. No new room is added for a year in which you are a non-resident for the whole year. The year you leave still earns the full limit, because part-year residents get the full annual amount. Section 11 covers the other rules that apply while you live abroad.

Keep your own record. The room figure CRA holds is built from what your TFSA issuers report. A simple list of every contribution and withdrawal, with dates and the institution, lets you check that figure and catch mistakes early. The TFSA contribution room calculator rebuilds your room from those inputs.

4. Tax Treatment In, During and Out

Going in. TFSA contributions are not deductible. You contribute money that has already been taxed, so there is no refund the way there is with an RRSP. The benefit comes later, because nothing that comes out is taxed.

While the money is invested. Interest, dividends and capital gains earned inside a TFSA are not taxed and do not go on your tax return. Buying and selling inside the account has no tax effect. Three exceptions break the shelter: US withholding tax on US dividends (section 9), the special taxes on non-qualified or prohibited investments (section 7), and income CRA treats as business income from frequent trading (section 8).

Coming out. Withdrawals are not taxed and are not added to your income. That includes the growth, not only the money you put in. An RRSP withdrawal, by contrast, is fully taxable and has tax withheld at source.

What the shelter is worth. Interest is the most heavily taxed kind of investment income, because all of it is added to your income. 5. TFSA Withdrawal Rules: Timing and Re-Contributions

Any time, any reason. You can withdraw whenever you like and as often as you like, and the tax rules put no conditions on how you use the money. Your institution may need a few business days to sell investments and send the money, and a locked-in GIC may not be cashable before it matures, so plan ahead for large amounts.

Room comes back on January 1. The amount you withdraw is added to your room on January 1 of the next calendar year, at the same time as the new annual limit. It is not added back in the year you take it out. This one rule is behind many TFSA penalties.

The same-year trap. Suppose you have no room left, you withdraw $5,000 in June 2026, and you put it back in August 2026. The redeposit is a $5,000 over-contribution from August. If it stays until the room returns on January 1, 2027, the tax is $250 ($5,000 x 1% x 5 months, August to December). If you still have unused room, a redeposit up to that amount is fine.

Growth comes back too. The full amount you withdraw returns as room, including growth. If you contributed $10,000, it grew to $15,000 and you withdraw all $15,000, your room rises by $15,000 the next January 1. Losses work the other way: if $10,000 falls to $6,000 and you withdraw it, only $6,000 of room comes back.

Timing a planned withdrawal. If you know you will need money early next year, a withdrawal in December gets the room back on January 1, a few weeks later. The same withdrawal made in January waits almost a full year. Our full guide to TFSA withdrawal rules covers transfers, non-residents and more worked examples.

6. Over-Contributions and Penalties

The 1% monthly tax. If your contributions go over your available room, the excess is taxed at 1% for each month it stays in the account, based on the highest excess amount in each month. There is no grace amount. An RRSP allows a $2,000 cushion before its penalty applies, but a TFSA does not: a $1 excess is taxed.

How long it runs. The tax continues every month until you withdraw the excess or until new room on January 1 absorbs it. A month counts in full even if the excess existed for only part of it.

Reporting and paying. Please withdraw the excess as soon as you notice it. Then file the TFSA Return and pay the tax by June 30 of the following year. Leaving an excess in place to "wait for January" costs 1% for every month of the wait.

Contributions while non-resident. A separate 1% monthly tax applies to contributions made while you are a non-resident, until you withdraw them or become a resident again. If the same contribution also exceeds your room, the 1% excess tax applies on top, so the cost is 2% a month.

How excesses usually happen. Three patterns cause many of them: putting back a withdrawal in the same year, moving a TFSA between institutions by withdrawing and redepositing instead of a direct transfer, and contributing to two TFSAs at two institutions without adding them up. Automatic monthly contributions that keep running after your room is used are a fourth.

What it costs. A $2,000 excess left for 3 months costs $60 ($2,000 x 1% x 3). Use this calculator to see the tax on any excess and how much you save by removing it a month sooner.

7. What You Can Hold in a TFSA

Qualified investments. Most mainstream investments are allowed: cash, GICs and other deposits; most securities listed on a designated stock exchange, including shares, warrants, options, and ETF and REIT units; mutual funds and segregated funds; Canada and provincial savings bonds; debt of listed corporations; investment-grade debt; and insured mortgages. CRA says the list is generally the same as for an RRSP.

Non-qualified investments. Anything outside the qualified list is non-qualified. If your TFSA buys one, or an investment it holds stops qualifying, a tax of 50% of its fair market value applies. Income and gains on it are taxed in the hands of the trustee, and income left in the account instead of being withdrawn promptly can attract the 100% advantage tax.

Prohibited investments. A prohibited investment is one closely connected to you, for example shares of a company in which you hold a significant interest of 10% or more. It also carries the 50% tax, and any income or gain on it is taxed at 100% as an advantage. Shares of your own private company are a typical case.

Getting the 50% back. The 50% tax may be refunded if you dispose of the investment, or it stops being non-qualified or prohibited, before the end of the calendar year after the year the tax arose. There is no refund if you knew or should have known it did not qualify. The 100% advantage tax is never refunded.

The advantage tax. An advantage is a benefit or loan that exists only because of your TFSA, or a transaction that artificially shifts value into or out of it. CRA taxes any advantage at 100%, so the scheme gains nothing.

8. Frequent Trading: When TFSA Gains Become Business Income

What CRA says. CRA states that TFSA holders who invest with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have their account deregistered and the income earned taxed as business income. In that case the gains lose their tax-free status, and the tax shelter you thought you had is gone.

No numeric test. CRA gives no number of trades, holding period or dollar amount that triggers the rule. Each case turns on its facts. That uncertainty is the risk: you cannot count your way to safety.

Who is most exposed. The concern is a TFSA run like a trading business, with many short-term trades, active use of options and a large account built quickly from trading profits. Because CRA's wording mentions experience as well as frequency, people who work in the securities industry should take particular care.

Options and margin. Listed options are qualified investments, and some brokers allow covered calls, secured puts and long options inside a TFSA. Heavy options trading adds to the frequency side of CRA's test, so it raises the same business-income question.

A practical approach. A TFSA works best as a long-term account. If you want to trade actively, many investors keep that activity in a taxable account, where business income or capital gains are reported in the normal way, and leave the TFSA for buy-and-hold investing. 9. US Stocks and Foreign Investments in a TFSA

The withholding rate. The Canada-US tax treaty caps US tax on dividends paid to a Canadian resident at 15%, as long as your broker has a valid Form W-8BEN on file. Without the form, the US rate is 30%.

Why a TFSA pays it. Article XXI(2) of the treaty exempts dividends and interest earned by arrangements operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits. An RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF qualifies, so US-listed shares held directly in them have no US withholding. A TFSA, RESP and FHSA are not retirement arrangements, so the 15% is withheld. Inside a TFSA there is no Canadian tax for a foreign tax credit to offset, so the withholding is a permanent cost.

What it costs. This example is hypothetical. $10,000 of US shares paying a 2% dividend produces $200 a year ($10,000 x 2%), and $30 is withheld ($200 x 15%). In a taxable account you could claim a foreign tax credit for that $30, up to the Canadian tax on the same income. In a TFSA it is simply lost.

Currency conversion. Buying US shares with Canadian dollars means converting currency, and that fee can cost more than the withholding. Several brokers charge 1.5% on each conversion. Holding US dollars inside your TFSA avoids converting back and forth, but not every broker offers a USD side in a TFSA.

10. Multiple TFSAs, Transfers and Deposit Protection

More than one TFSA. You can hold TFSAs at several institutions at once, for example a savings TFSA at a bank and a self-directed TFSA at a broker. Your room is the total you can contribute to all of them combined, so every deposit anywhere counts against the same figure.

Direct transfers keep your room. To move a TFSA, open the new account and ask the receiving institution to transfer the old one directly. A direct transfer between your own TFSAs has no effect on your room. Most brokers in our data charge a transfer-out fee: eight of the 13 we track charge $150 to move a full account, while Wealthsimple lists $0 and moomoo $75 plus tax. Some receiving firms rebate the fee. Our guide on how to transfer a TFSA walks through the forms.

Do not do it yourself. Withdrawing from one TFSA and depositing at another is not a transfer. The deposit is a new contribution, and the withdrawal only comes back as room next January. With a full TFSA, a $40,000 move done this way creates a $40,000 excess that costs $400 a month.

CDIC deposit insurance. Eligible deposits held in a TFSA at a CDIC member, including savings balances, GICs and term deposits in Canadian or foreign currency, are insured up to $100,000. The TFSA is its own category, separate from deposits in your name, joint deposits, RRSPs, RRIFs, RESPs, RDSPs and FHSAs, and each member institution has its own coverage. CDIC does not cover stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds or crypto. Provincially regulated credit unions use provincial deposit insurance instead.

CIPF coverage at investment dealers. If a dealer that is a CIPF member fails and client property is missing, CIPF covers up to $1,000,000 for all your general accounts combined, a group that includes cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA accounts. Retirement accounts have a separate $1,000,000 limit. CIPF does not cover market losses or crypto assets. Investment dealers are overseen by the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO). Our guide to Wealthsimple's CIPF and CDIC coverage shows how this works at one platform.

11. Special Situations: Leaving Canada, Separation and Death

Leaving Canada. You can keep your TFSA after you become a non-resident, and withdrawals made while you live abroad are not taxed in Canada. The country you move to may tax the income under its own rules, so please get advice there before you leave. You earn no new room for any full year of non-residence, and a contribution made while non-resident is taxed at 1% a month until you withdraw it or return.

Coming back. Amounts you withdraw while non-resident are added back to your room only once you are a resident again. Room you had not used before you left is still there when you return.

Separation or divorce. A direct transfer from one partner's TFSA to the other's does not affect either person's room, as long as you live separate and apart and the transfer follows a court order or a written separation agreement. Done any other way, the receiving partner's deposit is an ordinary contribution.

Successor holder. Only a surviving spouse or common-law partner can be named successor holder. They become the new holder right away, the account keeps its tax-free status, and their own room is not affected as long as the deceased's TFSA had no excess. CRA notes that Quebec does not recognize successor holder designations for TFSAs.

Designated beneficiary. Anyone else you name, such as a family member, another person or an organization like a charity, is a designated beneficiary. They receive the account's value at the date of death tax-free, but earnings after the date of death are taxable. The account's exempt period ends on December 31 of the year after the death; a trust that continues past then becomes a taxable trust.

Survivor's exempt contribution. A surviving spouse or partner who receives a payment from the deceased's TFSA can contribute it to their own TFSA without using room. The deposit must be made by December 31 of the year after the death, and Form RC240 must be filed within 30 days of the contribution.

12. TFSA vs RRSP vs FHSA

The core trade-off. An RRSP and an FHSA give you a deduction now. A TFSA gives you no deduction but taxes nothing on the way out. Which comes first depends on your tax rate now, your expected rate later, and what the money is for.

When a TFSA tends to fit. Lower or middle incomes where a deduction is worth less, savings you may need before retirement, and retirees who want income that does not count toward the OAS recovery tax. Because withdrawals come back as room, a TFSA also works as a flexible reserve.

When the others tend to fit. An RRSP suits higher earners who expect a lower rate in retirement. An FHSA suits first-time buyers, because a qualifying withdrawal for a home is tax-free and the contribution was deductible. Many people use all three. 13. Where to Open a TFSA

Deposit TFSAs at banks and credit unions. A savings or GIC TFSA is the simplest kind. The balance earns interest and eligible deposits are covered by CDIC or provincial insurance. It suits an emergency reserve or money you need within a few years. Compare current rates on our high-interest savings accounts and GIC rates pages, and see GIC vs high-interest savings for the trade-off.

Self-directed TFSAs at online brokers. A brokerage TFSA lets you buy stocks, ETFs, bonds and GICs yourself. Commissions, currency conversion fees and transfer-out fees vary widely between brokers, and they compound over decades. Our best TFSA accounts in Canada list ranks the main platforms by a published score, and reviews such as the Wealthsimple TFSA review, the Questrade review and the Qtrade review go into each one.

Managed TFSAs at robo-advisors. A robo-advisor builds and rebalances a portfolio for a yearly management fee on top of the funds' own costs. It suits investors who want the TFSA invested without choosing holdings. Compare fees on our best robo-advisors page.

What to compare. Look at the full yearly cost for your pattern of trading, whether the TFSA can hold US dollars, the fee to leave, and whether the firm is a CDIC member for deposits or a CIPF member for investments. What You Need and What You Get

What you need to open a TFSA

  • A valid social insurance number
  • Residency in Canada for tax purposes
  • Age 18, or 19 where that is the age of majority
  • Government photo ID for the institution's identity check
  • A bank account to fund deposits and receive withdrawals
  • Your current room figure, from your own records or CRA
  • The name of a successor holder or designated beneficiary
  • A recent statement from any TFSA you plan to transfer in

What you get from a TFSA

  • No tax on interest, dividends or capital gains inside the account, apart from US withholding
  • Withdrawals at any time with no tax
  • Withdrawn amounts restored as room on January 1 of the next year
  • Unused room that never expires
  • Withdrawals that do not count toward income-tested benefits
  • A separate CDIC category for eligible deposits, up to $100,000 per member institution
  • CIPF coverage within the $1,000,000 general-accounts limit at member dealers
  • The option to pass the account to a spouse as successor holder

How a TFSA Works, Step by Step

  1. Check that you qualify. You need to be a resident of Canada, 18 or older (19 in some provinces), with a valid SIN.
  2. Work out your room. Add every annual limit since you turned 18 or became a resident, subtract contributions and add back withdrawals from earlier years. The TFSA calculator does this for you.
  3. Choose the kind of TFSA. Savings or GIC TFSA for short-term money, self-directed for choosing your own investments, managed for a ready-made portfolio.
  4. Open it and name who inherits it. Name your spouse or partner as successor holder, or name a beneficiary.
  5. Fund it. Deposit cash, or request a direct transfer from an existing TFSA so your room is not used twice.
  6. Invest and keep records. Note every contribution and withdrawal with its date, so you always know your room.
  7. Withdraw when you need to. Take money out tax-free, and wait until January 1 of the next year to put it back if you have no other room.

10 TFSA Strategies That Save You Tax

1. Contribute early in the year

New room arrives on January 1. Money invested in January is sheltered for 11 more months than money added in December, and that head start repeats every year. If a lump sum is not available, a monthly automatic contribution set to stop at your room figure gets most of the benefit.

2. Shelter fully taxed income first

Interest is added to your income in full, while Canadian dividends and capital gains get gentler treatment in a taxable account. If you hold both a TFSA and a taxable account, keeping interest-paying holdings such as GICs and savings inside the TFSA removes the most tax per dollar of room.

3. Put US dividend payers in an RRSP when you can

If you have both accounts, US-listed holdings that pay dividends keep the full dividend in an RRSP under the treaty exemption, while a TFSA loses 15%. Holdings that pay little or no dividend lose little to withholding, so they fit a TFSA better.

4. Recycle your RRSP refund

An RRSP contribution produces a refund at your marginal rate. Depositing that refund in your TFSA puts money the government returned to work where its future growth is never taxed, which turns one deduction into two kinds of shelter.

5. Rebalance inside the TFSA

Selling a winner in a taxable account creates a capital gain. Selling it inside a TFSA does not. When your mix drifts, make the trades inside the TFSA where you can, and let new money in the taxable account fill the gaps.

6. Give each TFSA a job

You may hold more than one TFSA under one room limit. Some people keep a savings TFSA for emergencies and a brokerage TFSA for long-term investing, so a sudden withdrawal never forces a sale of investments at a bad time.

7. Match the holding to the date you need the money

Money needed within a few years is usually kept in savings or GICs, where its value does not swing. Money for goals a decade or more away can take more risk for more growth. A loss inside a TFSA also shrinks the room that comes back when you withdraw.

8. Draw retirement income from the TFSA first in high-income years

RRIF withdrawals count as income and can push net income over the OAS recovery threshold of $95,323 for the 2026 income year. TFSA withdrawals do not count, so mixing the two lets a retiree control taxable income year by year.

9. Use the TFSA as a staging account

Savings built in a TFSA can later fund an FHSA or RRSP contribution, which earns a deduction. The TFSA withdrawal comes back as room the next January, so nothing is lost by parking the money there first.

10. Keep a one-page ledger

List every deposit and withdrawal by date and institution. Add the new limit each January 1, add last year's withdrawals and subtract this year's deposits. A ledger catches errors before they become a 1% monthly tax.

Illustrative Example: One Year of TFSA Activity

This example is hypothetical.

Maya was born in 1996 and has lived in Ontario all her life. She opens her first TFSA in March 2026 and earns $70,000 a year.

  1. Room earned. She turned 18 in 2014, so every limit from 2014 to 2026 counts: $83,500 ($5,500 for 2014 + $10,000 for 2015 + $16,500 for 2016 to 2018 + $24,000 for 2019 to 2022 + $6,500 for 2023 + $21,000 for 2024 to 2026).
  2. First deposit. In March 2026 she contributes $30,000. Room left for 2026: $53,500 ($83,500 - $30,000).
  3. Tax saved on income. Suppose the TFSA earns $1,200 of interest in 2026. She pays no tax on it. In a taxable account at her income, the same $1,200 would cost $355.80 ($1,200 x 29.65%).
  4. A withdrawal. In July she withdraws $8,000 for a car repair. Her room for the rest of 2026 stays at $53,500, because withdrawals return only next January 1.
  5. Putting money back. In October she deposits $8,000 again. Because she still had $53,500 of unused room, there is no excess. Room left: $45,500 ($53,500 - $8,000).
  6. January 1, 2027. Her room becomes $53,500 ($45,500 unused + $8,000 withdrawn in 2026), plus the 2027 limit once CRA publishes it.

Had Maya used all her room before July, the October redeposit would have been an $8,000 excess, costing $240 for October to December ($8,000 x 1% x 3).

TFSA FAQ

What is a TFSA and how does it work?

A TFSA is a registered account for Canadian residents 18 or older. You contribute money that has already been taxed, invest it in cash, GICs, stocks, ETFs or funds, and pay no tax on the growth or on withdrawals. Each year adds new room, $7,000 for 2026. Unused room carries forward, and amounts you withdraw are added back to your room on January 1 of the next year.

How much can I contribute to my TFSA in 2026?

The 2026 dollar limit is $7,000. Your own room is that $7,000 plus unused room from earlier years plus any withdrawals you made in 2025. Someone who was 18 or older and resident in Canada every year since 2009 and never contributed has $109,000. A room calculator using your birth year and history gives your exact figure.

What is the TFSA limit for 2027?

The 2027 TFSA limit has not been published yet. As of October 22, 2026, the Canada Revenue Agency limits table shows TFSA dollar limits only up to 2026. Any 2027 figure you see before CRA publishes it is an estimate. We will update this guide with the official limit when it is released.

Are TFSA withdrawals taxable?

No. Withdrawals from a TFSA are not taxed and are not added to your income, whether the money is your original contribution or growth. The amount you take out is added back to your contribution room on January 1 of the following year. Canada also does not tax TFSA withdrawals you make while living abroad as a non-resident.

Can I have more than one TFSA?

Yes. You can open TFSAs at as many institutions as you like, but they all share one contribution room. Add up deposits across every account before you contribute. To move money from one TFSA to another, ask the receiving institution for a direct transfer, which does not use any room.

What happens if I over-contribute to my TFSA?

You pay a tax of 1% per month on the highest excess amount in each month, until you withdraw the excess or new room arrives on January 1. There is no grace amount. Withdraw the excess as soon as you notice it, then file the TFSA Return and pay the tax by June 30 of the following year.

Can I hold US stocks in a TFSA?

Yes. US-listed shares and ETFs are qualified investments. US dividends paid into a TFSA have 15% withheld under the Canada-US tax treaty when a W-8BEN is on file, or 30% without one. A TFSA is not covered by the treaty's retirement-account exemption, and the withholding cannot be recovered as a foreign tax credit.

Can I day trade in a TFSA?

Trading is allowed, but CRA says holders who trade with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have the income taxed as business income and the account deregistered. CRA gives no trade-count test, so there is no safe number. A TFSA run like a trading business risks losing its tax shelter.

Do TFSA withdrawals affect OAS, GIS or the Canada child benefit?

No. TFSA withdrawals are not income, so they do not raise the net income used for the OAS recovery tax, the Guaranteed Income Supplement, the Canada child benefit or the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit. For the 2026 income year, the OAS recovery tax starts at $95,323 of net world income.

What happens to a TFSA when the holder dies?

If a spouse or common-law partner is named successor holder, they become the new holder and the account stays tax-free without using their own room. A designated beneficiary receives the value at the date of death tax-free, but earnings after death are taxable. The account's exempt period ends on December 31 of the year after the death.

Can a newcomer to Canada open a TFSA?

Yes, once you are a resident for tax purposes, 18 or older, and have a valid SIN. Your room starts on the day you become a resident, and you get the full limit for your arrival year with no proration. Years before you arrived do not count, so a 2026 arrival has $7,000 of room for 2026.

Is a TFSA better than an RRSP?

Neither is better for everyone. An RRSP gives a deduction now and taxes withdrawals later, so it tends to suit people who expect a lower tax rate in retirement. A TFSA gives no deduction but never taxes withdrawals, which tends to suit lower incomes, flexible goals and retirees managing income-tested benefits. Many people use both.

Is my TFSA protected if my bank or broker fails?

Eligible deposits and GICs in a TFSA at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 in their own TFSA category at each member institution. Investments at a CIPF member dealer are covered up to $1,000,000 for all general accounts combined if the firm fails and property is missing. Neither protects you against market losses.
