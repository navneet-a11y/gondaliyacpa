US Stocks in a TFSA: Withholding Tax and Currency Costs

Holding US stocks in TFSA accounts is allowed, and Canada does not tax the growth, but two costs follow every US holding. First, the United States keeps 15% of each dividend under the Canada-US tax treaty, or 30% if your broker has no W-8BEN on file, and a TFSA cannot get that tax back. An RRSP is exempt. Second, most brokers charge to turn Canadian dollars into US dollars: 1.5% at Wealthsimple, Questrade and Webull, and a US$2 minimum at Interactive Brokers. This guide puts both costs in dollars, shows which brokers offer a USD TFSA, and explains how to keep more of your return.

At a glance

Can a TFSA hold US stocks? Yes. Listed shares and ETFs, including those listed on US stock exchanges, are qualified investments for a TFSA. Canada does not tax the dividends or capital gains earned inside the account.

US withholding tax in a TFSA. The Canada-US tax treaty caps US tax on dividends paid to Canadian residents at 15% when a Form W-8BEN is on file, and the rate is 30% without one. In a TFSA this tax is a permanent cost: it cannot be claimed back as a foreign tax credit.

The RRSP difference. Article XXI(2) of the treaty exempts retirement arrangements. US-listed securities held directly in an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF have no US tax withheld on dividends once the broker has documented the account. A TFSA, FHSA and RESP do not qualify.

Currency conversion fees. As of October 22, 2026, converting $10,000 costs $150 at Wealthsimple (from a CAD account), Questrade and Webull (1.5%), $126 at TD Direct Investing (1.26%), and US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers, where the US$2 minimum applies.

USD TFSA accounts. Questrade lists no separate fee for US dollars in registered accounts. Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax for a USD account on its Core tier and includes it at Premium ($100,000+) and Generation ($500,000+). Qtrade charges US$15 a quarter per account unless you qualify for Investor Plus.

Your room is in Canadian dollars. The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000, and total room since 2009 is $109,000 for someone eligible every year who has never contributed. US holdings count at their Canadian-dollar value.

1. Can You Hold US Stocks in TFSA Accounts?

The short answer. Yes. A TFSA can hold cash, GICs, most listed securities (shares, options, ETFs and REITs), mutual and segregated funds, government savings bonds and certain bonds and debt. Shares and ETFs listed on US stock exchanges are listed securities, so they are qualified investments. You can buy a US company's shares inside a TFSA just as you would a Canadian company's shares.

What the TFSA shelters. Canada does not tax the interest, dividends or capital gains earned inside a TFSA, and withdrawals are tax-free. That part works the same for a US holding as for a Canadian one. If a US share you bought for $5,000 grows to $15,000, you can sell it and withdraw the $15,000 with no Canadian tax on the $10,000 gain.

What the TFSA does not shelter. The TFSA is a Canadian tax shelter. The United States does not treat it as a retirement account, so US tax is withheld from US dividends before the cash reaches your account. Your broker also charges a fee or a spread each time it converts Canadian dollars to US dollars. These are the two costs this guide puts in dollars.

Watch for holdings that are not listed. Shares of a private US company, or other assets not listed on a stock exchange, may not be qualified investments. A non-qualified investment is taxed at 50% of its fair market value when you acquire it or when it becomes non-qualified. The 50% tax may be refunded if you dispose of the holding by the end of the following calendar year, unless you knew or should have known it was non-qualified. Confirm the status of anything unusual with your broker before you buy it.

Where this fits in your plan. A TFSA works best for money that will grow for years. If you are new to the account, start with our TFSA guide, and see the guide to investment accounts in Canada for how it compares with the other registered plans.

2. US Dividends: TFSA Withholding Tax Explained

The treaty rate. Article X(2)(b) of the Canada-US tax treaty caps the US tax on dividends paid to a Canadian resident at 15%. A lower 5% rate exists only for a company that owns at least 10% of the voting stock of the payer, so it never applies to an individual investor. For a TFSA holding US shares, 15% is the rate to plan around.

Who takes the tax. You never write a cheque to the US government. The payer, usually through your broker acting as the withholding agent, keeps the tax before the dividend lands in your account. A US$100 dividend shows up as US$85 in cash. Your statement or trade history usually shows the gross dividend and the tax withheld on separate lines.

Why the tax is lost in a TFSA. In a taxable account, Canada lets you claim a foreign tax credit for US tax already paid. That credit reduces Canadian tax owing on the same income. Inside a TFSA there is no Canadian tax on the dividend to reduce, so there is nothing to claim the credit against. The 15% is gone for good.

How big the cost really is. The withholding is 15% of the dividend, not 15% of your investment. The yearly cost as a share of the holding's value is the dividend yield times 15%. On a US$10,000 holding, a 1% yield means US$100 of dividends and US$15 withheld (0.15% of the value). A 3% yield means US$300 of dividends and US$45 withheld (0.45%).

What this means for choosing holdings. A US company that pays little or no dividend loses little or nothing to withholding in a TFSA, and its growth is fully sheltered. A high-dividend US holding gives up more each year. That is why many investors place their highest-yielding US holdings in an RRSP when they have room, and use the TFSA for lower-yield US holdings. 3. Why the RRSP Is Exempt and the TFSA Is Not

The treaty article that matters. Article XXI(2) of the Canada-US tax treaty exempts dividends and interest earned by a trust or other arrangement that is generally exempt from tax in Canada and is operated exclusively to provide pension, retirement or employee benefits. The exemption is written for retirement plans, not for every tax-sheltered account.

Accounts that qualify. RRSPs, RRIFs and locked-in retirement accounts such as LIRAs and LIFs are retirement arrangements. US-listed securities held directly in them are exempt from US withholding on dividends once your broker has the account documented. A spousal RRSP is an RRSP, so it qualifies too.

Accounts that do not qualify. A TFSA, an FHSA and an RESP are not operated to provide retirement benefits. A TFSA can be used for anything, an FHSA is built for a first home, and an RESP is for education. None of them meets the Article XXI(2) test, so the 15% treaty rate (30% without a W-8BEN) is withheld, and the tax cannot be recovered inside the account. A non-registered cash or margin account also pays 15%, but there you can claim a foreign tax credit.

The condition people miss. The RRSP exemption applies to US-listed securities you hold directly. If your RRSP holds a Canadian-listed fund that owns US shares, the fund itself receives the US dividends, and the RRSP exemption does not reach through the fund. Section 6 explains this in plain terms.

Not a reason to skip the TFSA. The exemption makes the RRSP the better home for US dividend payers when you have RRSP room. It does not make the TFSA a poor home for US stocks in general. Gains are still tax-free, and many US companies pay small dividends. 4. W-8BEN for Your TFSA: 15% Instead of 30%

What the form does. Form W-8BEN is a US Internal Revenue Service form. A foreign person who is the beneficial owner of income subject to US withholding gives it to the withholding agent, which for most investors is the broker. The form certifies that you are not a US person and lets you claim the reduced treaty rate. You do not send it to the IRS yourself.

What happens without it. Most US-source income paid to a foreign person is taxed at 30% unless a treaty reduces it. If your broker has no valid W-8BEN for you, a US dividend in your TFSA can have 30% withheld instead of 15%. On US$1,000 of dividends, that is US$300 instead of US$150, an extra US$150 lost every US$1,000.

No W-8BEN on file30%US$300US$700

When you file it. Brokers that let you trade US securities collect the form, often as part of the application or the first time you enable US trading. Please check your account documents or ask your broker to confirm that a W-8BEN is on file before you buy a US dividend payer. Keep the details on the form current: a change of address or citizenship can make an old form invalid.

How to spot a problem. Look at the first US dividend after you buy. Divide the tax withheld by the gross dividend. If the result is 30% rather than 15%, contact your broker right away so a valid form can be put on file before the next payment.

The W-8BEN does not create the RRSP exemption. The form gets you from 30% to 15% in a TFSA, FHSA, RESP or taxable account. The 0% rate in an RRSP or RRIF comes from the treaty's retirement exemption and the broker's documentation of the account as a retirement plan. A W-8BEN cannot bring a TFSA below 15%.

The taxable account, step by step. In a non-registered account, you report the gross dividend as foreign income on your Canadian return and claim a foreign tax credit. The credit is the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income. You claim the federal part on Form T2209 (line 40500) and, outside Quebec, the provincial part on Form T2036. US dividends do not get the Canadian dividend tax credit, so they are taxed at your full marginal rate, like interest.

A worked comparison. This example is hypothetical. Take an Ontario resident with $70,000 of taxable income in 2026 and, for simplicity, a dividend worth $1,000 in Canadian dollars. Her combined marginal rate is 29.65% (20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario). Canadian tax on the dividend is $296.50 ($1,000 x 29.65%). That is more than the $150 of US tax, so the full $150 is credited (the credit is the lesser of the two). Net Canadian tax is $146.50 ($296.50 - $150), total tax is $296.50 ($150 US + $146.50 Canada), and she keeps $703.50.

What the comparison shows. In the TFSA she keeps $850, because Canada takes nothing. In the taxable account she keeps $703.50. In an RRSP the full $1,000 stays invested, though it is taxed as income when withdrawn. So the TFSA's 15% loss is real, but for this person a TFSA still beats a taxable account for the same US dividend. Use the capital gains tax calculator to see the taxable-account side for gains.

6. US-Listed, Canadian-Listed and Canadian-Wrapped Holdings

Three ways to own US companies. You can own US companies through a share or ETF listed on a US exchange and traded in US dollars. You can own them through a Canadian-listed fund that holds US shares directly. Or you can own them through a Canadian-listed fund that holds a US-listed ETF, often called a wrapped fund. The underlying companies can be the same. The tax path is different. This section explains the levels of withholding in general terms; it is not a recommendation of any fund.

US-listed holdings. The US dividend is paid to your account, so the withholding happens at your account. In a TFSA, 15% is lost. In an RRSP, the retirement exemption applies and nothing is withheld. In a taxable account, 15% is withheld and you claim it back as a foreign tax credit. You need US dollars to buy, so a currency conversion applies.

Canadian-listed funds holding US shares directly. The fund, not your account, receives the US dividends, so the 15% is withheld inside the fund before it pays distributions. That layer is lost in a TFSA and also in an RRSP, because the RRSP exemption does not reach through a Canadian fund. In a taxable account, the fund generally reports the foreign income and tax to you, so a foreign tax credit can usually be claimed. You buy in Canadian dollars, so there is no conversion at your broker.

Canadian-wrapped funds. A Canadian-listed fund that holds a US-listed ETF behaves like the previous case for US stocks: one 15% layer is withheld inside the structure. For funds that hold companies outside the US, wrapping can add layers: the foreign country may withhold tax on dividends paid to the US fund, and then the US may withhold 15% on the US fund's distributions to the Canadian fund.

What it means in a TFSA. For US companies, a TFSA loses one 15% layer whichever of the three structures you use. The choice then turns on the currency conversion cost, the fund's own fees and how you plan to trade. In an RRSP, a US-listed holding avoids the layer entirely, which is the main reason US-listed holdings are often kept in RRSPs. For international stocks outside the US, a US-listed fund in a TFSA can mean two layers of tax, so a structure with fewer layers tends to cost less. Read each fund's tax and distribution documents before you buy.

8. USD TFSA Accounts: Which Brokers Offer Them

What a USD TFSA is. A USD TFSA, sometimes called a dual-currency TFSA, lets the account hold US dollars as US dollars. You convert once, buy US-listed shares, keep the dividends and sale proceeds in US dollars, and convert back only when you choose. Without a US-dollar side, the broker converts on every purchase, sale and dividend.

9. Dividends, DRIPs and Currency Inside a TFSA

What happens to a US dividend. The US tax is withheld first. The rest is paid into your TFSA in US dollars if the account has a US-dollar side. If it does not, the broker converts the dividend to Canadian dollars at its rate. On a US$850 dividend (US$1,000 less 15%), a 1.5% conversion costs US$12.75 (US$850 x 1.5%). Small, but it repeats every quarter for as long as you own the shares.

Examples from broker pricing. Wealthsimple applies its currency fee when US-dollar dividends are reinvested in an account without a USD side. RBC Direct Investing converts US-dollar dividends and interest at a spread of 60 basis points, as of March 9, 2026, which is lower than its rate on trades under $25,000.

Dividend reinvestment plans. A DRIP uses each dividend to buy more of the same holding. Inside a TFSA the reinvested amount is not a new contribution, so it does not use room. DRIP terms differ by broker.

Full-share DRIPs leave cash behind. Where only whole shares are bought, the part of a dividend too small to buy a share stays as cash. In a CAD-only TFSA that leftover may also have been converted. Check your statements once a quarter so idle cash does not build up.

Keeping US cash in US dollars. If you plan to buy more US shares later, letting US dividends collect in the US-dollar side avoids converting them to Canadian dollars now and back to US dollars later, which would cost two conversions.

10. TFSA Room, Withdrawals and Over-Contributions With US Dollars

Room is a Canadian-dollar figure. The 2026 TFSA dollar limit is $7,000, and the 2027 limit has not been published yet. When you contribute US dollars, or move US shares into a TFSA in kind, the contribution counts at its value in Canadian dollars on the day it goes in. A US$5,000 contribution uses more or less room depending on the exchange rate that day. The TFSA contribution limit by year page lists every limit since 2009.

Withdrawals come back next year. Whatever you withdraw is added back to your room on January 1 of the following year, at the Canadian-dollar value of the withdrawal. If your US shares have grown, you can withdraw more than you put in and get the larger amount back as room. If they have fallen, the room you get back is smaller. Our TFSA withdrawal rules guide covers timing in full.

Currency moves count too. A US holding's Canadian-dollar value moves with both its share price and the exchange rate. A weaker Canadian dollar raises the value of US holdings in Canadian dollars, and a stronger one lowers it. Neither affects your room until you withdraw.

The over-contribution trap. The tax on an excess TFSA amount is 1% per month on the highest excess in each month, with no grace amount. The common US-investor mistake is moving a USD TFSA by withdrawing and redepositing. A withdrawal followed by a new deposit counts as a fresh contribution, so it can create an excess. A direct transfer between TFSAs has no effect on room. If you do over-contribute, the TFSA Return is due June 30 of the following year.

Transfer-out fees. Moving a TFSA costs $150 at Questrade, Qtrade, National Bank, TD Direct Investing, RBC and Scotia iTRADE, and $150 for a total transfer at Desjardins. moomoo charges $75 for a full transfer or $50 for a partial one, per currency, which matters for an account holding both. Wealthsimple charges $0. Our guide on how to transfer a TFSA or investment account explains the direct-transfer forms. Use the TFSA room calculator before any large deposit.

11. Frequent Trading, Options and Prohibited Holdings

Frequent trading can cost the shelter. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) says a holder who trades with the frequency and experience of a professional trader may have the income taxed as business income, and the account may be deregistered. CRA gives no trade-count test. Long US market hours make frequent trading easy: Wealthsimple, for example, offers trading in US-listed stocks and ETFs 24 hours a day, five days a week, from Sunday 8 pm to Friday 8 pm Eastern time, with extended-hours orders placed as limit orders.

Options are qualified investments. Listed options are on the list of qualified investments, so buying US-listed options inside a TFSA is allowed. The same business-income caution applies to heavy options trading. Costs vary: $0 per contract on US equity and ETF options at Wealthsimple and Questrade, US$0.65 per contract at Interactive Brokers (minimum US$1) and at moomoo, and US$0.99 at Webull. Options are also traded in US dollars, so the same conversion costs apply.

Prohibited investments. A prohibited investment is one closely connected to you, for example a 10% or greater interest in a company. It is taxed at 50% of its fair market value, and income or gains on it attract a 100% advantage tax. This matters mainly for founders and employees who hold large stakes in a US company and think about moving shares into a TFSA.

The advantage tax. The 100% advantage tax also applies to income from a non-qualified investment that is not withdrawn promptly. Combined with the 50% tax on the holding itself, these rules can wipe out the benefit of the TFSA, so check any unusual US holding before it goes into the account.

Losses inside a TFSA. A capital loss on a US share sold inside a TFSA cannot be claimed against gains elsewhere. In a taxable account, net capital losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, but only against taxable capital gains. Speculative US positions that may lose money are therefore not a natural fit for a TFSA, because the loss also shrinks the room you get back on withdrawal.

14. Common Mistakes With US Stocks in a TFSA

No W-8BEN on file. Without it, 30% of each US dividend can be withheld instead of 15%. Check the rate on your first US dividend.

High-yield US holdings in the TFSA while RRSP room sits empty. Each US$1,000 of dividends loses US$150 in the TFSA and nothing in a documented RRSP. If you have unused RRSP room, the RRSP is usually the better home for US dividend payers.

Paying for conversions twice. Buying US shares in a CAD-only TFSA, selling, and buying again means a conversion on every step. A round trip on $10,000 at 1.5% costs $300.

Thinking a Canadian-listed fund in an RRSP avoids withholding. The RRSP exemption applies only to US-listed securities held directly. A Canadian-listed fund that holds US shares pays the 15% inside the fund in every account.

Withdrawing and redepositing to change brokers. That counts as a new contribution and can trigger the 1% monthly tax. Use a direct transfer.

Assuming the TFSA makes US income fully tax-free. Canada does not tax it. The United States still takes 15% of dividends, and no form or account setting changes that inside a TFSA.

10 Strategies to Cut Tax and Fees on US Stocks in a TFSA

1. Fill the RRSP with US dividend payers first

A documented RRSP pays 0% US withholding on US-listed holdings held directly, while a TFSA pays 15%. If you have room in both, placing the higher-yield US holdings in the RRSP keeps the full dividend working. The TFSA then holds what gains least from the exemption.

2. Use the TFSA for growth

Withholding applies only to dividends. A US holding that pays little or nothing loses little or nothing to the 15%, while its growth is never taxed in Canada. That makes low-yield US holdings a natural fit for a TFSA when you have to choose.

3. Convert once, then stay in US dollars

A USD TFSA lets you convert one time and keep dividends and sale proceeds in US dollars. Each avoided round trip at 1.5% saves $300 on $10,000. Compare the monthly or quarterly USD fee with what you would pay in conversions.

4. Batch conversions to reach lower tiers

Rates fall on larger conversions at several brokers. Inside a Wealthsimple USD account, a conversion of $10,000 or more costs 1.0% instead of 1.5%, and $25,000 or more costs 0.5%. At bank-owned brokers, conversions of $25,000 or more (US$25,000 at BMO and National Bank) get a lower spread.

5. Consider Norbert's Gambit for large amounts

Buying an interlisted security in Canadian dollars and selling its US-dollar listing replaces the broker's conversion rate with trading costs and a short wait. It suits larger sums, since the steps and the market risk do not shrink with the amount. Our Norbert's Gambit guide has the details.

6. Keep US dividends in US dollars

In a CAD-only TFSA, each US dividend is converted at the broker's rate. Letting dividends collect in a US-dollar side and reinvesting them in US holdings avoids converting out and back in. On US$850 of dividends, 1.5% is US$12.75 a year that you keep.

7. Confirm your W-8BEN

A missing form doubles the withholding from 15% to 30%. On US$1,000 of dividends that is an extra US$150 a year. Check the rate on the first dividend after you buy, and update the form when your address or status changes.

8. Compare structures, not only holdings

For US companies, a US-listed holding and a Canadian-listed fund holding US shares both lose one 15% layer in a TFSA. The difference is the conversion cost and the fund's own fees. In an RRSP, the US-listed version avoids the layer.

9. Move a TFSA by direct transfer

A direct transfer between TFSAs has no effect on room. Withdrawing and redepositing counts as a new contribution and can create an excess taxed at 1% a month. Ask the new broker whether it covers transfer-out fees, which are $150 at several brokers.

10. Match the USD account fee to your balance

A $10 monthly fee is $120 a year before tax. That is 1.2% of a $10,000 account but 0.12% of a $100,000 one. On a small account, a broker with no separate USD fee, or converting less often, may cost less.

Illustrative Example: $50,000 of US Stocks in a TFSA

This example is hypothetical.

Priya has $50,000 of unused TFSA room and wants to invest it in US-listed shares. Her holdings will pay US$1,000 of dividends a year. She compares three ways to do it.

  1. Broker conversion in a CAD-only TFSA. At a broker charging 1.5%, converting $50,000 costs $750 ($50,000 x 1.5%).
  2. Wealthsimple Core with a USD account. Converting $50,000 inside the USD account costs 0.5%, or $250 ($50,000 x 0.5%). The USD account costs $120 a year before tax ($10 x 12). First-year cost: $370 ($250 + $120).
  3. Interactive Brokers. The conversion commission is 0.20 basis points, or $1.00 ($50,000 x 0.002%), which is below the minimum, so she pays US$2.00. Whether her TFSA there can hold US dollars was not published on the pages we checked, so she confirms it first.
  4. US withholding each year. With a W-8BEN on file, US$150 is withheld (US$1,000 x 15%) and US$850 is credited. Over 10 years at the same dividend that is US$1,500 (US$150 x 10). None of it can be recovered in a TFSA.
  5. Converting dividends in a CAD-only TFSA. At 1.5%, each year's US$850 costs US$12.75 to convert (US$850 x 1.5%), or US$127.50 over 10 years (US$12.75 x 10).
  6. Without a W-8BEN. US$300 a year would be withheld (US$1,000 x 30%), or US$3,000 over 10 years (US$300 x 10).
  7. In an RRSP instead. If the same holdings sat in a documented RRSP, nothing would be withheld, keeping US$1,500 more over 10 years. RRSP withdrawals are taxed as income, though, so Priya weighs her tax rate now against her rate in retirement.

Priya's lowest-cost route depends on whether her broker's TFSA holds US dollars. Converting once into a USD side and keeping dividends in US dollars removes the repeat conversions in step 5 and any conversion back to Canadian dollars on a sale, but nothing removes the 15% in step 4 while the holdings stay in a TFSA.

US Stocks in a TFSA FAQ

Can I hold US stocks in a TFSA?

Yes. Shares and ETFs listed on US stock exchanges are qualified investments for a TFSA, as are listed options. Canada does not tax the dividends or capital gains earned inside the account. The United States still withholds tax on US dividends, and your broker charges a fee or spread to convert Canadian dollars to US dollars.

How much US withholding tax is taken from dividends in a TFSA?

The Canada-US tax treaty caps the US tax on dividends paid to Canadian residents at 15% when your broker has a W-8BEN on file. Without the form, the rate is 30%. On US$1,000 of dividends, that is US$150 with the form or US$300 without it. The tax is withheld before the dividend reaches your account.

Can I get the US withholding tax back in a TFSA?

No. In a taxable account you claim a foreign tax credit against Canadian tax on the same income. Inside a TFSA there is no Canadian tax on the dividend, so there is nothing to claim the credit against. The 15% withheld in a TFSA is a permanent cost.

Why is an RRSP exempt from US withholding tax but a TFSA is not?

Article XXI(2) of the Canada-US tax treaty exempts arrangements operated exclusively to provide pension or retirement benefits. RRSPs, RRIFs, LIRAs and LIFs qualify, so US-listed securities held directly in them have no US tax withheld on dividends once the broker documents the account. A TFSA, FHSA and RESP are not retirement arrangements, so 15% is withheld.

Do I need a W-8BEN for my TFSA?

Yes, if you hold US dividend payers. The W-8BEN is the form a foreign beneficial owner gives the withholding agent, usually your broker, to claim the treaty rate. With it, US dividends in a TFSA have 15% withheld. Without it, 30% can be withheld. Please confirm with your broker that the form is on file before your first US dividend.

Are capital gains on US stocks in a TFSA taxed in Canada?

No. Canada does not tax capital gains earned inside a TFSA, whether the shares are Canadian or US. You can sell and withdraw the proceeds tax-free. The withholding tax covered on this page applies to US dividends. A loss on a US share inside a TFSA cannot be claimed against gains elsewhere.

Which brokers offer a USD TFSA?

As of October 22, 2026, Questrade, National Bank Direct Brokerage, Desjardins Online Brokerage, Wealthsimple, Qtrade, moomoo and TD Easy Trade state that a TFSA can hold US dollars. Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core, and Qtrade US$15 a quarter unless you have Investor Plus. Interactive Brokers, BMO, Webull and CIBC did not publish it.

What does it cost to convert Canadian dollars for my TFSA?

On $10,000, as of October 22, 2026: US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers, $126 at TD Direct Investing, $150 at Wealthsimple, Questrade and Webull, $160 at CIBC, RBC and BMO, and $170 at National Bank. moomoo charges no fee but has an undisclosed spread, Qtrade and Scotia iTRADE do not publish a rate, and Desjardins charges up to 1.90%.

Is a US-listed or Canadian-listed holding better for US stocks in a TFSA?

For US companies, both lose one 15% layer of US withholding in a TFSA: at your account for a US-listed holding, inside the fund for a Canadian-listed one. The difference is the conversion cost to buy in US dollars and the fund's own fees. In an RRSP the US-listed version avoids withholding. This is general information, not a recommendation.

Should US dividend stocks go in my TFSA or my RRSP?

If you have room in both, US-listed dividend payers usually keep more in an RRSP, where nothing is withheld once the account is documented. Lower-yield US holdings lose little in a TFSA and their growth is tax-free. If you have no RRSP room or expect a low retirement income, the TFSA can still be the better home.

Does contributing US dollars to a TFSA use more room?

Your room is measured in Canadian dollars. A US-dollar contribution, or US shares moved in kind, counts at its Canadian-dollar value on the day it goes in, so the exchange rate that day decides how much room it uses. The 2026 limit is $7,000. Withdrawals come back as room on January 1 of the next year.

Can I use Norbert's Gambit in a TFSA?

It can work in any account where your broker lets you buy an interlisted security in Canadian dollars, move it to the US-dollar side and sell it in US dollars. You need a TFSA that can hold US dollars. Wealthsimple lists Norbert's Gambit as available. Our Norbert's Gambit guide sets out the steps, costs and risks.
