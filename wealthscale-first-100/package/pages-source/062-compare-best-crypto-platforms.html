Only authorized platforms qualify. A platform can be ranked only if it appears on the Canadian Securities Administrators' list of authorized crypto trading platforms. Every qualifying platform then gets a sub-score from 0 to 5 on five factors, and we add the weighted results. Fees carry the most weight because they are charged on every trade. The weights are the same for every platform, and no platform can pay to change them. Our full rules are on how we rate brokers and platforms, and how WealthScale earns money is set out in our advertiser disclosure.

2. CSA Registered Crypto Exchange: What Authorization Means

The list that matters. The Canadian Securities Administrators publish a list of crypto trading platforms authorized to do business with Canadians. When we checked it on October 22, 2026, it showed a last-refreshed date of September 18, 2025. We re-check the list at each update and remove any platform that no longer appears on it. A platform that is not on the list should not be serving Canadians, however polished its app looks.

Two levels of registration. Among the nine platforms we rank, Wealthsimple, Newton, Shakepay and Robinhood Canada's Coinsquare are investment dealers that belong to CIRO, the national self-regulatory body for investment dealers. Coinbase Canada, Crypto.com and Kraken are restricted dealers, a narrower registration with conditions set by the securities regulators. Netcoins is registered with the British Columbia Securities Commission, and Ndax Canada Inc. is on the list with exchange and clearing relief from the Alberta and British Columbia commissions.

3. Crypto Trading Fees and Spreads Compared

Fee or spread. A platform can charge you in two ways. A fee is a stated percentage added to your trade. A spread is the gap between the price you pay to buy and the price you get to sell, built into the quote. Some platforms charge both, some only a spread. A published fee can be compared; an undisclosed spread cannot, unless you compare live quotes for the same amount at the same moment.

9. Taxes on Crypto in Canada: The General Rules

No tax shelter on these platforms. Crypto bought directly on a platform sits in a taxable, non-registered account. Wealthsimple, for example, states that crypto can be held only in its non-registered crypto account, not in a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA. Gains and losses therefore have to be reported on your return.

What counts as a disposition. The CRA treats selling crypto for dollars, trading one crypto asset for another, paying for goods or services with crypto, and giving crypto away as dispositions. Each one can create a gain or a loss, measured in Canadian dollars at the time of the transaction. Moving coins between two wallets you own is generally not a disposition, but keep records so your cost follows the coins.

Capital gain or business income. Most people who buy and hold report a capital gain or loss, and only part of a capital gain is included in income. Someone who trades often, holds for short periods and spends a lot of time on it may instead be carrying on a business, and business income is fully taxable. The CRA looks at the facts, such as how often you trade, how long you hold and your knowledge, and it applies the same idea to frequent trading inside a TFSA. There is no fixed number of trades that decides it.

Your adjusted cost base. For identical coins bought at different prices, your cost per coin is the average cost of all your purchases. Trading fees you pay to buy are added to your cost, and fees you pay to sell reduce your proceeds. The illustrative example below walks through the arithmetic with published fees.

Losses and the superficial loss rule. A capital loss can offset capital gains. If you, or a person affiliated with you such as your spouse, buy the identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after, the loss is denied and usually added to the cost of the replacement. A net capital loss you cannot use this year can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, and it offsets only taxable capital gains.

Staking rewards and records. Record the Canadian-dollar value of every staking reward on the day you receive it, along with each trade's date, amount, value and fees. Download your platform's transaction history at least once a year; if a platform closes, the records may be harder to get. Because the treatment of rewards and frequent trading depends on your facts, please speak with a tax professional if your crypto activity is large.

10. Crypto Platforms Canada Investors Should Treat With Care

Not on the CSA list. If a platform serving Canadians does not appear on the CSA list of authorized crypto trading platforms, do not open an account. Look for the exact company name, not just a brand, because the list names companies such as Payward Canada Inc. for Kraken and Foris DAX CAN ULC for Crypto.com.

Promised returns. Any offer of a fixed or guaranteed return on crypto, beyond a platform's published staking rate shown as "up to", is a warning sign. Staking rates on authorized platforms are variable and come with a stated fee.

Requests for your recovery phrase or remote access. No authorized platform needs your wallet recovery phrase, your one-time codes or control of your computer. Anyone who asks is trying to take your crypto.

Pressure to move off the platform. Scams often start on an authorized platform and then push you to send coins to another app or "trading desk" for a better return. Once crypto leaves a platform, it usually cannot be recovered.

No published fees or custody. Several authorized platforms publish only an undisclosed spread or no custody detail. That is legal, but it makes them harder to compare. Prefer platforms that publish a fee you can check and say how coins are held.

Celebrity and social media ads. Ads that use well-known names to promote a platform or coin are a common route for fraud. Check the platform against the CSA list yourself rather than following a link in an ad or message.

13. Common Crypto Platform Mistakes

Comparing only the headline fee. A 0% commission with an undisclosed spread can cost more than a published 0.5% fee. Compare live quotes for the same amount at the same moment on spread-only platforms.

Assuming CIPF covers your coins. It does not, at any platform. CIPF membership protects the cash in your account at member platforms that state it, not the crypto.

Making many tiny purchases. At Wealthsimple, a $50 market order carries a $1 surcharge on top of the 0.5% fee, or 2.5% in total. Use recurring buys or larger orders instead.

Not tracking cost base. Without records of every purchase, fee and swap, you cannot work out your gain or loss when you sell. Start a record with your first trade.

Forgetting that swaps are taxable. Trading one coin for another is a disposition even though no dollars reach your bank account. Report it the same way as a sale.

Sending to the wrong network. Coins sent on a network the receiving platform does not support can be lost. Always send a test amount first.

Staking money you may need. Lock-ups can stop you from selling for a period. Stake only coins you plan to hold anyway.

10 Strategies to Cut Crypto Costs and Risk

1. Use limit orders where spreads apply

At Wealthsimple, market orders pay 0.5% plus a spread, while limit orders have no spread. If you are not in a hurry, a limit order at a price you choose can cut the cost of each trade.

2. Use free recurring buys

Wealthsimple states its recurring crypto buys carry no fee or spread, and Shakepay's bitcoin recurring buys carry neither. Regular automatic purchases also spread your buying across different prices.

3. Avoid orders under $100 at Wealthsimple

The $1 surcharge on orders under $100 turns a 0.5% fee into 2.5% on a $50 order. Combine small buys into one larger order or a recurring plan.

4. Compare live quotes on spread-only platforms

Shakepay, Netcoins and Crypto.com's app do not publish their spread. Before a large trade, check the buy and sell quotes for the same amount on two platforms at the same moment.

5. Batch your withdrawals

At NDAX, each Canadian-dollar withdrawal costs $1.50 by e-Transfer or $4.99 by EFT. One withdrawal a quarter instead of one a week cuts that cost sharply over a year.

6. Pick the free lane when sending coins

Newton's free lane and Shakepay's normal speed cost nothing beyond waiting. Use the fast options only when timing truly matters.

7. Keep idle cash small

Coins are not CIPF-covered anywhere, and cash is covered only at member platforms that state it. Move cash you are not about to invest back to an insured savings account.

8. Track your cost base from day one

Record every buy, sell, swap, fee and reward in Canadian dollars. Accurate records can lower the gain you report, because fees add to your cost and reduce your proceeds.

9. Respect the 30-day window on losses

If you sell at a loss, do not rebuy the identical coin within 30 days, yourself or through your spouse, or the superficial loss rule can deny the loss.

10. Re-check the platform each year

Fees, coin lists and registration change. Once a year, confirm your platform is still on the CSA list, re-read its fee page and export your history.

Illustrative Example: Fees and Cost Base on $6,000 of Purchases

This example is hypothetical. Alex buys $500 of one crypto asset each month for 12 months with market orders, then sells it all a year later. The prices are made up to show the arithmetic; nothing here suggests what any coin will do.

  1. Total purchases. $500 x 12 = $6,000.
  2. Buying fees by platform, at published rates. NDAX: $6,000 x 0.20% = $12.00. Wealthsimple market orders: $6,000 x 0.5% = $30.00, plus the spread; set up as a recurring buy, Wealthsimple states there is no fee or spread. Kraken app instant buys: $6,000 x 1% = $60.00, plus the spread. Newton at its top Tier 1 rate: $6,000 x 1.15% = $69.00.
  3. Cost base if Alex used NDAX. Purchases plus buying fees: $6,000 + $12.00 = $6,012.00.
  4. The sale. Alex sells everything for $7,200 before fees. The NDAX selling fee is $7,200 x 0.20% = $14.40, so net proceeds are $7,200 minus $14.40 = $7,185.60.
  5. The gain. Net proceeds minus cost base: $7,185.60 minus $6,012.00 = $1,173.60. If Alex is investing rather than carrying on a business, this is a capital gain, and only part of it is taxable.
  6. Getting the cash out. One e-Transfer withdrawal costs $1.50. Spreading it over four withdrawals would cost $6.00.
  7. What to do next. Alex enters $7,185.60 in proceeds and $6,012.00 in cost base into the capital gains tax calculator to estimate the tax for the year of the sale. Had Alex swapped the coin for a different coin instead of selling, the same calculation would apply using the value received.
