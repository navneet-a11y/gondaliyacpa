1. How to Use the Currency Conversion Fee Calculator

Amount to convert. Enter the Canadian-dollar amount you plan to turn into US dollars, for example the cash you will use to buy US-listed shares or a lump sum moving into a US-dollar account. The calculator starts at $10,000; change it to your own figure.

One way or round trip. Choose one way if you will convert once and keep the US dollars, for example in a US-dollar account. Choose round trip if the money will come back to Canadian dollars later, for example when you buy US shares in an account with no US-dollar side and later sell them. A round trip doubles the cost, because the broker charges on the way in and on the way out.

What you get back. The calculator shows the lowest published cost first, then a table with each broker, its published rate, your cost and whether the broker lets registered accounts hold US dollars. Brokers with no single published rate are named below the table with their own wording, so you can see who they are without a guessed number.

What it leaves out. The calculator measures the conversion only. It does not include trading commissions, pass-through exchange fees, US withholding tax on dividends, or any monthly fee for a US-dollar account. For those, see our guide on how to buy US stocks in Canada and our US stocks in a TFSA guide.

Large amounts and the exchange rate field. The calculator applies each broker's published tier for the amount you enter. BMO and National Bank set their tiers in US dollars, so for those two it first converts your amount at the rate in the last field, an example assumption of 1.38 Canadian dollars per US dollar that you can change to your broker's quote. Wealthsimple's lower tiers apply only inside its USD account, so the calculator shows them as a note beside Wealthsimple's 1.5%. The reference table in section 4 shows the same tiers on $1,000, $10,000 and $50,000.

2. The Rules Behind the Numbers

Percentage brokers. Most brokers build their charge into the exchange rate as a percentage of the amount. The cost is the amount times the rate: $10,000 x 1.5% = $150. Wealthsimple (on US trades from a CAD account), Questrade and Webull charge 1.5% on every amount. TD Direct Investing charges 1.26% under $25,000.

Tiered brokers. CIBC, RBC, BMO and National Bank publish a spread in basis points that falls as the amount rises, with an approximate percentage beside it, and TD publishes illustrative percentages by amount. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 100 basis points is 1%; section 5 explains why the banks' basis points and percentages differ. National Bank describes its percentages as illustrative as of July 21, 2026.

Interactive Brokers. It quotes the exchange rate with no markup and charges a separate commission of 0.20 basis points of the amount, which is 0.002%, with a minimum of US$2.00 per conversion. On $50,000 that is $1.00 ($50,000 x 0.002%), below the minimum, so you pay US$2.00. The minimum applies whenever 0.002% of the amount comes to less than US$2.00.

Mixed currencies. Interactive Brokers charges its minimum in US dollars, while the other brokers' costs show in Canadian dollars because the spread is taken from the Canadian dollars you convert. The tables keep each in its own currency rather than converting at a rate we would have to assume.

3. Worked Examples: Three Investors

This example is hypothetical.

Aisha: $500 a month. Aisha buys US-listed shares with $500 each month in a TFSA that has no US-dollar side, at a broker charging 1.5%.

  1. Each purchase costs $7.50 to convert ($500 x 1.5%).
  2. Over a year that is $90.00 ($7.50 x 12).
  3. At Interactive Brokers, each conversion would cost the US$2.00 minimum, or US$24.00 a year (US$2.00 x 12).
  4. At a flat 1.5% broker, converting $6,000 once a year instead still costs $90.00 ($6,000 x 1.5%). Batching does not lower a flat percentage; a US-dollar side helps by stopping the conversions on her dividends and sales.

Ben: a $50,000 lump sum. Ben has $50,000 of cash in his RRSP to move into US-listed holdings.

  1. At a 1.5% broker: $750.00 ($50,000 x 1.5%).
  2. At National Bank (about 1.20% from US$25,000): $600.00 ($50,000 x 1.20%).
  3. At RBC (about 1.0% from $25,000): $500.00 ($50,000 x 1.0%).
  4. At CIBC (about 0.9% from $25,000) or BMO (about 0.9% from US$25,000): $450.00 ($50,000 x 0.9%).
  5. At TD (0.78% from $25,000): $390.00 ($50,000 x 0.78%).
  6. Inside a Wealthsimple USD account (0.5% from $25,000): $250.00 ($50,000 x 0.5%).
  7. At Interactive Brokers: US$2.00, since $1.00 ($50,000 x 0.002%) is below the minimum.
  8. With Norbert's Gambit at $0 commissions, and assuming for this example only that bid-ask spreads cost 0.05%: $25.00 ($50,000 x 0.05%), before any price change during the wait.

Chloe: a Wealthsimple Core client with $10,000. Chloe wants to put $10,000 into US-listed shares and hold them for years.

  1. From a CAD account: $150.00 to convert in ($10,000 x 1.5%). Every later sale, and every dividend she does not want converted, is charged 1.5% again.
  2. Through a USD account: $100.00 to convert in at the published 1.0% tier for conversions from $10,000 ($10,000 x 1.0%), plus the USD account at $10 a month plus tax, which is $120.00 a year before tax ($10 x 12). First-year total: $220.00 ($100 + $120).
  3. In year one, the USD route costs $70.00 more ($220 - $150), so it pays off that year only if the conversions it avoids on dividends, sales and reinvestment would cost more than $70. In later years the bar is $120 a year, the cost of the USD account.
  4. Once Chloe reaches Premium ($100,000 or more), the USD account is included and the USD route costs only the conversion itself.

5. How Currency Spreads Work

Two prices for every currency. In the currency market there is a price to buy US dollars and a slightly lower price to sell them. The middle of the two is the mid-market rate. A broker that converts for you gives a rate worse than the market rate by its markup or spread. That gap, times the amount, is your cost.

Why you rarely see it. Most brokers do not show the spread as a fee line. Your trade confirmation shows the exchange rate you received, and the cost is hidden inside it. That is why a "$0 commission" US trade from a Canadian-dollar account can still cost 1.5% of the amount. Webull describes its charge as the exchange rate plus a 1.5% markup; Wealthsimple applies 1.5% on top of its corporate exchange rate.

"0% fee" is not zero cost. moomoo has waived its currency exchange fee "until further notice", but its conversions use the moomoo Preferred Rate, which includes a spread of undisclosed size. Without the spread, the cost cannot be calculated, so we list moomoo as not published rather than as free.

Basis points are not your percentage. CIBC, RBC, BMO and National Bank quote their spread in basis points over a reference rate, then give an approximate percentage of the amount. RBC's 230 basis points, for example, is published as about 1.6% of a conversion under $25,000. Use the percentage to estimate your cost, as the tables on this page do.

Checking your own cost. After a conversion, compare the rate on your confirmation with the market rate at the same time. The difference, divided by the market rate, is roughly the percentage you paid. Please do this once with your own broker: it is the only way to measure a spread the broker does not publish.

Spreads on dividends. Some brokers convert US dividends at a different rate from trades. RBC Direct Investing converts US-dollar dividends and interest at 60 basis points as of March 9, 2026, lower than its 230 basis points on trades under $25,000.

6. USD Accounts and the USD Conversion Fee

Convert once, not every trade. A US-dollar account, or a US-dollar side of a registered account, holds US dollars as US dollars. You convert once, buy US-listed shares, and keep dividends and sale proceeds in US dollars until you choose to convert back. Without one, every purchase, sale and dividend is converted at the broker's rate.

7. Norbert's Gambit vs Broker Conversion

How it works. Norbert's Gambit converts currency by trading instead of paying the conversion rate. You buy a security listed in Canadian dollars that is also listed in US dollars, ask your broker to journal the position to the US-dollar listing, then sell it for US dollars. The interlisted US Dollar Currency ETF, which trades as DLR in Canadian dollars and DLR.U in US dollars, is the mechanism most often used. We name it only to explain the method; this is not a recommendation.

What it costs. Two commissions at your broker's rate, the bid-ask spread on both trades, and any change in price while the journal is processed. There is no published conversion fee. At a broker with $0 commissions on Canadian-listed ETF trades, the spreads are most of the cost.

The break-even point. The method saves money once the conversion fee you avoid is larger than the trading costs. At a broker charging $9.95 a trade and 1.6% to convert, the two commissions ($19.90) equal the conversion fee at $1,243.75 ($19.90 / 1.6%). Spreads and price movement push the real break-even higher, which is why the method is mostly used for larger sums.

Use this calculator to compare your broker's conversion cost with Norbert's Gambit for your amount. Choose your broker, check the commission it charges, and adjust the example bid-ask assumption if you know the current spread.

The risks. The journal is not instant, and the security's price can move while you wait. You also need an account with both currency sides and a broker that will journal the position; Wealthsimple lists Norbert's Gambit as available, and other brokers should be asked first. Our Norbert's Gambit step-by-step guide covers the process and record-keeping in detail.

8. CAD to USD Broker Fee Mistakes to Avoid

Letting every trade convert. In an account with no US-dollar side, buying, selling and receiving dividends all trigger a conversion. On $10,000 at 1.5%, a round trip costs $300.

Reading "$0 commission" as no cost. A $0 commission covers the trade, not the conversion. Many brokers charge 1.5% or more to convert on top of a $0 trade.

Treating a 0% fee as free. A 0% fee with an undisclosed spread still has a cost. Compare the rate you receive with the market rate before you rely on it.

Splitting a large conversion at a tiered broker. At TD, five $10,000 conversions cost $630 ($126 x 5), while one $50,000 conversion costs $390 (0.78%). The same applies at CIBC and RBC from $25,000, and at BMO and National Bank from US$25,000.

Tiny conversions at a minimum. Interactive Brokers' US$2.00 minimum is very low on large sums, but on a $100 conversion it is a large share of the amount. Group small conversions together where you can.

Paying a monthly USD fee on a small balance. At $120 a year before tax, a USD account fee costs more than it saves if you convert less than about $8,000 a year at 1.5%.

Forgetting the trip home. Money converted to US dollars for investing usually comes back to Canadian dollars one day. Count that second conversion when you compare brokers; the calculator's round-trip option does.

8 Ways to Pay Less to Convert Canadian Dollars

1. Hold US dollars in a USD account

A US-dollar side stops repeat conversions on dividends and sale proceeds. Questrade lists no separate fee for it in registered accounts; Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core and includes it from Premium.

2. Convert in one larger amount

At the bank-owned brokers the rate drops on larger amounts, and inside a Wealthsimple USD account from $10,000. One larger conversion can cost less than several small ones. At a flat 1.5% broker, size does not change the rate.

3. Compare the published rate first

On $10,000 the published cost ranges from US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers to $170 at National Bank. If US investing is a big part of your plan, the conversion rate deserves as much weight as the commission.

4. Use Norbert's Gambit for lump sums

For a large one-time conversion, two trades and a journal can replace a percentage fee. Count the commissions, the spreads and the wait before you start, and confirm your broker will journal the position.

5. Use US dollars you already have

US-dollar income or savings can go straight into the account's US-dollar side, with no conversion at all. Ask your broker how it accepts US-dollar deposits.

6. Keep dividends in US dollars

If you plan to reinvest in US holdings, let dividends collect in US dollars. Converting them to Canadian dollars and back later means paying twice.

7. Hold US dividend payers in an RRSP

Currency is not the only cost. A documented RRSP pays no US withholding on US-listed holdings, while a TFSA pays 15%. Put the conversion and the withholding together when you decide where to hold US shares.

8. Check your confirmations

Compare the rate on each conversion with the market rate at the time. It is the only way to measure a spread a broker does not publish, and it shows whether your broker's rate matches its published schedule.

Currency Conversion Fee Calculator FAQ

How much do Canadian brokers charge to convert Canadian dollars to US dollars?

As of October 22, 2026, on $10,000: US$2.00 at Interactive Brokers, $126 at TD Direct Investing (1.26%), $150 at Wealthsimple, Questrade and Webull (1.5%), $160 at CIBC, RBC and BMO (about 1.6%), and $170 at National Bank Direct Brokerage (about 1.70%). moomoo, Qtrade, Scotia iTRADE and Desjardins do not publish a single rate.

Which Canadian broker has the lowest currency conversion fee?

Among published rates, Interactive Brokers Canada: it adds no markup to the exchange rate and charges 0.20 basis points of the amount with a US$2.00 minimum, so $1,000, $10,000 and $50,000 each cost US$2.00. TD Direct Investing has the lowest published percentage among the other brokers, at 1.26% under $25,000 and 0.78% from $25,000.

What is a currency spread?

It is the gap between the market exchange rate and the rate a broker gives you. Most brokers build their charge into that gap rather than showing a separate fee, so a $0 commission trade can still cost 1.5% to convert. Your cost is the spread times the amount converted.

Does moomoo charge a currency conversion fee?

moomoo has waived its currency exchange fee until further notice, so the fee is 0%. Its conversions use the moomoo Preferred Rate, which includes a spread of undisclosed size. Because the spread is not published, we cannot calculate the cost and list it as not published rather than free.

Am I charged again when I sell US stocks?

If the account has no US-dollar side, yes. The sale proceeds are converted back to Canadian dollars at the broker's rate, so a purchase and sale is a round trip. Two conversions of $10,000 each at 1.5% cost $300. In a US-dollar account, the proceeds stay in US dollars until you choose to convert.

Does a USD account save money?

It saves the repeat conversions on dividends, sales and reinvestment, but some brokers charge for it. Wealthsimple charges $10 a month plus tax on Core, which is $120 a year before tax, and Qtrade US$15 a quarter per account unless you have Investor Plus. Questrade lists no separate fee. Compare the fee with what you would otherwise pay to convert.

When is Norbert's Gambit worth it?

When the conversion fee you avoid is larger than two commissions, the bid-ask spreads and the risk of a price move while the journal is processed. At $9.95 a trade and a 1.6% rate, the commissions alone equal the fee at $1,243.75. It is mostly used for larger sums. The DLR and DLR.U pair is the usual mechanism; this is not a recommendation.

Do bank-owned brokers charge less on large conversions?

Yes. From $25,000 (US$25,000 at BMO and National Bank), TD's published spread falls from 1.26% to 0.78%, CIBC's and BMO's to about 0.9%, RBC's to about 1.0% and National Bank's to about 1.20%. On a $50,000 conversion that means $390 at TD, $450 at CIBC or BMO, $500 at RBC and $600 at National Bank, against $750 at a flat 1.5%.

Are US dividends converted automatically?

In an account with no US-dollar side, yes, at the broker's rate. RBC Direct Investing converts US-dollar dividends and interest at 60 basis points as of March 9, 2026, and Wealthsimple applies its currency fee when US-dollar dividends are reinvested in an account without a USD side. A US-dollar account lets dividends stay in US dollars.

Does the calculator include commissions or tax?

No. It measures the currency conversion only. Trading commissions, pass-through exchange fees, monthly USD account fees and the 15% US withholding on dividends are separate. Our guide on how to buy US stocks in Canada covers each of those costs.
