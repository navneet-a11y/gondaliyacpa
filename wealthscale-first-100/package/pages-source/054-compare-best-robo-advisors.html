1. How We Ranked These Robo-Advisors

Five factors, fixed weights. Every robo-advisor gets a sub-score from 0 to 5 on five factors. We multiply each sub-score by its weight and add them up. Fees carry almost half the weight because a fee is a certain cost every year, while most other features are a matter of taste. The weights are the same for every provider, and no provider can pay to change them. The full rules for all our ratings are on how we rate brokers and robo-advisors, and the way WealthScale earns money is set out in our advertiser disclosure.

How the fee score works. Questwealth's total cost on $50,000 is 0.37% (0.25% fee plus 0.12% fund cost), so its fee score is 5 minus (0.37 minus 0.35) x 5 = 4.9. Wealthsimple's total is 0.65%, which gives 5 minus 0.30 x 5 = 3.5. RBC InvestEase at 0.70% scores 3.25, and CI Direct Investing at 0.83% scores 2.6.

A worked total. Wealthsimple: fees 3.5 x 0.45 = 1.575; accounts 4.5 x 0.15 = 0.675; portfolios 5.0 x 0.15 = 0.75; minimum 5.0 x 0.10 = 0.50; advice 4.5 x 0.15 = 0.675. The total is 4.175, shown as 4.2. Questwealth: 2.205 + 0.675 + 0.45 + 0.45 + 0.30 = 4.08, shown as 4.1. The gap is narrow, and it comes from portfolios and advice, not cost.

Why we use the highest fund cost. Most robo-advisors publish a range of fund costs across their risk levels. Using the top of that range for the core portfolios keeps the comparison fair and conservative: your cost may be a little lower, but it should not be higher for a core portfolio. Responsible, income and private portfolios are priced separately in section 4.

What the score leaves out. Performance is not scored, because past returns depend on each portfolio's mix and say little about the next ten years. App design, speed of account opening and promotions are not scored either. Our rules for sourcing and dating every figure are in our editorial standards.

3. The Cheapest Robo-Advisor in Canada at Each Balance

The short answer. Questwealth Portfolios is the cheapest ranked robo-advisor at every balance in our tables, from $10,000 to $250,000. The order behind it never changes either: Wealthsimple second, RBC InvestEase third and CI Direct Investing most expensive. On $50,000, the gap between first and last is $230 a year ($415 minus $185).

Breakpoints can reward one extra dollar. Wealthsimple and Questwealth both price the whole balance at the lower rate once you reach $100,000. At Wealthsimple, $99,999 costs about $649.99 a year (0.65%), but $100,000 costs $550.00 (0.55%), so crossing the line saves about $100 a year. At Questwealth, the management fee on $99,999 is about $250.00, against $200.00 on $100,000. If you are near a breakpoint, adding a small amount can lower your total bill.

Household and account totals. Wealthsimple sets its tier on the total of almost all your Wealthsimple accounts, and two or more clients at the same address can form a household so their combined assets count toward Premium. CI blends its tiered fee across all of a client's accounts. Spreading money across several robo-advisors can push each balance below a breakpoint, which raises your blended fee.

Responsible portfolios cost more. Choosing a socially responsible version changes the ranking of costs on $50,000. Questwealth SRI at up to 0.24% in fund costs totals $245 ($125 + $120). Wealthsimple's Socially Responsible portfolio, at up to 0.23%, totals $365 ($250 + $115). RBC InvestEase Responsible Investing, at up to 0.25%, totals $375 ($250 + $125). Questwealth stays cheapest, but its own cost rises by $60 a year over its standard portfolios.

Very small balances. Percentage fees are tiny on small accounts: on $1,000, Questwealth costs about $3.70 a year and Wealthsimple about $6.50. A flat minimum fee changes that. Justwealth, which we do not rank, has a minimum fee of $4.99 a month, or $59.88 a year, which is 5.99% of a $1,000 account ($59.88 / $1,000). A minimum fee matters more than the percentage until your balance is large.

Cheapest is not always best. Questwealth does not describe client advice. If you want someone to talk to about your plan, the extra $140 to $230 a year on $50,000 at Wealthsimple, RBC InvestEase or CI buys access to advisors or planners. Decide what you need first, then pay as little as possible for it.

8. Tax Features: Tax-Loss Harvesting and Registered Accounts

What tax-loss harvesting does. When an ETF in a non-registered account falls below what you paid, the robo-advisor sells it to realize a capital loss and buys a similar, but not identical, ETF so you stay invested. The loss can offset capital gains this year. A net capital loss you cannot use can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, and it only offsets taxable capital gains.

The superficial loss rule. A loss is denied if you, or a person affiliated with you such as your spouse or common-law partner, buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days after. The denied loss is usually added to the cost of the replacement property, so it is deferred rather than lost. This is why harvesting services switch to a similar ETF, and why you should not buy the same ETF yourself in another account during that window.

Harvesting only helps outside registered accounts. Gains and losses inside a TFSA, RRSP, FHSA or RESP are not taxed year by year, so there is nothing to harvest there. If most of your money is in registered accounts, tax-loss harvesting is worth little to you and should not drive your choice. Our TFSA vs RRSP comparison helps you decide where new money goes first.

Asset location. Wealthsimple's Generation tier offers asset location, which places different holdings in different account types to reduce tax across your household. Below Generation, each account holds its own complete portfolio.

Working out tax on a gain. When you sell a non-registered robo portfolio, the gain is the proceeds minus your adjusted cost base. Your robo-advisor's tax slips report the sales, and our capital gains tax calculator estimates the tax on a gain in your province.

10. Safety and Regulation: CIRO, CIPF and Custody

Who regulates robo-advisors. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO) is the national self-regulatory body that oversees all investment dealers and mutual fund dealers in Canada. Firms that belong to the Canadian Investor Protection Fund (CIPF) are CIRO members. The ETFs in your managed portfolio are client property held at the dealer or custodian, and client property is what CIPF protects if a member firm fails.

What CIPF covers and what it does not. CIPF covers missing securities and cash if a member firm becomes insolvent. It does not cover a drop in market value, unsuitable advice, the failure of a company whose shares you own, or crypto assets. CDIC deposit insurance does not cover ETFs, stocks, bonds or mutual funds, so a robo portfolio relies on CIPF, not CDIC.

The firms behind each robo. Wealthsimple Managed Investing is offered by Wealthsimple Investments Inc., a CIRO dealer member on CIPF's member list. CI Direct Investing's firm, CI Investment Services Inc., is on CIPF's list, and it is also the custodian for Justwealth. Questwealth Portfolios is offered by Questrade Wealth Management Inc., and Questrade, Inc. is on CIPF's list. We did not confirm the legal entity behind RBC InvestEase in this update, so check your account agreement for the name of the member firm that holds your assets.

Account security. Wealthsimple says clients who use passkeys are reimbursed for all direct losses from unauthorized activity on their account. Whatever robo you choose, turn on two-step verification, use a unique password, and never share a one-time code. Our Is Wealthsimple safe? page explains its CIPF and CDIC coverage in detail.

Firms that change or close. Three robo services in our unranked list either no longer appear among their firm's current services or could not confirm they accept clients. If a robo service closes, you may have to move your account, and fees or portfolios can change along the way. A firm with clear, current published pricing reduces that risk.

12. How to Switch to a Robo-Advisor

Always use a direct transfer. Open the new account first, then ask the robo-advisor to request the transfer from your current institution. For a TFSA, a direct transfer does not affect your contribution room. If you withdraw and redeposit it yourself, the redeposit counts as a new contribution and the withdrawal is only added back to your room on January 1 of the next year, which can cause an over-contribution taxed at 1% a month. 13. Common Robo-Advisor Mistakes

Comparing the headline fee only. A 0.25% fee and a 0.50% fee look far apart, but fund costs narrow or widen the gap. Always compare the management fee plus fund costs, as in section 2.

Splitting money across too many robos. Breakpoints at $100,000 at Wealthsimple and Questwealth, and blended tiers at CI, reward keeping assets together. Three $40,000 accounts at three robos can each pay the top rate.

Choosing an aggressive portfolio for short-term money. A down payment you need in two years does not belong in a growth portfolio. Robos offer lower-risk choices too, such as RBC InvestEase's Short-Term Bond portfolio; match the risk level to when you need the money.

Paying for advice you never use. If you never call or book a planning session, you are paying for a service you do not use. Either use the advice or move to the cheaper option.

Withdrawing to switch. Taking money out of a TFSA or RRSP to move it yourself can cost tax or contribution room. Use a direct transfer every time.

Over-contributing. A robo-advisor does not track contribution room you have used elsewhere. Please check your room on your latest notice of assessment before a large deposit, since TFSA and FHSA excess amounts are taxed at 1% a month.

10 Strategies to Pay Less at a Robo-Advisor

1. Compare total cost, not the fee

Add the highest fund cost to the management fee for every option. On $50,000, the ranked robos run from $185 to $415 a year once both layers are included, a gap that a headline fee alone hides.

2. Keep assets together

Hold your accounts at one robo to reach breakpoints sooner. Wealthsimple counts almost all your accounts toward Premium at $100,000, and household members at one address can combine. CI blends its tiered fee across all your accounts.

3. Time a top-up near a breakpoint

If you hold $95,000 at Wealthsimple, a $5,000 TFSA contribution takes you to $100,000, cutting the fee from 0.5% to 0.4% on the whole balance. That saves about $100 a year from that point.

4. Fill registered room first

The 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000 and the 2026 RRSP dollar limit is $33,810. Tax-sheltered growth usually outweighs any fee gap between robos, so use your room before you invest in a non-registered account.

5. Get your transfer fees covered

Move $25,000 or more to Wealthsimple or CI, or $15,000 or more to RBC InvestEase, and they cover your old institution's transfer fee up to their stated caps. Leaving fees can be $150 per account.

6. Price the responsible version

Responsible portfolios cost more in fund fees: Questwealth SRI runs up to 0.24% against 0.12% for standard. On $50,000 that is $60 a year more. Decide if the screening is worth it to you.

7. Use the advice you pay for

If you choose CI, RBC InvestEase or Wealthsimple partly for advice, book the call. Ask about account order, contribution room and your risk level once a year.

8. Harvest losses, then wait 30 days

If your robo harvests losses in a non-registered account, do not buy the same ETF yourself in another account or your spouse's account within 30 days, or the superficial loss rule can deny the loss.

9. Automate monthly deposits

A fixed monthly deposit keeps you invested through ups and downs and builds your balance toward fee breakpoints. Wealthsimple invests new deposits within five business days.

10. Review once a year

Fees, tiers and portfolio lineups change. Each year, recheck your total cost on this page and in the calculator, confirm your tier, and make sure your risk level still fits your timeline.
