How to invest in Canada comes down to ten steps: budget, clear expensive debt, build an emergency fund, set goals, choose the right account, decide how hands-on to be, pick a platform, learn the asset classes, diversify and count every cost, then place your first trade. For 2026, the TFSA limit is $7,000 and the RRSP dollar limit is $33,810. The robo-advisors we rank cost 0.37% to 0.83% a year in total on $50,000, and seven of the 13 online brokers we track charge $0 commission on stocks and ETFs, though currency fees still apply to US trades. This guide covers each step in plain terms, with no stock or fund picks.

1. Step 1: Budget and Clear High-Interest Debt

Find the amount you can invest. Start with your take-home pay each month and subtract what you spend on housing, food, transport, insurance and debt payments. What is left is the amount you can save. Investing works best as a habit, so a steady monthly amount you can keep up matters more than a large one-off sum.

Pay off expensive debt first. Every dollar of high-interest debt you repay earns you that interest rate with no risk. Credit card rates are high: Wealthsimple's credit card, for example, charges 20.99% on purchases. No investment can promise a return like that, so clearing card balances usually comes before investing beyond any employer match.

Do not invest with borrowed money as a beginner. Brokers lend against your investments through margin accounts, at rates such as 4.95% at Wealthsimple on its Core tier or prime plus 4.25%, about 8.70%, at Questrade. Borrowing to invest magnifies gains and losses alike, and a falling market can force you to sell. Learn how your investments behave before you borrow against them.

Use any employer plan. If your employer adds money when you contribute to a group retirement plan, that match is extra pay. Note that a workplace pension creates a pension adjustment, which reduces next year's RRSP room, so check your notice of assessment for your actual RRSP limit.

Pay yourself first. Set up an automatic transfer on payday into your savings or investment account. Money that moves before you can spend it is the simplest way to stick to a budget.

2. Step 2: Build an Emergency Fund Before You Invest

Why it comes first. Markets fall from time to time. If a job loss or a large repair arrives during a fall, and you have no cash, you may have to sell investments at a loss. An emergency fund lets your investments stay invested.

How much to hold. Aim for enough to cover several months of essential costs: rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance and minimum debt payments. The right number depends on how steady your income is, whether your household has one or two earners and what insurance you have.

Where to keep it. Keep it somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as a high-interest savings account. At a member of the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC), eligible deposits are insured up to $100,000 per category per institution, and deposits in a TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, RESP or FHSA are each a separate category. Our best high-interest savings accounts page compares rates, and GIC vs high-interest savings explains when locking money in makes sense.

Use the TFSA for savings too. A TFSA can hold cash and GICs as well as investments. Interest earned inside it is tax-free, and you can take money out at any time, with the amount added back to your room on January 1 of the next year.

What CDIC does not cover. Stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds and crypto are not covered by CDIC, even when you buy them at a bank. Your emergency fund belongs in deposits and GICs, not in the stock market.

3. Step 3: Set Your Goals and Time Horizon

Name each goal. Write down what the money is for and when you need it: a car in two years, a home down payment in five, a child's education in fifteen, retirement in thirty. Each goal gets its own time horizon, and the horizon drives everything else.

Short horizons need stable money. Money you need within a few years should not depend on the stock market, because markets can fall and take years to recover. Savings accounts and GICs suit short goals; see our best GIC rates.

Long horizons can take more risk. Money you will not touch for many years has time to recover from falls, so it can hold more stocks or stock funds, which carry a bigger risk of short-term losses in exchange for the chance of more growth. How much of that swing you can live with is your risk tolerance; how much you can afford is your risk capacity.

4. Step 4: Choose Your Accounts: TFSA, RRSP, FHSA and RESP

Use registered accounts first. Canada's registered accounts shelter investment growth from tax. Which one comes first depends on your income now, your income later and your goal. The table shows the 2026 rules.

TFSA. Any Canadian resident 18 or older with a social insurance number can open one (19 where that is the age of majority). Room builds every year from 18, and unused room carries forward. Contributing more than your room costs 1% a month on the excess. Read the TFSA guide and check your figure with the TFSA contribution room calculator.

RRSP. Contributions reduce your taxable income this year, and withdrawals are taxed when you take them. It tends to suit people who expect a lower tax rate in retirement than today. The 2027 dollar limit is already published at $35,390. The RRSP guide and RRSP tax refund calculator show what a contribution saves you.

FHSA. If you have not owned and lived in a home in the current year or the previous four calendar years, and neither has a current spouse or partner, an FHSA gives you a deduction going in and a tax-free withdrawal to buy your first home. Room starts only when you open the account, so opening early matters. See the FHSA guide and FHSA vs TFSA.

RESP. The Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% of the first $2,500 you contribute each year, or $500, up to $7,200 per child over time. Lower-income families can get more. Our RESP guide covers the grants.

Which first? For many people the order depends on whether they are buying a first home, how high their income is and whether they have an employer match. Our TFSA vs RRSP comparison works through the trade-off with tax rates, and the investment accounts hub covers every account type.

7. Step 7: Learn the Main Asset Classes

What an asset class is. An asset class is a group of investments that behave in a similar way. Knowing the main ones helps you understand what you own, how it earns money and what can go wrong. This is general information about types of investments, not a suggestion to buy any of them.

Main asset classes in plain terms

Cash and GICs. These are the steadiest, and eligible deposits at a CDIC member are insured. Their weakness is that the interest may not keep up with rising prices over long periods, so they suit short goals and emergency funds better than retirement savings.

Bonds. A bond pays interest and returns your money at maturity, if the issuer can pay. Bond prices move in the opposite direction to interest rates, so a bond fund can lose value when rates rise.

Stocks. Owning a stock means owning part of a company. Stocks can pay dividends and grow with the company, but prices can drop a lot in a short time, and a single company can fail. Holding many stocks, for example through a fund, spreads that risk.

Funds. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) trades on a stock exchange like a share, and a mutual fund is bought from the fund company or a dealer. Both pool money into many holdings, and both charge a yearly MER that comes out of the fund's return.

Crypto. Crypto is not a security covered by CIPF and is not a deposit covered by CDIC. If you choose to hold any, use a platform authorized to deal with Canadians; our best crypto platforms page lists only those platforms.

8. Step 8: Diversify and Set Your Asset Mix

What diversification means. Diversification means spreading your money so one bad result cannot sink the whole portfolio. You can spread across asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash), across countries (Canada, the US and other markets) and across industries. A portfolio of many holdings tends to swing less than any one of them.

Set an asset mix. Your asset mix is the share of your money in each asset class, for example how much is in stocks and how much in bonds and cash. A longer time horizon and a higher tolerance for drops can support more stocks; a shorter horizon or a need for steady value points to more bonds and cash. We do not publish model portfolios, because the right mix depends on your own goals and situation.

One fund or many. You can build a diversified portfolio from several funds, or hold a single fund that already mixes asset classes and countries. A robo-advisor does the same job for you: Wealthsimple's Classic portfolios, for example, hold 8 to 10 ETFs and rebalance automatically.

Rebalance. Over time, the parts that grow fastest take up more of the portfolio than you planned. Rebalancing means selling a little of what has grown or adding new money to what has lagged, to bring the mix back to target. A common approach is once a year, or when one part drifts well away from its target.

Mind the account for foreign holdings. Under the Canada-US tax treaty, US dividends on US-listed securities held directly in an RRSP, RRIF, LIRA or LIF are exempt from US withholding tax. In a TFSA, RESP or FHSA, 15% is withheld (30% without a Form W-8BEN on file), and the tax cannot be recovered. Our US stocks in a TFSA guide explains the trade-off, and how to buy US stocks in Canada covers the currency cost.

9. Step 9: Count Every Cost Before You Invest

Costs are the one return you control. You cannot choose what markets do, but you can choose what you pay. Every fee comes straight out of your return, every year, and the effect compounds. On $50,000, each 0.10% of yearly cost is ($50,000 x 0.10% = $50) a year.

10. Step 10: Make Your First Trade and Automate

Fund the account. Link your bank account and move money in. A deposit to a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA is a contribution, so it counts against your room the moment it lands. Check your room before you deposit, not after.

Know the two main order types. A market order buys at the best price available right now, and the price can be different from the last quote. A limit order sets the most you will pay and fills only at that price or better. For funds that trade on an exchange, a limit order near the current ask gives you control over the price.

Start small if you like. Fractional shares let you invest a dollar amount instead of buying whole shares. Wealthsimple offers them from $1 in thousands of Canadian and US stocks and ETFs, TD Easy Trade takes dollar-amount orders from $1, and Questrade and Interactive Brokers offer them too. moomoo does not.

Automate the habit. Set a recurring contribution from your bank on payday, and where your platform allows it, a recurring purchase. Wealthsimple, for example, lets you set recurring investments daily, weekly, every two weeks or monthly. Investing the same amount on a schedule, known as dollar-cost averaging, means you buy more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high.

Reinvest dividends. A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) uses dividends to buy more units automatically. Questrade reinvests at no charge for almost every stock or ETF, Wealthsimple turns it on for a whole account, and TD Direct Investing and National Bank reinvest in whole shares on request. moomoo does not offer a DRIP.

11. How Investment Income Is Taxed in Canada

The account decides the tax. The same investment is taxed very differently depending on the account it sits in. Inside a TFSA, growth and withdrawals are tax-free. Inside an RRSP or RRIF, nothing is taxed until you withdraw, and then every dollar withdrawn is taxed as income. In a non-registered account, each type of income has its own rule.

How common investment income is taxed, by account
IncomeNon-registered accountTFSARRSP or RRIF
InterestFully taxable each yearTax-freeTaxed only when withdrawn, as income
Canadian eligible dividendsGrossed up by 38%, then reduced by the dividend tax creditTax-freeTaxed only when withdrawn, as income
Capital gainsOnly part is taxable: one-half for 2001 to 2025Tax-freeTaxed only when withdrawn, as income
US dividends15% US withholding with a W-8BEN; a foreign tax credit can offset it15% withheld and not recoverableExempt when US-listed securities are held directly

Dividends. An eligible dividend from a Canadian company is grossed up by 38% (multiplied by 1.38) on your return, and the dividend tax credit then reduces the tax. Other than eligible dividends are grossed up by 15%. The federal credit is 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount for eligible dividends and 9.0301% for other dividends, and each province adds its own credit.

Capital gains and losses. You report a capital gain only when you sell. For 2001 to 2025, one-half of a net capital gain was included in income. A net capital loss can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely, and can only reduce taxable capital gains. The superficial loss rule denies a loss if you or your spouse buy identical property within 30 calendar days before or after the sale and still hold it 30 days later. Our capital gains tax calculator works out the tax.

Foreign income. In a non-registered account, you report foreign dividends in full and can claim a foreign tax credit, generally the lesser of the foreign tax paid and the Canadian tax on that income, on Form T2209. Inside a TFSA there is no Canadian tax to credit against, so US withholding there is a permanent cost.

Trading inside a TFSA. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) can treat TFSA income as business income, and tax it, when the holder trades with the frequency and experience of a professional trader. There is no set number of trades. Long-term investing is not what this rule targets.

12. Keep Going: Review, Rebalance and Stay Protected

Review once a year. Pick a date, such as early January when new TFSA room arrives. Check your contributions against your room, your asset mix against your target, and the fees you paid. Your latest notice of assessment shows your RRSP deduction limit for the year.

Update the plan when life changes. A new job, a pension plan, a child, a home purchase or a separation can change which account comes first and how much risk you can carry. A pension adjustment, for example, reduces next year's RRSP room.

Know your protection. At a CIPF member, CIPF covers client property that goes missing if the firm fails, up to $1,000,000 for your general accounts combined (cash, margin, TFSA and FHSA), a separate $1,000,000 for your retirement accounts (RRSP, RRIF and LIF) and a separate $1,000,000 for RESPs. It does not cover a fall in market value or crypto assets. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO) oversees all investment dealers in Canada.

Do not leave cash idle by accident. Cash sitting in a brokerage account often earns little or nothing. Questrade's interest on uninvested cash works out to effectively 0%, and CIBC Investor's Edge, National Bank and Scotia iTRADE list 0% on Canadian cash. If cash is waiting to be invested, know what it earns.

Keep records. In a non-registered account, keep every trade confirmation so you can work out your adjusted cost base when you sell. In registered accounts, keep contribution records so you never exceed your room.

Switch if you need to. If your platform no longer suits you, move with a direct transfer, never a withdrawal. Our investing for beginners hub links every guide in this series, and our editorial standards explain how we check the figures on this page.

Investing for Beginners: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Investing money you will need soon. Putting next year's down payment in stocks means a market drop can force you to sell low. Keep short-term money in savings or GICs.

Over-contributing to a TFSA. Contributing more than your room costs 1% a month on the highest excess in each month, with no grace amount. Withdrawing and re-depositing in the same year is a common cause, because withdrawals only come back as room on January 1.

Buying a few hot tips. A portfolio of a handful of stocks rises and falls with those few companies. Spreading money across many holdings and asset classes lowers the damage any one can do.

Ignoring fees. A total yearly cost of 0.83% instead of 0.37% on $50,000 is $230 a year, and the gap grows as your balance grows.

Paying the currency fee over and over. Buying US-listed securities from a Canadian-dollar account converts on every trade, at 1.5% at Questrade, Wealthsimple and Webull. Holding US dollars, where the account allows it, avoids repeat conversions.

Selling in a panic. Selling after a fall locks in the loss. An emergency fund and a written plan make it easier to sit still.

Putting US dividend payers in the wrong account. In a TFSA, 15% US withholding on dividends is lost for good, while an RRSP holding US-listed securities directly is exempt.

Trading a TFSA like a day trader. CRA can tax a TFSA's income as business income when it is traded with the frequency and experience of a professional.

How to Start Investing in Canada: Your First-Month Checklist

  1. Week 1: Write the budget. List income and costs, pick a monthly amount to invest and set an automatic transfer for payday.
  2. Week 1: Check your room. Find your TFSA room and RRSP deduction limit, and work out FHSA eligibility if you plan to buy a first home.
  3. Week 2: Top up the emergency fund. Move enough to cover several months of essential costs into a CDIC-insured savings account before investing the rest.
  4. Week 2: Choose your approach. Compare a do-it-yourself broker with a robo-advisor on total yearly cost in dollars, not just the headline fee.
  5. Week 3: Open the account. Open the registered account that fits your first goal, with your ID and social insurance number, and link your bank.
  6. Week 3: Fund it within your room. Make the first deposit and set up a recurring contribution that stays under your limit for the year.
  7. Week 4: Invest and switch on reinvestment. Place your first purchase with a limit order, or let the robo-advisor invest the deposit. Turn on dividend reinvestment and save the confirmation.

10 Strategies for New Investors in Canada

1. Automate on payday

A recurring transfer and purchase on payday turns investing into a habit you do not have to remember. It also spreads your buying over time, so you never put everything in at a single price.

2. Use registered room first

TFSA, RRSP and FHSA room shelters growth from yearly tax. For 2026 that is $7,000 of new TFSA room, up to $33,810 of RRSP room and $8,000 of FHSA room, so many beginners can invest for years before they need a taxable account.

3. Open an FHSA early if you qualify

FHSA room starts only when you open the account, then builds by $8,000 a year to a $40,000 lifetime limit. Opening it early, even with a small deposit, starts the clock on room you can carry forward.

4. Claim the RESP grant every year

The Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% of the first $2,500 a year, or $500, per child, to a lifetime $7,200. Contributing $2,500 each year collects the full basic grant. Lower-income families can receive more.

5. Compare total cost in dollars

Ask what you will pay each year in dollars, including fund MERs. On $50,000, the ranked robo-advisors cost from $185 to $415 a year, and a $0-commission broker charges nothing per trade.

6. Diversify with funds

A fund holds many securities at once, so one company's bad year does less harm. Spreading across asset classes and countries adds another layer. This is a general principle, not a suggestion of any particular fund.

7. Put US dividend payers in the RRSP

US-listed securities held directly in an RRSP are exempt from US withholding on dividends under the tax treaty. In a TFSA, 15% is withheld and cannot be recovered.

8. Convert currency once

Every conversion at 1.5% costs $150 per $10,000. Converting a lump sum once, and holding US dollars where your account allows, beats converting on each trade. Norbert's Gambit can cut the cost further on larger amounts.

9. Rebalance once a year

Pick one date each year to bring your asset mix back to target. Using new contributions to top up whatever has lagged avoids selling and, in a taxable account, avoids realizing gains.

10. Keep the emergency fund full

The best defence against selling low is not needing to sell. Refill the emergency fund after you use it, before adding new money to investments.

  2. TFSA contributions of $500 a month over the 12 months of 2026: ($500 x 12 = $6,000), inside the 2026 limit of $7,000, leaving ($7,000 - $6,000 = $1,000) of 2026 room.

  4. The same balance at CI Direct Investing's 0.83% total: ($50,000 x 0.83% = $415.00), a gap of ($415.00 - $185.00 = $230.00) a year.
  5. Doing it yourself with one purchase a month: $0 in commissions at a $0-commission broker, or ($9.99 x 12 = $119.88) at TD Direct Investing, plus the MER of the funds chosen.
  6. Over 20 years, $50,000 growing at an assumed 5% a year before costs: at 0.37% the net growth rate is 4.63%, giving ($50,000 x 1.0463 to the power of 20 = $123,621.64); at 0.83% it is 4.17%, giving ($50,000 x 1.0417 to the power of 20 = $113,193.97). How to Invest in Canada: FAQ

How do I start investing in Canada with little money?

Open a TFSA at a broker or robo-advisor with a low or no minimum and set up a small monthly contribution. Wealthsimple offers fractional shares from $1, TD Easy Trade takes dollar-amount orders from $1, and CI Direct Investing has a $100 minimum. Build an emergency fund first, then invest a set amount each payday.

Should I open a TFSA or an RRSP first?

It depends on your income now and later. An RRSP gives a deduction today and taxes withdrawals later, so it tends to suit people who expect a lower tax rate in retirement. A TFSA gives no deduction but tax-free withdrawals at any time. First-time home buyers may also want to look at the FHSA, which offers both a deduction and a tax-free home withdrawal.

How much do robo-advisors cost in Canada?

On $50,000, the total yearly cost, meaning management fee plus fund costs, is 0.37% ($185) at Questwealth Portfolios, 0.65% ($325) at Wealthsimple Managed Investing, 0.70% ($350) at RBC InvestEase and 0.83% ($415) at CI Direct Investing, as of October 22, 2026. Ranked by our robo score, Wealthsimple Managed Investing leads at 4.2 / 5.

Is it better to invest on my own or use a robo-advisor?

Investing on your own costs less: seven of 13 brokers charge $0 commission, and you pay only fund MERs and any currency fees. A robo-advisor costs more, from 0.37% a year in total on $50,000 among those we rank, but it builds, rebalances and reinvests for you. The right choice depends on your time, interest and discipline.

Where should a beginner invest money in Canada?

Start with a registered account, such as a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA, at a CIPF member. For people investing on their own, the top three brokers in our beginners ranking are Wealthsimple (4.5 / 5), Interactive Brokers Canada (4.4 / 5) and Questrade (4.1 / 5). For a hands-off option, compare robo-advisors on total yearly cost.

Is commission-free investing really free?

No. A $0 commission removes the charge per trade, but you still pay the bid-ask spread on each trade, the MER of any fund you hold, and a currency fee when you buy US-listed securities with Canadian dollars, such as 1.5% at Questrade, Wealthsimple and Webull. Some brokers also charge account or transfer fees.

What does diversification mean?

Diversification means spreading your money across many investments so one poor result cannot sink the whole portfolio. You can spread across asset classes such as stocks, bonds and cash, across countries and across industries. Funds and robo-advisor portfolios hold many securities at once, which makes diversifying easier for beginners.

How is investment income taxed in Canada?

It depends on the account. In a TFSA, income and withdrawals are tax-free. In an RRSP, nothing is taxed until you withdraw. In a non-registered account, interest is fully taxable, eligible Canadian dividends are grossed up by 38% and receive a dividend tax credit, and one-half of a capital gain was taxable for 2001 to 2025.

Is my money safe with an online broker?

At a CIPF member, client property missing because the firm failed is covered up to $1,000,000 for general accounts, including TFSA and FHSA, another $1,000,000 for retirement accounts and another $1,000,000 for RESPs. CIPF does not cover market losses or crypto. Cash deposits and GICs at a CDIC member are insured up to $100,000 per category.

Should I pay off debt before investing?

Usually, high-interest debt comes first. Paying off a card that charges 20.99%, as Wealthsimple's credit card does on purchases, saves that rate with no risk, which no investment can promise. Low-rate debt such as a mortgage is a closer call, and many people invest while paying it down, especially in registered accounts.

Can I buy US stocks from Canada?

Yes. All 13 online brokers we track trade US-listed stocks and ETFs. The main extra cost is currency conversion, 1.5% at Questrade, Wealthsimple and Webull. US dividends have 15% withheld in a TFSA, but US-listed securities held directly in an RRSP are exempt under the tax treaty. Our guide on buying US stocks covers the details.

What are the biggest mistakes new investors make?

The most costly are investing money needed soon, over-contributing to a TFSA (1% a month on the excess), concentrating in a few stocks, ignoring fees, converting currency on every trade and selling in a panic after a fall. A budget, an emergency fund, a written plan and automatic contributions prevent most of them.
