FHSA Eligibility: Who Counts as a First-Time Home Buyer

FHSA eligibility comes down to four tests applied on the day you open the account: you must be a resident of Canada, at least 18 (19 where that is the age of majority), no older than 71 at December 31 of the year you open it, and a first-time home buyer. For the FHSA, that means you did not live in a home owned by you or your current spouse or common-law partner at any time in the current year before opening or in the previous four calendar years. A slightly different test applies when you withdraw. This page explains both, with 16 situations checked.

At a glance

The four opening tests. To open your first FHSA you must be a resident of Canada, at least 18 or 19 depending on your province or territory, no older than 71 at December 31 of the year you open it, and a first-time home buyer. All four are checked when the account is opened.

What first-time home buyer means. At no time in the current year before opening, or in the previous four calendar years, did you live in a home owned by you or by your current spouse or common-law partner. Someone who owned a home years ago can qualify again.

The spouse rule. Living in a home your current spouse or common-law partner owns fails the opening test, even if your name is not on the title. The spouse part only applies if you have a spouse or partner when you open the account.

The withdrawal test is different. For a qualifying withdrawal you must be a first-time home buyer again, but the test ignores the 30 days before the withdrawal and, as published, looks only at homes you owned and lived in yourself.

Age of majority. You can open at 18 in Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec and Saskatchewan, and at 19 in British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut and Yukon.

After you open. The opening tests are not run again to keep the account open. A later change, such as buying a home, matters only for whether a later withdrawal qualifies.

1. The Four FHSA Eligibility Tests

All four, on opening day. FHSA eligibility is decided when you open your first account. You must pass every test at that moment. Passing three out of four is not enough, and there is no partial account or reduced room for someone who almost qualifies.

The name misleads. "First-time home buyer" sounds as if it means someone who has never owned a home. It does not. The test is a rolling look-back of about four to five years, so a person who sold a home and moved out long enough ago qualifies again. Someone who rules themselves out because they once owned a condo may, in fact, be eligible.

Ownership plus living there. The test is about homes that were owned and lived in. Owning a rental property you never lived in does not fail it. Living in a home your parents own does not fail it either, because only your own ownership and your current spouse's or partner's count.

Why opening day matters so much. FHSA room starts only in the year you open your first account: $8,000 a year, with a $40,000 lifetime limit. Every year you could have opened but did not is a year of room that never exists. Knowing exactly when you become eligible lets you open on the first possible day. Our FHSA guide covers room, deductions and withdrawals once you are in, and our overview of investment accounts in Canada shows where the FHSA fits beside the TFSA and RRSP.

More than one account. You can hold more than one FHSA. The four tests apply when you open each one, and your limits are shared across all of them.

2. Test 1: Residency in Canada

The rule. You must be a resident of Canada to open an FHSA. Citizenship is not the test: a Canadian citizen living abroad who is not a resident cannot open one, while a permanent resident or other newcomer who has become a resident can, if the other tests are met.

Residency again at withdrawal. Residency is also one of the conditions for a qualifying withdrawal. If you open an FHSA, then move abroad and buy a home there, the withdrawal would not meet the conditions. The FHSA is built for a home in Canada bought by someone living in Canada.

Leaving after you open. The opening tests are not re-checked to keep an account open, so leaving Canada does not by itself close your FHSA. The account still ends on the normal schedule: December 31 of the year of its 15th anniversary, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal, whichever comes first. If you will not come back to buy, the options are a direct transfer to an RRSP or RRIF or a taxable withdrawal before the period ends.

Coming back. A former resident who returns and becomes a resident again can open an FHSA once all four tests are met, including the first-time home buyer look-back.

Residency is a tax question. Residency for tax purposes is not the same thing as citizenship or immigration status, and the line can be blurry for people who split time between countries. If your status is unclear, please settle it before you open, because an account opened by someone who did not meet the tests is a problem you want to avoid rather than fix.

Compared with the TFSA. A TFSA also requires residency, and TFSA room builds only for years you are a resident. The FHSA is simpler: room does not build by year of residency at all. It starts the year you open your first FHSA, whenever that is.

3. Test 2: Minimum Age of 18 or 19

The rule. You must be at least 18 to open an FHSA, or 19 in a province or territory where 19 is the age of majority. The age that applies is the age of majority where you live.

Minimum age to open an FHSA, by province and territory

18Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec, Saskatchewan

No catch-up for the wait. The TFSA has a catch-up rule: in a province where you must be 19 to open, the room from the year you turned 18 carries over once you open. The FHSA has nothing like it. FHSA room starts only in the year you open your first account, so an 18-year-old in Ontario who opens this year starts building room a year earlier than an 18-year-old in British Columbia, who must wait until 19.

Should an 18-year-old open one? Opening at 18 starts both the room and the 15-year clock. An account opened in 2026 at age 18 must close by December 31, 2041, when the account holder will be 33. For someone who expects to buy in their late twenties or early thirties, that timing can line up well. For someone who expects to buy at 40, opening at 18 would end the account before the purchase, though the money could still move to an RRSP.

Living with parents. Young adults who live in a parent's home pass the first-time home buyer test, because parents' ownership does not count. Only homes you own, or your current spouse or partner owns, matter.

Gifts from parents. Parents often help fund a child's FHSA. The account and its room belong to the child, who must meet all four tests and who claims the deduction for contributions to their own FHSA.

4. Test 3: No Older Than 71 at December 31

The rule. You can open an FHSA if you are 71 or younger at December 31 of the year you open it. In practice, anyone who turns 71 or younger during 2026 can open an FHSA in 2026; anyone turning 72 or older cannot.

The catch for older buyers. The age limit also ends the account. An FHSA must close by December 31 of the year you turn 71, if that comes before its 15th anniversary. So an older first-time buyer can open, but has fewer years of room. Someone who opens in the year they turn 71 must close the account by December 31 of that same year.

5. Test 4: The First-Time Home Buyer Definition

The FHSA first-time home buyer test. For opening an FHSA, you are a first-time home buyer if, at no time in the current year before you open the account or in the previous four calendar years, you lived in a home owned by you or by your current spouse or common-law partner. This is the first-time home buyer definition that matters for the FHSA in Canada, and it is narrower than everyday speech.

Whole calendar years. The look-back covers the four previous calendar years in full, plus the current year up to the day you open. It does not count back 48 months from today. That makes your eligibility date predictable: if you last lived in a home you owned at any time in a given year, you can open an FHSA from January 1 of the fifth year after it.

The December trap. Because whole years count, the date you move out matters more than the date you sell. Someone who moves out of a home they own on December 30, 2026 can open an FHSA from January 1, 2031. Someone who moves out three days later, on January 2, 2027, waits until January 1, 2032. Moving out in late December rather than early January can bring your eligibility forward by a full year.

Owned and lived in. Both parts must be true for a home to count against you. A rental property you own but have never lived in does not count. A home you lived in but did not own, such as a rented apartment or a parent's house, does not count either, unless your current spouse or partner owned it.

Not the same as never owning. A person who owned a home ten years ago, sold it, and has rented since is a first-time home buyer for the FHSA. The Home Buyers' Plan uses a similar four-year look-back for RRSP withdrawals.

6. The Spouse and Common-Law Partner Rule

The rule. When you open an FHSA, the first-time home buyer test also looks at homes owned by your current spouse or common-law partner. If, in the current year before opening or in the previous four calendar years, you lived in a home your current spouse or partner owned, you fail the test, even if your name was never on the title and you never paid toward it.

Only a current spouse or partner. The spouse part applies only if you have a spouse or common-law partner when you open the account. A single person is tested on their own ownership alone. A former partner's home does not count under the spouse part once that person is no longer your spouse or partner.

Living together first, partners later. Because the test asks about your current spouse or partner at opening, the home they owned before you were a couple can still count. If you moved into a friend's condo as a roommate in 2024 and you are now common-law partners, opening an FHSA in 2026 fails the test: you lived in a home your current partner owned within the look-back window.

Open before you move in. The opening test is applied once. If you open an FHSA while you still rent, and later move into a home your spouse or partner owns, your account stays open and keeps building room. This is the single most useful timing point for couples where one partner already owns.

The withdrawal test drops the spouse part. When you later make a qualifying withdrawal, the first-time home buyer test as published looks at homes you owned and lived in. It does not repeat the spouse part. So a person who opened while single, then moved into a spouse's home, can still make a qualifying withdrawal for a new home if they never owned one themselves and meet the other conditions.

7. FHSA Eligibility for Newcomers to Canada

Residency comes first. A newcomer can open an FHSA once they are a resident of Canada and meet the age and first-time home buyer tests. The published tests set no minimum time in Canada: residency on the day you open is what counts.

No room for years before you open. FHSA room is not tied to years of residency. It starts in the year you open your first FHSA, at $8,000, like everyone else's. A newcomer who arrives and opens in 2026 has $8,000 of room for 2026 and can reach the $40,000 lifetime limit by 2030 by contributing the maximum each year.

Compared with the TFSA. TFSA room for a newcomer starts the day they become a resident, and only years of residency count. A person who became resident in 2024 had the 2024 limit of $7,000, not the room from earlier years. The FHSA works on a different trigger, the opening date, so a newcomer is on the same footing as a long-time resident who opens in the same year. Our TFSA contribution limit page covers the TFSA side.

A home owned abroad. The published first-time home buyer test speaks of a home you owned and lived in, and it does not limit that to homes in Canada. If you owned and lived in a home outside Canada during the current year or the previous four calendar years, plan as though it counts, and confirm your facts with a tax professional before you open. The same applies to a home abroad owned by your current spouse or partner that you lived in.

A worked newcomer timeline. This example is hypothetical. Sofia owned and lived in an apartment in Lisbon until she moved to Toronto in August 2025. Treating that home as counting, her last year living in a home she owned is 2025, so her earliest FHSA opening date is January 1, 2030. In the meantime she builds TFSA room from the day she became a resident, and that room can hold her home savings until she can open an FHSA.

Getting set up. Many newcomers open a bank account and a TFSA first. 8. Separation, Divorce and FHSA Eligibility

Your own ownership always counts. If you owned the family home, alone or with your spouse, and lived in it, that home counts against you under the look-back no matter what happens to the relationship. Your clock runs from the last calendar year you lived there. Moving out in 2024 means your earliest FHSA opening date is January 1, 2029.

A former partner's home stops counting. The spouse part of the test looks only at your current spouse or common-law partner when you open. If the home you shared was owned only by your former spouse or partner, and that person is no longer your spouse or partner, their ownership no longer stands in your way. If you never owned a home yourself, you may be able to open an FHSA right away.

Separated but still married. A legal spouse you have separated from may still be your spouse when you open. If you are separated but not divorced and your spouse owns the home you lived in, please get advice on your status before relying on the spouse part not applying.

The HBP has a breakdown relief; the FHSA test does not show one. The Home Buyers' Plan waives its first-time buyer test for someone living apart from a spouse or common-law partner after a breakdown of the relationship. The FHSA opening test, as published, contains no matching relief. After a breakdown, the HBP may therefore be open to you years before an FHSA, which matters if you have RRSP savings. Our Home Buyers' Plan guide covers that route.

9. Opening Tests vs Qualifying Withdrawal Tests

Two different checkpoints. Eligibility to open an FHSA and eligibility to take money out tax-free are separate questions. The opening tests decide whether you can have an account at all. The qualifying withdrawal conditions decide whether a particular withdrawal for a home is tax-free. You can pass the first and later fail the second, or the other way around.

The 30-day window in practice. The withdrawal test ignores the 30 days before the withdrawal, and the home must not have been acquired more than 30 days earlier. Together, those rules let you take the money before closing or shortly after. If your purchase closes on June 1, a withdrawal on or before July 1 is within 30 days. A withdrawal on July 20, 49 days later, fails.

Buying a home without the FHSA. If you buy and move into a home using other money, you fail the withdrawal test while you live there and for the following four calendar years. Your FHSA stays open until its participation period ends, so the realistic options are usually a direct transfer to an RRSP or RRIF, which does not use your RRSP contribution room, or waiting out the look-back if time allows.

Marrying a homeowner after opening. Because the withdrawal test does not repeat the spouse part, living in your spouse's home after you opened your FHSA does not, by itself, stop a later qualifying withdrawal for a new home you buy, provided you never owned a home yourself in the look-back and meet the other conditions.

Each withdrawal is tested. The withdrawal test is applied again at the time of each qualifying withdrawal. Your first qualifying withdrawal also starts the wind-down: the account must be closed by December 31 of the following year. Our FHSA withdrawal rules cover the full conditions.

11. When Your Situation Changes After You Open

The account stays open. All four opening conditions apply when the account is opened. They are not re-run each year. Your FHSA stays open, and keeps adding $8,000 of room a year up to the $40,000 lifetime limit, until its participation period ends: December 31 of the year of the earliest of its 15th anniversary, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal.

You buy a home another way. If you buy and live in a home without using your FHSA, a later withdrawal will not qualify until the look-back has cleared again. If you will live in that home for years, the practical exit is a direct transfer to an RRSP or RRIF before the account must close; our guide on how to transfer an investment account explains how direct transfers work. That transfer is tax-deferred and does not use RRSP room, provided you have no excess FHSA amount.

You move in with a homeowner. Your open FHSA is not affected. At withdrawal, the published test looks at homes you owned and lived in, so the partner's home does not stop a qualifying withdrawal for a new home, if you meet the other conditions.

You leave Canada. The account is not closed by the move, but a qualifying withdrawal requires you to be a resident of Canada. If you will not return to buy, plan a transfer or taxable withdrawal before the account's closing date.

You reach your closing year. Anything left in the account after the period ends loses its FHSA status, and its fair market value is added to your income for that year. Our FHSA calculator shows your closing year from your birth year and opening year.

12. Records That Prove Your FHSA Eligibility

Why records matter. Your eligibility rests on dates: when you moved in and out, when you bought and sold, when you married or separated. If your eligibility is ever questioned, dated records are what show it. Keep them for the life of the account and for several years after. The rules on this page are drawn only from published government sources, as our editorial standards require.

Home history. Keep the purchase and sale documents for any home you owned, plus records that show when you moved out, such as a lease start date, a change-of-address confirmation or utility account closing dates. The date you stopped living in an owned home sets your look-back clock.

Relationship history. Keep a note of the date you married or became common-law partners, and of any separation or divorce. The spouse part of the test turns on who was your current spouse or partner on opening day.

Residency. If you arrived in or returned to Canada recently, keep the documents that show when you became a resident, such as your arrival records, lease and first Canadian pay slips.

Withdrawal file. For a qualifying withdrawal, keep the signed written agreement to buy or build, with its completion date, your copy of Form RC725, and the closing statement that shows the date you acquired the home. These show the October 1 deadline and the 30-day window were met.

Contribution records. Keep every contribution receipt and statement. Your room depends on the year you opened and every dollar since, and the 1% monthly over-contribution tax applies from the first dollar over.

13. Common FHSA Eligibility Mistakes

Assuming past ownership disqualifies you forever. A home you sold and left before the look-back window does not count. Check the year you last lived in an owned home against the table in section 5 before ruling yourself out.

Counting 48 months instead of calendar years. The look-back is the current year before opening plus four full calendar years. Someone who moved out in December 2021 is eligible from January 1, 2026, not from December 2025.

Forgetting the spouse part. People living in a partner's home are often surprised to fail the test. If you plan to move into a home your partner owns, open your FHSA first.

Treating a parent's home as yours. Living in a parent's house does not fail the test. Young adults sometimes wait for no reason.

Waiting until 19 in a province where 18 is the age of majority. In Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec and Saskatchewan you can open at 18, and room starts the year you open.

Mixing up the opening and withdrawal tests. Passing the opening test does not guarantee a qualifying withdrawal. Buying a home with other money, or acquiring the home more than 30 days before you withdraw, can turn a planned tax-free withdrawal into taxable income.

Opening too late in life. Someone who turns 71 in the year they open has one calendar year of room. Opening a few years earlier adds $8,000 for each year, up to $40,000.

What You Need to Confirm FHSA Eligibility and What You Get

What you need to confirm before opening

  • That you are a resident of Canada on the day you open
  • Your age, and the age of majority in your province or territory
  • Your birth year, to see how many years of room you can use before 71
  • Every address you lived at in the current year and the previous four calendar years
  • Who owned each of those homes: you, your current spouse or partner, or someone else
  • The date your current relationship began, if you have a spouse or partner
  • The date you last moved out of a home you owned, if any
  • For newcomers, the date you became a resident and any home you owned and lived in abroad

What FHSA eligibility gets you

  • $8,000 of room in the year you open, and $8,000 each year after
  • A $40,000 lifetime limit, with up to $8,000 of unused room carried forward
  • A deduction for each contribution, now or in any later year
  • A tax-free qualifying withdrawal for a home that is never repaid
  • An account that stays open even if your situation changes, until its closing year
  • The option to use the Home Buyers' Plan for the same home
  • A direct transfer to an RRSP or RRIF without using RRSP room if you do not buy

How to Check Your FHSA Eligibility in Six Steps

  1. Confirm residency. You must be a resident of Canada on the day you open. Citizenship alone does not count.
  2. Check your age. At least 18, or 19 in British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut and Yukon, and no older than 71 at December 31 of the year you open.
  3. List your homes. Write down every home you lived in during the current year and the previous four calendar years, and who owned it.
  4. Apply the ownership test. If any of those homes was owned by you, or by the person who is your spouse or partner today, you are not yet eligible. Find the last year that was true and add five to get your first eligible year.
  5. Run the checker. Use the FHSA eligibility checker on this page to confirm the result, then work out your room with the FHSA calculator.
  6. Open in your first eligible year. Room of $8,000 is available for the full calendar year you open in, so there is no benefit in waiting. Compare providers in our best FHSA accounts ranking or read our Wealthsimple FHSA review.

10 FHSA Eligibility Strategies

1. Work out your exact first eligible year

Take the last calendar year you lived in a home owned by you or your current partner and add five. That year is your first eligible year, from January 1. Write it down, and open in that year so no room is lost.

2. Open any time in your first eligible year

Room of $8,000 applies to the whole calendar year you open your first FHSA. Opening in November gives the same room for that year as opening in January, though the 15-year clock also starts that year.

3. Move out in December, not January

If you are selling a home you live in and can choose your move-out date, moving out before December 31 rather than after can make you eligible a full calendar year sooner, because the look-back counts whole years.

4. Open before moving into a partner's home

The spouse part of the test applies only at opening. If you rent now and plan to move into a home your partner owns, open your FHSA before the move. The account stays open afterward.

5. Open at 18 where you can

In Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec and Saskatchewan, the minimum age is 18. Room does not build before you open, so an 18-year-old who expects to buy within about 15 years gains room by opening now.

6. Newcomers: use a TFSA while you wait

If a home you owned and lived in abroad keeps you out of an FHSA for a few years, a TFSA builds room from the day you became a resident and can hold your home savings until you can open an FHSA. See FHSA vs TFSA for a down payment.

7. After a breakdown, look at the HBP

The Home Buyers' Plan waives its first-time buyer test for someone living apart from a spouse or partner after a breakdown. If you have RRSP savings, the HBP may be open to you years before an FHSA.

8. Older buyers: open before your 71st year

Each year you open before the year you turn 71 adds $8,000 of possible room, up to $40,000. Someone born in 1960 who opens in 2026 has six calendar years; waiting until 2030 would leave two.

9. Line up the withdrawal with your closing date

Ask for your qualifying withdrawal before closing or within 30 days after you acquire the home. A withdrawal 31 or more days after closing fails the test and becomes taxable income.

10. Keep a dated home-history file

Save purchase and sale documents, move-out evidence and relationship dates. If your eligibility is ever questioned, these records show the look-back was clear on opening day and at withdrawal.

Illustrative Example: A Couple Waits Out the Look-Back

This example is hypothetical. Lena and Theo are married and live in Ontario. Theo owned a condo, and they both lived in it until they sold and moved out in March 2022. They have rented since. In October 2026 they ask whether they can open FHSAs.

  1. Theo in 2026. He lived in a home he owned in 2022. The 2026 look-back covers 2022 to 2025 plus 2026 before opening, so he is not yet eligible.
  2. Lena in 2026. She never owned the condo, but she lived in a home owned by her current spouse in 2022. She is not yet eligible either.
  3. Their first eligible year. 2022 + 5 = 2027. From January 1, 2027, the look-back covers 2023 to 2026 plus 2027 before opening, with no owned home in it. They each open an FHSA on January 4, 2027.
  4. Contributions. Each contributes $8,000 in January of 2027, 2028 and 2029, holding the money in a savings account. Interest is left out to keep the arithmetic simple: $24,000 each ($8,000 x 3) and $48,000 together ($24,000 x 2).
  5. The purchase. In February 2029 they sign an agreement to buy a house with a completion date of May 1, 2029, well before October 1, 2030. In April 2029 each requests a qualifying withdrawal of $24,000 on Form RC725. Both pass the withdrawal test: neither has lived in a home they owned since 2022.
  6. The Home Buyers' Plan. Each also withdraws $25,000 from an RRSP under the HBP. A first withdrawal in 2029 falls after the 2026 to 2028 relief years as published, so repayment starts in 2031 at a minimum of $1,666.67 a year each ($25,000 / 15).
  7. Total toward the home. $98,000 ($48,000 from two FHSAs + $50,000 from two HBP withdrawals).
  8. Closing the FHSAs. Their first qualifying withdrawals were in 2029, so both accounts must be closed by December 31, 2030.

FHSA Eligibility Questions Answered

What are the FHSA eligibility requirements?

To open an FHSA you must be a resident of Canada, at least 18 (or 19 where that is the age of majority), no older than 71 at December 31 of the year you open it, and a first-time home buyer. All four tests apply on the day you open the account. They are not checked again to keep the account open, but separate conditions apply to each qualifying withdrawal.

Who counts as a first-time home buyer for the FHSA?

For opening an FHSA, you are a first-time home buyer if you did not live in a home owned by you or your current spouse or common-law partner at any time in the current year before opening or in the previous four calendar years. You do not need to have never owned a home. If you last lived in a home you owned in 2021, you can open from January 1, 2026.

Can I open an FHSA if my spouse owns a home?

Not if you lived in it. If, in the current year before opening or the previous four calendar years, you lived in a home your current spouse or common-law partner owned, you fail the first-time home buyer test, even if your name is not on the title. If you open an FHSA before moving into your partner's home, the account stays open, because the opening test is applied only once.

Can I open an FHSA if I owned a home before?

Yes, once the look-back window has passed. The test counts the current year before opening plus the previous four calendar years. Someone who last lived in a home they owned in 2023 can open an FHSA from January 1, 2028. Owning a rental property you never lived in does not count against you at all.

Can I open an FHSA at 18?

Yes in Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec and Saskatchewan, where 18 is the age of majority. In British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut and Yukon you must be 19. FHSA room starts only in the year you open, so there is no catch-up for the year of waiting.

What is the oldest age to open an FHSA?

You must be 71 or younger at December 31 of the year you open the account. In 2026, that means anyone born in 1955 or later can open one. The account must close by December 31 of the year you turn 71, so someone born in 1955 who opens in 2026 has one year of room, $8,000.

Can newcomers to Canada open an FHSA?

Yes, once they are residents of Canada and meet the age and first-time home buyer tests. The published tests set no minimum time in Canada. Room starts at $8,000 in the year you open your first FHSA. If you owned and lived in a home abroad in the look-back years, plan as though it counts and confirm your facts before opening.

Can a non-resident open an FHSA?

No. You must be a resident of Canada to open an FHSA, whatever your citizenship. Residency is also a condition of a qualifying withdrawal. If you become a non-resident after opening, the move does not close the account, but you would need to be a resident again to make a qualifying withdrawal, or you could plan a transfer to an RRSP or RRIF.

Does living in my parents' home affect FHSA eligibility?

No. The first-time home buyer test looks only at homes owned by you or by your current spouse or common-law partner. Living in a home your parents own does not count against you, so an adult who lives with parents can open an FHSA once they meet the residency and age tests.

What happens to my FHSA if I buy a home without using it?

The account stays open until its participation period ends, but a later withdrawal will not qualify while you live in that home and for the following four calendar years. The other option is a direct transfer to an RRSP or RRIF, which is tax-deferred and does not use RRSP room, provided you have no excess FHSA amount.

Is the first-time home buyer test the same when I withdraw?

Not quite. At withdrawal, the look-back covers the current year before the withdrawal, ignoring the 30 days just before it, plus the previous four calendar years, and as published it looks only at homes you owned and lived in. You also need a written agreement with a completion date before October 1 of the following year, and you must not have acquired the home more than 30 days earlier.

Can I open an FHSA after a divorce or separation?

It depends on who owned the home. If you owned the family home and lived in it, your look-back runs from the last year you lived there. If only your former spouse or partner owned it, their ownership stops counting once they are no longer your spouse or partner. The Home Buyers' Plan has a breakdown relief that the FHSA opening test does not show.
