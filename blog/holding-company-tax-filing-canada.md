## Quick Answer: Holding Company Tax Filing in Canada

A holding company files the same T2 return as any other corporation, every year, and reports what it holds and what it received on that return.

Many owners set up a second corporation to hold shares of their operating company or a portfolio, then assume it has nothing to file. That assumption is where most problems start. Our corporate tax filing work for these corporations begins with one rule: the return is due whether or not anything happened. A CPA compilation of the year's statements usually supplies the financial information the return needs.

### TL;DR

- "Holding company" describes what a corporation owns. It is not a tax status and carries no filing exemption.
- A T2 is required for every tax year from incorporation to dissolution, nil years included (ITA 150(1)).
- Statement information goes in with the return, even when every balance is unchanged.
- Interest, rent and portfolio gains are income from property, reported apart from business income.
- A dividend from another corporation is tested every time: the deduction under ITA 112(1), then Part IV tax under ITA 186(1).
- Refundable tax comes back only when the corporation pays a taxable dividend, through two separate pools and an ordering rule (ITA 129(1)).
- Foreign property, foreign affiliates and transactions with related non-residents each have their own form, threshold and penalty.
- The return deadline and the balance-due date are two different clocks.

### Quick Comparison Table

| Receipt | Treatment | Where reported |
|---|---|---|

**Verdict:** Every receipt lands in a different place on the return, and dividends carry two tests, not one.

## People Also Ask

**Does a dormant holding company need to file a T2 return?**
Yes. A dormant holding company files a T2 for every tax year it exists, even with no income, no expenses and no tax payable. ITA 150(1) sets the filing duty, and CRA expects statement information with the return. Dormancy describes the year's activity; it is never an exemption from filing.

**Is a dividend from my operating company to my holding company tax-free?**
Not automatically. The dividend is first tested for the deduction under ITA 112(1), which depends on the payer and the relationship. Part IV tax under ITA 186(1) can then apply, for example where the payer received a dividend refund. Each dividend is tested on its facts before any result is stated.

**What is Part IV tax on a holding company?**
Part IV tax is a refundable tax a private corporation pays on certain dividends it receives. On portfolio dividends it is 38 1/3% of the dividend [EDITOR: verify]. On dividends from a connected corporation it equals the recipient's share of the payer's dividend refund. It is recovered only when the recipient pays taxable dividends.

**When is a holding company T2 return due?**
The T2 is due 6 months after the end of the tax year under ITA 150(1)(a). Any balance owing is due earlier, usually 2 months after year-end, or 3 months for certain Canadian-controlled private corporations [EDITOR: verify]. The two dates are separate, and interest runs from the balance-due date.

**Does a holding company need financial statements?**
A holding company must report financial statement information with its T2, through CRA's general index of financial information. That means a balance sheet and an income statement in the required format. Many owners have a CPA compilation prepared so the figures reported are organized and supported.

**Can a holding company get back the tax it pays on investment income?**
Part of the tax on investment income is refundable, but only through a dividend refund. The corporation must pay a taxable dividend to its shareholders, and the refund is limited by two tracked pools and an ordering rule under ITA 129(1). Nothing comes back automatically when the return is filed.

**Does my holding company's investment income affect my operating company?**
It can. Where the corporations are associated, passive investment income over $50,000 across the group reduces the business limit available to the group under ITA 125(5.1) [EDITOR: verify]. Whether corporations are associated is a factual question settled in an engagement, not assumed.

**Does the T2 return cover foreign investments held by a holding company?**
No. Foreign property with a total cost over $100,000 is reported on Form T1135 under ITA 233.3 [EDITOR: verify]. Foreign affiliates and transactions with related non-residents have their own forms. Each form has its own deadline and penalty, and filing the T2 does not satisfy any of them.

**What happens if a holding company does not file for several years?**
Each unfiled year remains an open obligation. CRA can charge late-filing penalties where tax was owing, can issue demands to file, and can raise assessments. Penalties on separate forms can apply even when no tax is owed. The usual route is to file the missing years in order with complete records.

**Is a nil T2 return different from a regular T2?**
A nil return is a regular T2 for a year where taxable income and tax payable are zero. It still carries identification, shareholder information, statement information and any schedules the facts trigger. The word "nil" describes the result of the year, not a different or shorter obligation.

**How long must a holding company keep its records?**
A corporation generally keeps books and records for 6 years from the end of the last tax year they relate to, as CRA's record-keeping guidance explains [EDITOR: verify]. Records supporting share cost, dividends and pool balances often matter for much longer, since those amounts carry forward.

**Do I need to file instalments for a holding company?**
Instalments are required where tax payable was more than $3,000 in the current or previous tax year [EDITOR: verify]. A holding company with investment income can cross that line. Refundable taxes do not remove the instalment requirement, and missed instalments attract interest.

## Must a Holding Company File a T2 Every Year?

Yes. A holding company files a T2 for every tax year it exists, from incorporation to dissolution, whether or not it earned income or owes tax (ITA 150(1)).

The filing duty in ITA 150(1)(a) applies to every corporation resident in Canada, with narrow exceptions that do not cover ordinary holding companies [EDITOR: verify the filing positions]. Nothing in the Act classifies a corporation as a holding company. The label tells you what the corporation owns. The return is the ordinary T2, on the ordinary clock.

The trigger is existence. The form is the T2 with its schedules and statement information. The exposure, if a year is missed, is a late-filing penalty where tax was owing, interest, demands to file, and penalties on separately required forms.

Two separate tracks sit beside the return. First, the return itself: income, deductions, dividends received, refundable taxes and pools. Second, obligations that attach to what the corporation holds: foreign property, foreign affiliates and transactions with related non-residents. The second track is never met by filing the first.

> **CRA Requirement:** A corporation files a T2 for every tax year it exists, incorporation to dissolution, whether tax is payable and whether anything happened or not, with the statement information the return carries. A separate obligation on what it holds, such as foreign property reporting, is not met by that return.

## What Is a Holding Company for Tax?

For tax purposes, a holding company is simply a corporation; the term describes what it owns and changes neither its return nor its deadlines.

A holding company usually owns shares of one or more operating companies, a portfolio of securities, a loan receivable, or real property. The Income Tax Act does not define the term. What the Act does care about is the character of income (business or property), the payer of each dividend, the relationship between corporations, and whether the corporation is a Canadian-controlled private corporation. Each of those is a fact question.

This guide does not say a holding company saves tax, defers tax, or suits any reader. Whether a structure fits depends on facts. Creating, collapsing or restructuring a structure, an estate freeze and surplus stripping are each matters for a professional in an engagement and, for the corporate law side, a lawyer.

> **Our Take:** The word "holding" causes more missed filings than any rule in the Act. Owners hear it and think "passive, so nothing to do." The return does not care how busy the year was.

## Which Return and Which Tax Year?

A holding company files the T2 Corporation Income Tax Return for each tax year, and the first tax year starts on the date of incorporation (ITA 150(1), ITA 249).

The tax year ends on the year-end the corporation chooses, which cannot be more than 53 weeks after the year began. A corporation incorporated mid-year often has a short first tax year. It still files for that short year. The tax year, not the calendar year, sets both deadlines.

Changing a fiscal period later needs CRA concurrence in most cases. That topic, and the 53-week ceiling, is covered in our separate guide on changing a fiscal period; we do not re-explain it here.

Ontario corporations report Ontario tax on the same T2 through the Ontario schedules. Corporations in other provinces file the same federal return with their own provincial schedules, except where a province administers its own corporate return [EDITOR: verify the provincial filing positions].

| Filing by Year | Hypothetical corporation (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Incorporated | Date of incorporation; first tax year begins |
| First tax year | Short year ending on the chosen year-end; T2 required |
| Second tax year | Full 12-month year; T2 required |
| Year with no transactions | T2 required; statement information still attached |
| Final tax year | Ends on dissolution or deemed year-end; final T2 required |

**Verdict:** One return per tax year, every tax year, with no gaps between incorporation and dissolution.

## What If It Did Nothing All Year?

A holding company with no activity still files a T2 for that year, with statement information, because ITA 150(1) has no dormancy exemption.

"Dormant," "inactive" and "nil" describe a year's activity. None is an exemption. A quiet year can still hold facts the return needs: a balance owing to a shareholder, an investment carried at cost, a pool balance carried forward, shares held in a connected corporation. The return records them so the next year opens on the right numbers.

A late nil return is not consequence-free. Where no tax is owing, the late-filing penalty computed on unpaid tax may be zero, but CRA can still issue a demand to file, and separate forms tied to holdings carry their own fixed penalties. Unfiled years also block refunds, delay clearance on dissolution and make later years harder to support.

| Nil Year | What still applies |
|---|---|
| T2 return | Required (ITA 150(1)) |
| Statement information | Required, even with unchanged balances |
| Shareholder information | Required |
| Pool balances | Carried forward and reported |
| Foreign reporting forms | Required if a threshold is met, regardless of income |
| Penalty exposure if late | Demand to file; fixed penalties on separate forms; penalty on any tax owing |

**Verdict:** A nil year changes the numbers on the return, not the duty to file it.

> **Risk Warning:** We regularly see a holding company left unfiled for years because nothing happened in it, dividends recorded as tax-free with payer status and relationship never tested, a refundable balance treated as money the corporation simply receives, and foreign holdings never reported on their own form. Each of these starts small and compounds.

## What Statement Information Goes In?

A holding company reports its balance sheet and income statement information with the T2, using CRA's general index of financial information, every tax year.

The general index is the standard coded format CRA uses to capture financial statement data [EDITOR: verify the GIFI guidance]. A holding company's balance sheet usually shows cash, investments in shares at cost, loans receivable, amounts due to or from shareholders and connected corporations, and share capital. Its income statement shows interest, dividends, rent, gains and modest expenses.

Statement information is not a substitute for proper books. If the figures are not supported, the return is not supported. Lenders and other users may want compiled financial statements; the reasons they treat compiled and other statements differently are covered in a separate guide.

| Statement Information | What a holding company typically reports |
|---|---|
| Cash | Bank and investment account cash balances |
| Investments in shares | Shares of connected and non-connected corporations, at cost |
| Portfolio investments | Securities held, usually at cost |
| Loans receivable | Amounts lent to connected corporations or others |
| Due to or from shareholder | Shareholder account balance |
| Share capital | Issued shares and stated capital |
| Retained earnings | Opening, income, dividends paid, closing |
| Income | Interest, dividends, rent, gains, by type |
| Expenses | Professional fees, bank charges, interest, property costs |

**Verdict:** Report every balance, including unchanged ones, so the return matches the books year after year.

## Which Schedules Does It File?

A holding company files the schedules its facts trigger, most often the dividends received schedule, the investment income schedule, shareholder information and related corporation information.

Schedules are named here by what they do. Your preparer will map each to its CRA schedule number.

| Schedules | Trigger | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Shareholder information | Every corporation | Shareholders holding 10% or more of any class |
| Related and associated corporations | Any related or associated corporation | Names, relationships, business numbers |
| Dividends received, paid and Part IV tax | Any taxable dividend received or paid | Payer, connection, amount, Part IV computation, dividends paid |
| Aggregate investment income | CCPC with investment income | Income from property, taxable capital gains, refundable portion |
| Capital gains or losses | Disposition of capital property | Proceeds, cost, gain or loss |
| General rate income pool | CCPC paying or designating eligible dividends | Pool continuity and designation |
| Capital dividend account | Capital dividends received or paid | Covered in a separate guide |
| Ontario schedules | Ontario-resident corporation | Ontario tax and credits |
| Statement information | Every corporation | Balance sheet and income statement |

**Verdict:** The schedule list grows with the holdings, not with the corporation's activity level.

## How Is Investment Income Reported?

A holding company reports interest, rent, royalties and portfolio income as income from property, kept apart from active business income, on the T2 and its investment income schedule.

The line between property income and business income turns on the facts. Interest on a term deposit is usually property income. Rent can be property income or, with enough activity and employees, business income. Income from property is not taxed like active business income. For a CCPC, it is taxed at a higher rate with part of the tax refundable, and it does not get the small business deduction.

Investment income also feeds aggregate investment income, which drives the refundable portion of Part I tax under ITA 129(4) and ITA 123.3. That refundable amount goes into a pool. It is not refunded on filing.

| Investment Income by Source | Character | Reported on |
|---|---|---|
| Interest on deposits and loans | Income from property | Income statement information; investment income schedule |
| Rent from real property | Property income unless facts show a business | Income statement information; investment income schedule |
| Royalties | Usually income from property | Income statement information; investment income schedule |
| Foreign interest and dividends | Income from property; foreign tax credit may apply | Income statement information; foreign reporting forms if triggered |
| Taxable capital gains | Part of aggregate investment income | Capital gains schedule; investment income schedule |
| Taxable dividends from Canadian corporations | Tested separately under ITA 112(1) and ITA 186 | Dividends received schedule |

**Verdict:** Sort every receipt by source before it touches the return.

> **Key Stat:** The refundable portion of Part I tax on a CCPC's investment income is 30 2/3% of aggregate investment income, subject to limits in ITA 129(4) [EDITOR: verify against the current T4012]. That amount is added to a pool, not paid back on filing.

## How Is a Dividend From Another Corporation Reported?

A holding company reports each taxable dividend received on the dividends received schedule and tests it for the ITA 112(1) deduction; the result depends on the payer and the relationship.

ITA 112(1) allows a corporation to deduct, in computing taxable income, taxable dividends received from taxable Canadian corporations, subject to conditions and exceptions. The test is not a formality. Payer status matters: a foreign corporation's dividend does not qualify. The relationship matters for Part IV tax. Other provisions in the Act can change the result for particular dividends, which is one reason each dividend is reviewed in an engagement.

Two limbs, every time:

1. Is the dividend deductible under ITA 112(1), on the payer's status and the facts?
2. Does Part IV tax apply under ITA 186(1), on the relationship and on whether the payer got a dividend refund?

| Dividend by Payer Type | Deduction test (ITA 112(1)) | Part IV test (ITA 186(1)) |
|---|---|---|
| Taxable Canadian corporation, connected | Tested on the facts | Share of the payer's dividend refund, if any |
| Taxable Canadian corporation, not connected | Tested on the facts | 38 1/3% of the dividend for a private corporation recipient [EDITOR: verify rate] |
| Public company (portfolio) | Tested on the facts | 38 1/3% of the dividend [EDITOR: verify rate] |
| Foreign corporation | Not available under ITA 112(1) | Not applicable; foreign rules apply |
| Capital dividend | Separate regime; covered in a separate guide | Not applicable |

**Verdict:** No dividend is labelled tax-free until both limbs are tested on its own facts.

## When Does Part IV Tax Apply?

Part IV tax applies to a private corporation that receives portfolio dividends, and to one that receives dividends from a connected payer that got a dividend refund on that payment (ITA 186(1)).

A payer is connected if the recipient controls it, or holds more than 10% of its issued shares by votes and by value (ITA 186(4)). Connection is decided on the facts and is never assumed from a name or an org chart.

For a non-connected payer, Part IV tax is 38 1/3% of the taxable dividend [EDITOR: verify rate]. For a connected payer, Part IV tax equals the recipient's share of the dividend refund the payer received on that dividend. Part IV tax goes into the recipient's refundable pools. It is recovered only when the recipient pays taxable dividends of its own.

| Connected Against Portfolio Payer | Connected payer | Portfolio payer |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Controlled, or more than 10% of votes and value (ITA 186(4)) | Neither condition met |
| Part IV base | Share of the payer's dividend refund | The dividend received |
| Part IV rate | Not a flat rate; follows the payer's refund | 38 1/3% [EDITOR: verify rate] |
| Pool affected | Follows the pool the payer's refund came from | Eligible or non-eligible, by dividend type |
| Determination | On the facts, in an engagement | On the facts, in an engagement |

| Part IV Trigger | Part IV result |
|---|---|
| Portfolio dividend received by a private corporation | Part IV applies at 38 1/3% [EDITOR: verify rate] |
| Connected payer, no dividend refund to payer | No Part IV on that dividend |
| Connected payer, dividend refund to payer | Part IV equals share of payer's refund |
| Dividend from a foreign corporation | Not a Part IV item |
| Capital dividend | Not a Part IV item |

**Verdict:** Part IV depends on the payer's refund and the relationship, so the payer's return matters to the recipient's.

## How Is the Refundable Portion Recovered?

Refundable tax is recovered only through a dividend refund when the corporation pays a taxable dividend, limited by two separate pools and an ordering rule (ITA 129(1)).

There are two pools: eligible refundable dividend tax on hand and non-eligible refundable dividend tax on hand. Part IV tax on eligible portfolio dividends generally goes to the eligible pool. The refundable portion of Part I tax and Part IV tax on non-eligible dividends generally go to the non-eligible pool [EDITOR: verify pool allocation].

The dividend refund is 38 1/3% of taxable dividends paid in the year, but never more than the relevant pool balance [EDITOR: verify rate]. The ordering rule: an eligible dividend paid draws only on the eligible pool. A non-eligible dividend paid draws on the non-eligible pool first, then on the eligible pool. If no taxable dividend is paid, the balance stays in the pool and carries forward.

| Pool Continuity (illustrative) | Opening | Added | Refunded | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eligible pool | $0 | $1,150 | $0 | $1,150 |
| Non-eligible pool | $2,000 | $3,680 | $0 | $5,680 |

| Refund Ordering | Draws on |
|---|---|
| Eligible dividend paid | Eligible pool only |
| Non-eligible dividend paid | Non-eligible pool first, then eligible pool |
| No taxable dividend paid | Nothing; balances carry forward |
| Capital dividend paid | Not a taxable dividend; no refund |

**Verdict:** A pool balance is a potential recovery tied to a future dividend, not cash.

> **Pro Tip:** Keep a one-page pool continuity schedule for each tax year, with opening, additions, refunds and closing for both pools. It takes minutes once and saves hours when a dividend is finally paid.

## What Does Passive Income Do to Active Income?

Where corporations are associated, passive investment income over $50,000 across the group reduces the business limit available to the group, under ITA 125(5.1) [EDITOR: verify threshold].

The reduction is $5 of business limit for every $1 of adjusted aggregate investment income over $50,000 in the previous tax year, and it eliminates the limit at $150,000 [EDITOR: verify]. A holding company usually does not claim the small business deduction itself, but its passive income can affect an associated operating company.

This is a pointer only. The business limit, its sharing among associated corporations and the grind are explained in our small business deduction guide. Whether two corporations are associated is decided on the facts in an engagement.

## What Must Be Reported on Foreign Holdings?

A holding company with specified foreign property costing over $100,000 at any time in the year files Form T1135 under ITA 233.3, separately from the T2 [EDITOR: verify threshold].

Foreign holdings carry their own track. Each has a trigger, a form and a penalty, and none is met by filing the T2.

| Foreign Triggers | Threshold | Form | Penalty for late filing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specified foreign property | Total cost over $100,000 at any time in the year (ITA 233.3) | T1135 | $25 per day, up to $2,500, plus larger penalties in some cases [EDITOR: verify] |
| Foreign affiliate | Any foreign affiliate at any time in the year (ITA 233.4) | T1134 | $25 per day, up to $2,500, plus larger penalties in some cases [EDITOR: verify] |
| Transactions with related non-residents | Total over $1,000,000 in the year (ITA 233.1) | T106 | $25 per day, up to $2,500, plus larger penalties in some cases [EDITOR: verify] |

Specified foreign property includes foreign bank accounts, shares of foreign corporations that are not foreign affiliates, and debts owed by non-residents. Whether a holding triggers a form is decided on the facts, including cost, ownership and timing.

**Verdict:** Screen every foreign holding against each form, every year, independent of the T2 result.

## When Is the Return Due, and the Balance?

The T2 is due 6 months after the tax year ends, while any balance owing is due 2 months after year-end, or 3 months for qualifying CCPCs (ITA 150(1), ITA 248(1)) [EDITOR: verify].

These are two clocks. The return clock runs 6 months. The payment clock runs 2 or 3 months, depending on corporation type and conditions in the balance-due day definition. A holding company that does not claim the small business deduction may not meet the 3-month conditions, so the 2-month date often applies [EDITOR: verify the 3-month conditions]. Interest runs from the balance-due date, not the filing date.

| Return Against Balance-Due | Due | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| T2 return | 6 months after year-end | ITA 150(1)(a) |
| Balance owing, general | 2 months after year-end | ITA 248(1) |
| Balance owing, qualifying CCPC | 3 months after year-end | ITA 248(1); conditions apply |
| Foreign reporting forms | Same date as the T2 for T1135 and T106; T1134 on its own date [EDITOR: verify] | ITA 233.1, 233.3, 233.4 |

Instalments apply where tax payable was more than $3,000 in the current or previous tax year [EDITOR: verify].

| Instalments | Rule |
|---|---|
| Threshold | Tax payable over $3,000 in current or previous year [EDITOR: verify] |
| Frequency | Monthly; quarterly for certain small CCPCs [EDITOR: verify] |
| Refundable taxes | Do not remove the instalment requirement |
| Shortfall | Instalment interest, and penalty in some cases |

> **Key Stat:** The late-filing penalty is 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per complete month late, up to 12 months; for a repeated failure, 10% plus 2% per month, up to 20 months (ITA 162(1), 162(2)) [EDITOR: verify against the current T4012].

**Verdict:** Pay by the balance-due date and file by the filing date; one never stands in for the other.

## Best Practices for a Holding Company

Best practice for a holding company is a fixed annual routine: file every year, test every dividend, track both pools, and screen foreign holdings.

**Filing discipline**

1. Diarize the 6-month filing date and the 2- or 3-month balance-due date separately.
2. File every tax year, including nil years.
3. Keep a register of all returns filed and notices received.
4. Plan for the final return in the year of dissolution.

**Books and statements**

5. Keep monthly bookkeeping current, even with few transactions.
6. Reconcile the shareholder account and intercorporate balances each year-end.
7. Have statement information supported by books or a compilation.

**Dividends and pools**

8. Record every dividend received by payer, date, amount and relationship.
9. Ask the payer whether it claimed a dividend refund on each dividend.
10. Keep separate eligible and non-eligible pool continuity.
11. Review the eligible designation against the general rate income pool before paying.

**Investment income and group**

12. Sort investment income by source each year.
13. Measure adjusted aggregate investment income for the group annually.

**Foreign and records**

14. Screen foreign holdings against each form, every year.
15. Keep records for at least 6 years, and share cost and pool support for longer.
16. Bring in a tax planning engagement before any change to the structure.

**Does a holding company with no income file a T2?**

Yes. A holding company with no income files a T2 for every tax year it exists. ITA 150(1) sets the duty, and it has no dormancy exemption.

The return still carries statement information, shareholder information and any schedules the facts trigger. A quiet year is often the easiest year to file, and it keeps the next year's opening numbers clean.

**Is a dividend from my operating company tax-free to my holding company?**

Not automatically. The dividend is tested for the deduction under ITA 112(1), based on the payer's status and the facts.

Part IV tax under ITA 186(1) is then reviewed. If the payer is connected and claimed a dividend refund on that payment, Part IV tax applies equal to the recipient's share of that refund. Each dividend is tested before any result is stated.

**What is Part IV tax?**

Part IV tax is a refundable tax a private corporation pays on certain dividends it receives. On portfolio dividends it is 38 1/3% of the dividend [EDITOR: verify].

On connected-payer dividends, it follows the payer's dividend refund. Part IV tax goes into the recipient's pools and is recovered only when the recipient pays taxable dividends.

**How do I get the refundable tax back?**

The corporation must pay a taxable dividend. The dividend refund is 38 1/3% of taxable dividends paid, capped by the pool balance [EDITOR: verify].

There are two pools, eligible and non-eligible, and an ordering rule decides which is drawn. If no dividend is paid, balances carry forward.

**When is the T2 due?**

The T2 is due 6 months after the end of the tax year, under ITA 150(1)(a). The tax year end, not the calendar year, sets the date.

**When is the balance due?**

The balance is generally due 2 months after year-end. Certain Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting conditions in ITA 248(1) have 3 months [EDITOR: verify].

A holding company may not meet the 3-month conditions. Interest runs from the balance-due date, so paying with the return can be late.

**Does the T2 cover foreign property?**

No. Specified foreign property costing over $100,000 at any time in the year is reported on Form T1135 [EDITOR: verify].

Foreign affiliates use Form T1134, and transactions with related non-residents over $1,000,000 use Form T106 [EDITOR: verify]. Each form has its own penalty.

**Does a holding company need statements?**

The T2 requires statement information in CRA's standard format. That means a balance sheet and an income statement.

Many owners have a CPA compilation prepared so the figures are organized and supported. A compilation is not an audit or review.

**Can investment income affect my operating company?**

Yes, where the corporations are associated. Adjusted aggregate investment income over $50,000 in the previous year reduces the group's business limit under ITA 125(5.1) [EDITOR: verify].

Whether corporations are associated is a factual question, settled in an engagement.

**What if I have unfiled years?**

File each year in order, starting with the earliest, with complete records. Penalties and interest depend on the facts.

Relief and disclosure are named routes decided in an engagement. Please speak with a CPA before filing the first catch-up year.
