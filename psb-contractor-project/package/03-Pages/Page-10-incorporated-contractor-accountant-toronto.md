### H2: How does PSB risk show up for Toronto contractors?

A personal services business, or PSB, is defined in section 125(7) of the Income Tax Act. It applies when the person doing the work, called the incorporated employee, or someone related to them, owns 10% or more of any class of the corporation's shares, directly or indirectly. It also requires that the incorporated employee would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the client if the corporation did not exist. I explain the full rule on my page about [personal services business risk](https://gondaliyacpa.ca/psb-risk-incorporated-contractors/).

There are two exceptions. The first is a corporation that employs more than five full-time employees in the business throughout the year. That means at least six full-time employees, all year, and whether owners or family members count depends on their real roles, so it needs review. The second is income received from an associated corporation. For a one-person contractor corporation, neither one usually applies.

#### H3: The agency and the end client

Many Toronto contracts have three parties: your corporation, a staffing agency and the end client. CRA looks at whether you would reasonably be an employee of the party the services are really provided to. The agency contract and the end client's onboarding rules both matter. My page on [staffing agency contractor tax](https://gondaliyacpa.ca/staffing-agency-contractor-tax/) covers this setup.

#### H3: Hours, timesheets and equipment

Contracts at 35, 37.5 or 40 hours a week are common here. So are set schedules, timesheets approved by the end client, onsite days or VPN-only access, and client-supplied laptops. CRA reads these as control indicators.

Hours alone never decide PSB status. CRA tests the whole relationship, year by year. The tests come from the Wiebe Door and Sagaz cases, and the central question is whose business it is. The factors are control, ownership of tools and equipment, chance of profit and risk of loss, and whether the work is part of the client's business or your own.

#### H3: What PSB status would cost

The 44.5% PSB rate is 33% federal plus 11.5% Ontario. A PSB gets no small business deduction and no 13% federal general rate reduction, and it pays an extra 5% federal tax under section 123.5. The gap is large. Take $150,000 of income kept in the corporation:

- At the Ontario small business rate of 11.2%, the tax is $16,800.
- At the PSB rate of 44.5%, the tax is $66,750.
- The extra tax is $49,950 for one year.

The 11.2% rate applies from July 1, 2026. Income earned before that date was taxed at 12.2%. A corporation with a December 31, 2026 year-end gets a blended rate of 11.70%, which is $17,543.84 on the same $150,000.

PSB status also limits deductions under section 18(1)(p). Only a short list stays deductible: salary and other pay to you, benefits or allowances provided to you, certain costs of selling property or negotiating contracts that an employee could deduct, and legal costs to collect fees owed for your services. Rent, software, equipment, travel, phone, insurance and accounting fees are denied.

No one can promise how CRA will see your file. What we can do is review your facts each year, reduce risk where the facts allow it, and document your position.
