12 Contract Clauses That Raise PSB Risk for IT and Finance Contractors

Written by Sharad Gondaliya, CPA (Canada and USA). Last updated: October 20, 2026.

Contract clauses raise PSB risk when they make you look like the client's employee rather than the owner of an Ontario business: fixed hours, a client laptop, a client manager, no substitution, exclusivity and no-risk hourly pay. No single clause decides. CRA weighs the whole relationship, and the gap is 11.2% small business tax versus 44.5% on PSB income.

Why do contract clauses matter for PSB risk?

Under section 125(7) of the Income Tax Act, your corporation carries on a personal services business if you, or a person related to you, own 10% or more of any class of its shares, directly or indirectly, and you would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the client if your corporation did not exist. If your corporation employs more than five full-time employees throughout the year, or it is paid by a corporation associated with it, an exception can apply. Most one-person contractor corporations meet neither exception, so the working relationship decides it. Our page on personal services business risk explains the full rules.

CRA can ask for your contracts in a PSB review, and they show how the relationship was set up. Many IT and finance contractors work through a staffing agency, so there are often three layers of paper: the agency agreement, the statement of work and the end client's onboarding package. CRA looks at whether you would reasonably be an employee of the party you really provide the services to, which can be the end client. Our page on staffing agency contractor tax covers that setup.

The cost of getting it wrong is large. In Ontario, small business income is taxed at a combined 11.2% from July 1, 2026. Income earned before that date was taxed at 12.2%, so a December 31, 2026 year-end gets a blended 11.70%. PSB income is taxed at 44.5%, and section 18(1)(p) denies most expenses.

How should you read this list?

Each clause below can point toward employment. None of them decides the question alone. CRA uses the tests from Wiebe Door Services Ltd. v. M.N.R. and 671122 Ontario Ltd. v. Sagaz Industries Canada Inc. The central question is whose business it is. The factors are control, ownership of tools and equipment, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration. Hiring helpers, investment and management also count.

Two more points. CRA tests what actually happens, not only what the contract says. And the test applies year by year, so a renewal can change the picture. If your main worry is the hours clause, read our post on whether a 40 hour contract makes you an incorporated employee.

What are the 12 contract clauses that raise PSB risk?

1. Fixed weekly hours

Why it matters. A statement of work that sets 35, 37.5 or 40 hours a week, often with core hours, tells CRA the client decides when you work. That is a control indicator.

What can weigh the other way. Hours alone never decide PSB status. A set number of billable hours can simply be how the client buys capacity. It weighs less if you choose your own start and end times, take time off without needing approval, and the hours work as a billing cap rather than a schedule you must keep.

2. Required onsite days or location

Why it matters. Many IT and finance roles require a set number of days each week in the client's office. Being told where to work, and on which days, is a control indicator. A desk on the client's floor beside staff doing the same work also points to integration.

What can weigh the other way. Some location rules exist for security or regulatory reasons, such as access to restricted systems or trading floors. A rule that applies to every vendor carries less weight than a rule that applies only to staff. It also helps if you choose your remote days yourself.

3. Client-supplied laptop and VPN-only access

Why it matters. At banks, insurers and government clients, you usually get a client laptop, client software and access only through the client's VPN. Under the tools test, the work is done with the client's equipment, not your corporation's.

What can weigh the other way. In regulated sectors, data security rules often require this of every vendor, so it can carry less weight there. It helps if your corporation owns and pays for its own equipment, software licences, training and certifications that you use across clients. If the income were treated as PSB income, those costs would be denied, but they still show your corporation invests in its own business.

4. Reporting to a client manager

Why it matters. A statement of work that names a client manager you report to, who assigns your tasks, sets priorities and approves your methods, points to control. If you sit in a client team with staff doing the same work, it also points to integration.

What can weigh the other way. Every client sets goals and checks results. What matters is whether the manager directs how you do the work or only accepts what you deliver. A defined scope, clear deliverables and your own methods weigh the other way.

5. Timesheet approval by the end client

Why it matters. When the end client approves your weekly timesheet before the agency pays you, the client is checking your time. If that approval is used to manage attendance, it points to control.

What can weigh the other way. Timesheet approval is often just billing support. The agency needs proof of hours before it invoices the end client. It weighs less if approval only confirms the hours billed and nobody uses it to manage when you work. Invoices issued by your corporation, on its own terms, also help.

6. Exclusivity or non-compete

Why it matters. A clause that stops your corporation from serving other clients during the contract, or from working in the same field after it ends, limits your chance of profit. A business can usually serve more than one client at a time. It can also point to control.

What can weigh the other way. A narrow non-solicit is a different thing. It stops you from going around the agency to contract with the end client directly, and it is common in agency contracts. It weighs less if your corporation actually has other clients, or markets itself to them, during the year.

7. Termination at will on short notice with no cancellation fee

Why it matters. If the client or agency can end the contract at any time, on short notice, with no cancellation fee and no payment for planned work, the client holds the power in the relationship. That fits a control pattern more than a deal between two businesses with protection on both sides.

What can weigh the other way. This is a weaker indicator on its own. Employees usually have notice rights, so a contract with no notice either way does not fit employment cleanly either. A mutual right to end the contract, payment for work in progress, or a fee for early cancellation all weigh the other way.

8. Personal services only, no substitution

Why it matters. A clause that says only you can do the work, and you cannot send a substitute or hire a helper, is one of the stronger indicators. Personal service is a feature of employment. A business can usually decide who does the work and earn a margin on helpers.

What can weigh the other way. A real right to send a qualified substitute, at your corporation's cost, weighs the other way, but only if the client would accept one in practice. Security clearance often makes that hard. If your corporation has ever paid a subcontractor or helper, keep the records.

9. Mandatory client training and performance reviews

Why it matters. Required training on the client's internal processes, annual staff courses and formal performance reviews are how a client manages its own people. They point to control and integration.

What can weigh the other way. Regulated clients require some training, such as anti-money laundering, privacy and information security, from everyone who touches their systems, including vendors. That carries less weight than staff-style reviews, goals and ratings. Feedback on a deliverable is not the same as a performance review.

10. Client code of conduct, badges and policies

Why it matters. Onboarding packages often ask you to sign the client's employee code of conduct, follow HR policies, carry a staff badge, use a client email address and appear on the org chart. Each makes you look like part of the client's organization, which points to integration.

What can weigh the other way. A contractor badge that differs from staff badges, a vendor code of conduct rather than the staff handbook, and your own corporate email for your business all weigh the other way. Security and confidentiality policies that apply to every vendor carry less weight.

11. Open-ended term with automatic renewals

Why it matters. A contract with no end date, or one renewed again and again for years, suggests ongoing work inside the client's business rather than a defined project. That points to integration. Finance contractors often fill a seat, such as a controller or FP&A role, that staff would normally hold.

What can weigh the other way. A defined project with deliverables and an end date weighs the other way. So does a real change of scope at each renewal, or work for other clients between assignments. Long engagements happen between businesses too. What matters is whether the work stays a project or becomes a seat.

12. Hourly pay with no financial risk

Why it matters. If you are paid by the hour for every hour worked, with no fixed price, no cost for overruns and no bad debt risk, your corporation cannot really make or lose money beyond your own time. That points away from chance of profit and risk of loss.

What can weigh the other way. Hourly billing is normal for many businesses, so it is not decisive. It weighs less if your corporation carries real costs and risks: unpaid time between contracts, the cost of fixing its own errors, insurance it pays for, and investment in equipment and training. Negotiating your own rate also helps.

What could PSB status cost if these clauses describe your work?

Here is an illustration, not a client file. An IT contractor bills $95 an hour for 37.5 hours a week, 48 weeks a year, through an agency. Revenue is $171,000. The corporation spends $9,000 on its own laptop, software, insurance and accounting. To keep it simple, assume a full taxation year at 11.2% and no salary.

  • Not PSB: ($171,000 minus $9,000) x 11.2% = $18,144.
  • PSB: the $9,000 of expenses is denied under section 18(1)(p), so $171,000 x 44.5% = $76,095.
  • Extra corporate tax: $57,951 in one year.

Now say the corporation pays the contractor a salary of $120,000. Salary paid to you stays deductible even under the PSB rules.

  • Not PSB: $171,000 minus $9,000 minus $120,000 = $42,000, x 11.2% = $4,704.
  • PSB: $171,000 minus $120,000 = $51,000, x 44.5% = $22,695.
  • Extra corporate tax: $17,991.

I have left employer CPP out of these figures and not shown the personal tax on the salary. Salary narrows the gap but does not remove it. For a December 31, 2026 year-end, the small business figures would use the blended 11.70% rate.

What can you do about the clauses in your contract?

Start by reading your agency agreement, statement of work and onboarding package against this list. Then write down how the work actually happens. If the paper and the facts differ, the facts usually carry more weight.

Keep records that support your position: invoices to other clients, receipts for your own equipment and software licences, your insurance policy, and any payment to a subcontractor. Our PSB risk checklist lists the records worth keeping.

Frequently asked questions

Does one of these clauses make my corporation a PSB?

No. Each clause can point toward employment, but CRA weighs control, tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, and integration across the whole relationship, year by year. Many IT and finance contracts contain several of these clauses, so the working facts matter.

Which of the 12 clauses matters most?

There is no fixed ranking. Clauses that require you to do the work personally, or that let the client direct how you work, often go to the heart of the question. Even then, CRA weighs them with everything else.

Can I lower PSB risk by removing these clauses from my contract?

Contract wording is for your lawyer and the agency to agree. We are accountants, not lawyers. Also, CRA tests what actually happens. If the wording changes but the work stays the same, the change carries little weight.

My agency agreement looks fine, but the end client's onboarding papers do not. Which one counts?

Both. With an agency in the middle, CRA asks whether you would reasonably be an employee of the party that really receives your services. The end client's rules on hours, equipment, training and conduct can matter as much as the agency agreement.

Does a client laptop on its own mean my corporation is a PSB?

No. A client laptop with VPN-only access points to the client's tools, and banks and insurers often require it of every vendor. It is one factor, weighed with all the others.

If I pay myself a salary, do these clauses still matter?

Yes. Salary paid to you stays deductible under section 18(1)(p), which reduces the income exposed to the 44.5% rate. Whatever profit stays in the corporation would still be taxed at 44.5% instead of 11.2% if CRA treated it as PSB income.

Can CRA give me a ruling on my contract?

Not a PSB ruling for income tax. Form CPT1 asks CRA for a CPP and EI ruling on a worker's status. That ruling does not decide PSB status, although CRA looks at similar facts. If you are thinking about one, we first review whether it is useful in your facts.
