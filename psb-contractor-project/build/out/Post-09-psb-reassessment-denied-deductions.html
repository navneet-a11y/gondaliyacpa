What Happens in a PSB Reassessment: Denied Deductions and Double Tax

Written by Sharad Gondaliya, CPA (Canada and USA). Last updated: October 20, 2026.

In a PSB reassessment, CRA re-taxes your Ontario corporation's past income as personal services business income. The small business deduction is denied, the rate becomes 44.5% (versus 12.2% before July 1, 2026 and 11.2% now), and most expenses are denied under section 18(1)(p). Arrears interest is added, and dividends then carry personal tax on top.

What is a PSB reassessment?

A PSB reassessment happens when CRA reviews corporate tax returns you have already filed and decides that the corporation carried on a personal services business (PSB) in those years. CRA then issues a notice of reassessment for each year it changes, with the tax recomputed under the PSB rules.

Most of the contractors I work with are IT and finance professionals. They bill through their own Ontario corporation, often through a staffing agency, for a bank, an insurer, a government or a large enterprise. They file a T2 each year and claim the small business deduction. A reassessment takes that claim away for the years CRA changes.

Under section 125(7) of the Income Tax Act, a corporation carries on a PSB in a year when two things are true. First, the person who does the work, or a person related to them, is a specified shareholder, which means owning 10% or more of any class of shares, directly or indirectly. Second, that person would reasonably be regarded as an officer or employee of the client if the corporation did not exist. I explain the tests and the two exceptions on our page about personal services business risk.

A reassessment is the end of a process. It usually starts with a letter and a questionnaire, then document requests, possibly interviews, and a proposal letter. I walk through each stage on our page about a CRA PSB review. This post is about what happens to the numbers.

What changes in the tax calculation?

Three things change at once. Each one adds tax on its own, and together they add a lot.

The small business deduction is denied

The small business deduction is what brings the federal rate down to 9% on the first $500,000 of active business income. PSB income is not eligible for it. The Ontario small business rate goes too, and the Ontario general rate of 11.5% applies instead.

The full rate applies, plus an additional 5% federal tax

Federal tax on PSB income starts at 38%, less the 10% provincial abatement, which leaves 28%. A normal corporation would then get the 13% general rate reduction. A PSB does not. Section 123.5 then adds an additional 5% tax on PSB income. That makes the federal rate 33%.

Add the Ontario general rate of 11.5% and the combined rate is 44.5%. For comparison, ordinary active business income above the $500,000 limit is taxed at 26.5% in Ontario. PSB income pays more than that.

Most expenses are denied under section 18(1)(p)

Section 18(1)(p) limits what a PSB can deduct to four things:

  • salary, wages or other remuneration paid in the year to you, the incorporated employee
  • the cost of benefits or allowances provided to you
  • certain amounts spent selling property or negotiating contracts, of the kind an employee could deduct
  • legal expenses to collect amounts owing for your services

Everything else is denied. That includes rent, software, equipment, travel, phone, insurance, accounting fees and similar costs. The corporation still spent the money. It just cannot deduct it.

How much can a PSB reassessment cost for one year?

Here is one example. The corporation has a 2025 taxation year. It is in Ontario. It billed $159,000 and had $9,000 of expenses for software, phone, insurance and accounting fees. It paid no salary. Because the income was earned before July 1, 2026, the combined small business rate for that year was 12.2%.

The extra $52,455 comes from two places. The higher rate on the $150,000 that was already taxable adds $48,450. Losing the $9,000 deduction adds another $4,005, because that amount is now taxed at 44.5%.

Look at the last line. The corporation keeps $79,245 instead of $131,700 from the same year of work. If that cash has already been paid out, the corporation still owes the extra tax.

The rates have changed since 2025. From July 1, 2026, the combined Ontario small business rate is 11.2%, so the current comparison is 11.2% vs 44.5%. A taxation year ending December 31, 2026 gets a blended 11.70%. I set out the rate history in our post on the PSB tax rate in Ontario. How many years can CRA reassess?

For a Canadian-controlled private corporation, the normal reassessment period is three years from the date of the original notice of assessment for the year. Some situations allow CRA to go further back, so the dates on your own notices need to be checked.

PSB status is tested year by year, so each year is reviewed on its own facts. But when the same contract and the same way of working run through several years, the exposure can repeat in each of them.

If the facts and figures in my example were the same in three years, all at 12.2%, the extra tax would be $157,365. That is before arrears interest.

How does arrears interest add to the bill?

Arrears interest applies to the extra tax. It runs from the date the tax was originally due until it is paid, and it compounds daily. CRA sets the rate each quarter.

That means the oldest year in a reassessment usually carries the most interest, because it has been unpaid the longest. A three-year reassessment is not just three times the tax. It is three times the tax, plus interest that has been growing on each year since its original due date.

Why do people call it double tax?

The PSB rules are meant to take away the tax benefit of earning employee-like income through a corporation. In practice, they often go further than that.

The corporation pays 44.5% on the PSB income. Then you pay personal tax again when the corporation pays out what is left as a dividend. Here is the effect on $100,000 of PSB income, at the top 2026 Ontario rates:

  • Corporate tax at 44.5%: $44,500
  • Left to pay out as an eligible dividend: $55,500
  • Personal tax on the dividend at 39.34%: $21,833.70
  • Total tax: $66,333.70, or 66.33% of the income
  • The same $100,000 paid as salary, taxed at 53.53%: $53,530
  • Extra cost of the PSB route: $12,803.70

Salary has no corporate layer of tax, because salary paid to you in the year stays deductible under section 18(1)(p). In a reassessment, though, the years under review have already happened. Dividends already paid generally cannot be relabelled as salary after the fact. So for reassessed years, the dividend route is often the only route left.

How does GRIP narrow the gap?

PSB income is not eligible for the small business deduction, so it is taxed at the full rate. Income taxed at the full rate adds to the corporation's general rate income pool (GRIP). Dividends paid out of GRIP can be designated eligible dividends.

That matters. At the top 2026 Ontario rates, eligible dividends are taxed at 39.34% and non-eligible dividends at 47.74%. On the $55,500 in the example:

  • As an eligible dividend, personal tax is $21,833.70.
  • As a non-eligible dividend, personal tax would be $26,495.70.

The non-eligible rate rises to 48.89% from January 1, 2027, because Ontario is cutting its non-eligible dividend tax credit. So the eligible designation matters a little more from 2027.

So GRIP saves $4,662 in this example, at 2026 rates. But even with the eligible dividend, the total tax of 66.33% is still well above the 53.53% on salary. GRIP narrows the gap. It does not close it.

If the corporation paid non-eligible dividends in the years CRA reassesses, whether they can now be treated as eligible is a technical question about designation and timing. It needs review on the facts.

What happens before and after the notice of reassessment?

Before a notice of reassessment, CRA usually sends a proposal letter. It sets out the changes CRA plans to make, the years, the amounts and the reasons, and it states a deadline to respond. That is the stage to point out facts or documents the auditor has not considered and to check every number.

Our CRA audit support and representation covers that part of the process. You authorize us as the corporation's representative with CRA. We organize the documents, respond to CRA requests and questionnaires, communicate with the auditor, and review and respond to the proposal letter. Nobody can promise how CRA will decide, and I do not.

After a notice of reassessment, a notice of objection is generally due within 90 days of the date on the notice. There are later stages in the CRA process after that. Arrears interest keeps running on any unpaid balance during this time.

Does paying salary limit the cost of a PSB finding?

It can, for years that have not happened yet. Salary paid to you in the year stays deductible under section 18(1)(p). So less income is left in the corporation to be taxed at 44.5%.

Here is an example for a full taxation year at the current 11.2% rate. The corporation bills $171,000 and has $9,000 of expenses.

  • With no salary: tax is $18,144 if the corporation is not a PSB, and $76,095 if it is. The PSB cost is $57,951.
  • With a $120,000 salary paid to you: tax is $4,704 if the corporation is not a PSB, and $22,695 if it is. The PSB cost is $17,991.

These are corporate tax figures only. Employer CPP is left out, and the personal tax on the salary is not shown, so this is not a full comparison of pay mixes. The point is narrower. Salary reduces how much income is exposed to the 44.5% rate. It does not change whether the corporation is a PSB.

How can you reduce the risk before CRA asks?

The cheapest reassessment is the one that never comes, but I cannot promise that for anyone. What I can do is help you reduce risk and support your position with real facts.

Here is what I suggest each year:

  • Keep the agency agreement, every statement of work and every renewal.
  • Keep a record of who approves your time, who supervises your work, and whose equipment you use.
  • Keep evidence of your own business: other clients, your own tools, marketing, and any right to send someone else to do the work.
  • Think about salary as part of your pay mix. Salary does not change whether the corporation is a PSB, but if it is, salary stays deductible.

Frequently asked questions

What is a PSB reassessment?

It is a notice of reassessment in which CRA re-taxes a corporation's past income as personal services business income. The small business deduction is denied, the Ontario rate becomes 44.5%, and most expenses are denied under section 18(1)(p).

Which expenses are still deductible after a PSB reassessment?

Salary, wages or other remuneration paid in the year to the incorporated employee, the cost of benefits or allowances provided to them, certain amounts spent selling property or negotiating contracts that an employee could deduct, and legal expenses to collect amounts owing for services. Other costs are denied.

How far back can CRA reassess a corporation for PSB?

For a Canadian-controlled private corporation, the normal reassessment period is three years from the date of the original notice of assessment for the year. Some situations allow CRA to go further back, so please check the dates on your own notices.

Does arrears interest apply to a PSB reassessment?

Yes. Arrears interest applies to the extra tax from the date it was originally due until it is paid, and it compounds daily. The oldest year in a reassessment usually carries the most interest.

Why is PSB income said to be taxed twice?

The corporation pays 44.5% on the income, and you pay personal tax again on the dividend. On $100,000 at the top 2026 Ontario rates, the total is $66,333.70, compared with $53,530 if the same amount were paid as salary.

Can dividends paid from PSB income be eligible dividends?

Yes. PSB income is taxed at the full rate, so it adds to the general rate income pool (GRIP), and dividends paid from it can be designated eligible dividends. That narrows the gap with salary but does not close it.

What happens after I receive a notice of reassessment?

The notice shows the revised tax, the balance owing and arrears interest. A notice of objection is generally due within 90 days of the date on the notice of reassessment. Please do not wait until the end of that period to get advice.
